# Unchanged candidate applications

The papers occur in manifest order. Each application uses the frozen candidate terms and boundaries.

## 1. `10.1007/s11136-025-03930-1`

- **Aim, contribution, and status:** The paper develops the first EQ-5D-5L value set for Morocco. The study families are **Health-state valuation** and **Value-set development**. The paper reports completed primary research. Material topics are adult general-population preferences, bilingual valuation, data quality, model selection, and national scoring.
- **Components and relations:** A pilot component precedes and informs the main valuation component. The main component has linked cTTO and DCE task arms. A model-selection component uses evidence from both arms and produces the value set. The pilot involved 121 interviews and was reported. The main valuation and model-selection components were analyzed and reported.
- **Evidence, population, and flow:** Adult residents of Morocco were respondents and evidence providers. The referents were hypothetical EQ-5D-5L health states. The target population was the Moroccan general population, for health-economic and policy decisions. Recruitment used sex, age, and residence quotas across regions and used public and restricted-access places. Eligibility required age 18 years or older, consent, and Moroccan Arabic or French. Of 1,590 approached persons, 1,048 completed an interview. The authors removed 42 incomplete interviews and 30 interviews with poor quality, and analyzed 976. Rural and low-literacy groups were underrepresented.
- **Instruments and administration:** The target of valuation and source of health-state descriptions was EQ-5D-5L. The administered self-health measures were the Moroccan Arabic or French descriptive system and EQ VAS. Respondents selected one of these languages. EQ-VT version 2.6.1 provided the named protocol technology. Trained interviewers used face-to-face, full interviewer administration on laptops. The settings included public places. Interviewers gave verbal support to respondents who could not read.
- **Domain methods and analysis:** Each respondent completed ten cTTO tasks from one of ten blocks that covered 86 states. The procedure used conventional TTO for better-than-dead states and lead-time TTO for worse-than-dead states, with a 10-year frame and randomized state order. Each respondent also completed seven standard DCE pairs from one of 28 blocks that covered 196 states. A feedback module followed the cTTO tasks. Quality review covered wheelchair time, explanation of worse-than-dead tasks, inconsistency for state 55555, and cTTO task time. EQ-VT support continued during collection. The candidate models were a heteroskedastic censored Tobit model for cTTO, a conditional logit model for DCE, and a hybrid heteroskedastic Tobit model. Sensitivity analysis removed flagged interviews. Selection used logical consistency, coefficient significance, prediction error, parsimony, and information criteria.
- **Outcomes, product, and comparison:** The selected hybrid model transforms responses into a directly scored utility for each EQ-5D-5L profile. It produced a **value set** for Morocco. The development fact is a completed named output. Supporting evidence is the national valuation study and model selection. Governance and availability are not reported. The paper compares the result with the existing Moroccan EQ-5D-3L value set, but the version and descriptive-system differences limit direct comparison.
- **Findings and meaning:** The selected values range from 1 to -1.492. The pain/discomfort decrement is largest, followed by anxiety/depression, mobility, self-care, and usual activities. The authors interpret the result as a locally relevant basis for scoring all EQ-5D-5L states in Morocco.
- **Limits, implications, use, and future work:** The authors report limited representation of rural and low-literacy residents. They also note that EQ-VT was designed for literate respondents and that the sampling targets used the 2014 census. They propose use in cost-utility analysis, HTA, policy, and PROM work. These statements are author-reported implications, not documented use or effect. They call for methods that better include underrepresented groups.
- **Reuse and evidence relations:** The main cTTO and DCE tasks use the same respondent interviews. Their evidence relation is **documented same evidence**. The paper reuses the standard EQ-VT protocol and experimental designs. The Moroccan EQ-5D-3L value set is a comparison object, not reused evidence for the new model.
- **Source uncertainty:** The abstract and main flow report 976 analyzed respondents, but the sample-characteristic totals indicate 1,006. This is an unresolved count and denominator conflict. The relation between the 121 pilot interviews and the main evidence is unclear. The paper does not clearly state if pilot respondents are included in the main flow.

## 2. `10.1007/s40273-022-01210-1`

- **Aim, contribution, and status:** The paper develops an Indonesian EQ-5D-Y-3L value set. It also tests an expanded cTTO design and alternative anchoring functions. The study families are **Health-state valuation**, **Value-set development**, **Mapping or scoring**, and **Preference-method study**. The paper reports completed primary research.
- **Components and relations:** An adult DCE component estimates relative child-health preferences. A separate cTTO component provides cardinal values for selected states. A mapping and model-selection component maps DCE disutility to the cTTO scale and produces the value set. The two collection components use independent respondents and both inform the final component.
- **Evidence, population, and flow:** Indonesian adults were respondents. They valued hypothetical health for a 10-year-old child. This is an adult general-population preference source with a child referent. The DCE sample used multistage stratification and quotas for age, sex, urban status, education, religion, and island area across 14 urban and rural locations. Of 1,090 completed DCE respondents, 18 failed the dominant-pair rule and 1,072 were analyzed. The cTTO sample came from Jakarta and a Bandung-area district. Of 228 completed cTTO respondents, six were excluded for non-trading or lack of understanding, and 222 were analyzed. The target population was Indonesian children whose health is described with EQ-5D-Y-3L.
- **Instruments and administration:** EQ-5D-Y-3L was the target of valuation and source of child health-state descriptions. The DCE tasks used paper forms in face-to-face interviews. Each form used color and dimension overlap. Side, state, and task order were randomized as stated. The cTTO tasks used face-to-face, full interviewer administration on the offline EuroQol Portable Valuation Technology. A feedback module and continuing quality review were used.
- **Domain methods and analysis:** DCE respondents completed 15 standard health-state pairs and one dominant pair. Ten blocks covered the DCE design. A mixed logit model estimated relative disutilities and preference heterogeneity. The expanded cTTO design covered 23 unique states in two blocks, with state 33333 in both blocks. It used conventional and lead-time cTTO. The mapping analysis linked mean cTTO values to DCE-predicted disutilities. It compared linear and power functions, each with and without a constant. The power function without a constant was selected. The selection favored plausible prediction for mild states and the required value of 1 for full health, although another model had a slightly better overall fit.
- **Outcomes, product, and comparison:** The score path is EQ-5D-Y-3L profile to mixed-logit disutility to a power anchoring function to a directly scored utility. It produced a **value set** and a **mapping or scoring function** for Indonesia. Both are completed named outputs. Supporting evidence is the independent DCE and cTTO valuation. The intended use is child HRQoL scoring and cost-utility work in Indonesia. The paper states that data are available on request. Product governance is not reported.
- **Findings and meaning:** The values range from 1 to -0.086. Pain/discomfort has the largest effect and self-care has the smallest effect. The cTTO-to-DCE relation is non-linear, mainly because of the two most severe cTTO states. The authors interpret the expanded design as useful for detecting non-linearity, but they do not claim that one anchoring function will fit all settings.
- **Limits, implications, use, and future work:** The cTTO sample was limited to Java and differed from the national DCE sample. Severe cTTO states were sparse, and two states strongly affected the functional form. The selected non-linear transformation does not preserve the interval-scale assumption of a linear transformation. The large DCE sample can dominate a hybrid model. The paper proposes use of the value set in Indonesian child studies. This is an implication, not documented use. The authors call for more evidence on youth valuation designs and anchoring.
- **Reuse and evidence relations:** The DCE and cTTO samples are **documented independent**. The study reuses EuroQol task protocols and designs. The Indonesian adult EQ-5D-5L value set is only a comparison object.
- **Source uncertainty:** The cTTO design states 12 states per block, which gives 2,664 observations for 222 respondents. A later statement gives 2,220 observations, which implies ten tasks per respondent. This task-count conflict is unresolved. The main method and arithmetic support 12 tasks.

## 3. `10.1186/s41687-025-00928-8`

- **Aim, contribution, and status:** The paper assesses self and parent or caregiver reports of pediatric inpatient HRQoL at admission and discharge. It studies cross-informant agreement, convergent construct validity, responsiveness, and known-groups relations. The material study families are **Measurement-property study** and **Population-health study**. The paper reports completed primary research.
- **Components and relations:** One repeated-measures dyad component has admission and discharge time points. Child self-report and parent or caregiver proxy-report arms are paired within each dyad. The same evidence supports measurement-property and population-health analyses.
- **Evidence, population, and flow:** Children aged 4 to 18 years and their parents or caregivers were respondents and evidence providers. The child was the referent in both arms. The child arm used self-report. The adult arm used a proxy form, but the exact requested proxy perspective is not reported. The setting was pediatric inpatient care at the University of Gondar in Ethiopia from June to October 2023. Recruitment used age quotas and consecutive selection. Low consciousness, disorientation, and visual impairment were exclusion reasons. Of 985 dyads that completed collection, 28 had incomplete data and 957 were analyzed.
- **Instruments and administration:** A modified Amharic EQ-5D-Y-3L descriptive system and EQ VAS were administered measures and targets of measurement-property assessment. Children aged 8 to 18 years completed the self form. An interviewer read the exact items and gave clarification for children aged 4 to 7 years. Parent or caregiver completion occurred independently in a separate room. The paper reports bedside child administration and a nurses' room for adults. Admission and discharge were the two time points. Zimbabwe EQ-5D-Y-3L values were the scoring source for the derived index.
- **Domain methods and analysis:** Weighted kappa and percent agreement assessed dimension-level cross-informant agreement. Intraclass correlation assessed agreement for index and EQ VAS outcomes. Spearman correlation between the index and EQ VAS assessed convergent construct validity. Dimension problem reduction, paired tests, the Paretian Classification of Health Change, and physician-documented clinical progress assessed responsiveness. Subgroup analyses used age, sex, time, and residence. Socio-demographic comparisons assessed known-groups evidence.
- **Outcomes and comparisons:** Outcomes were item responses, health profiles, EQ VAS, a utility scored with the Zimbabwe value set, and change categories. Child and proxy reports are paired evidence. Admission and discharge are paired time points. Physician-documented progress is an anchor for change. Cross-informant agreement is not test-retest reliability.
- **Findings and meaning:** Dimension agreement was generally fair at admission. Agreement for usual activities was poor at discharge. Index and EQ VAS agreement varied by age and was weaker for the youngest and oldest groups. Index and EQ VAS had moderate convergent relations. Both reporters recorded marked improvement by discharge. The authors interpret the two perspectives as useful and complementary in inpatient assessment.
- **Limits, implications, use, and future work:** Expected treatment improvement can inflate apparent responsiveness. The study had no formal inter-collector reliability assessment or cognitive interviews with young children. It excluded children with severe symptoms and did not include developmental disability. Zimbabwe scoring limits transfer to Ethiopia. Acute illness and social desirability can affect reports. The authors propose both reports for practice and call for cultural research, self-proxy work, longer follow-up, mixed methods, and an Ethiopian youth value set. No documented routine use or effect is reported.
- **Reuse and evidence relations:** Child and proxy reports are **documented same evidence** at the dyad and time-point level. The Zimbabwe value set is reused as a scoring source. The paper does not report reuse of a prior respondent sample.
- **Source uncertainty:** The abstract gives the strongest age-specific agreement for ages 7 to 16, while the main text gives ages 7 to 15. The diagnosis table lists malaria twice with different counts; one label can be an error. The age-band heading for ages 12 to 18 overlaps the adjacent 8-to-12 group. The exact proxy form and requested perspective are not reported.

## 4. `10.1186/s41687-024-00761-5`

- **Aim, contribution, and status:** The paper translates and culturally adapts PedsQL 4.0 child and teen self-report forms into Chichewa for Malawi. It then assesses selected measurement properties. The study families are **Translation**, **Cultural adaptation**, and **Measurement-property study**. The paper reports completed primary research.
- **Components and relations:** A translation and adaptation component precedes and informs a psychometric component. The first component includes forward translation, back translation, reconciliation, cognitive interviews, revision, proofreading, and governance review. The psychometric component administers the approved language forms to separate child and teen groups.
- **Evidence, population, and flow:** Ten healthy school participants formed a convenience cognitive-interview sample, with five children and five teenagers. The psychometric sample included 289 literate participants aged 8 to 17 years in Blantyre. It included 98 child-form and 191 teen-form respondents from five schools and outpatient care. Recruitment was by convenience. Critically ill participants were excluded. Participants were respondents, referents, and evidence providers for self-report. The intended population is Chichewa-speaking children and teenagers in Malawi.
- **Instruments and administration:** The source versions were the US English PedsQL 4.0 child and teen self-report forms. The target and product were the Chichewa Malawi child and teen self-report language versions. The translation component used two independent forward translations and two back translations. Cognitive interviews tested wording with the target age groups. The psychometric forms were self-completed in classrooms or at the end of outpatient care. Staff could help with instructions. EQ-5D-Y-3L and EQ-5D-Y-5L were linked to the wider project, but results for their comparison are in another publication and are not findings of this paper.
- **Domain methods and analysis:** Adaptation changed six material terms or expressions. Changes addressed sport and exercise, shower or bath, hurt or ache, feeling unwell, classroom location, and a word with a menstrual meaning. Psychometric methods assessed missingness, maximum and adjacent endorsement, response redundancy, Cronbach alpha, inter-subscale correlations, gender, age and grade discrimination, healthy-versus-sick known groups, and item-to-own-subscale versus other-subscale relations.
- **Product and supporting evidence:** The paper produced two **language versions**: Chichewa PedsQL 4.0 child and teen self-report forms for Malawi. The development fact is a completed named output. Supporting evidence includes translation, cognitive interviews, and an initial psychometric test. Mapi Research Trust approved the translation. Availability and access are not reported.
- **Findings and meaning:** Internal consistency was acceptable for the total and main summary scores. Many child items had missing responses, and most items failed the adjacent-endorsement rule. Known-groups differences were clearer for teenagers than for children. Grade discrimination was weak for many teen outcomes. The authors recommend cautious use and more testing.
- **Limits, implications, use, and future work:** Cognitive interviews included only healthy participants. COVID-19 restrictions allowed one psychometric time point, so test-retest reliability and responsiveness were not assessed. Convenience selection, literacy requirements, and small subgroups limit transfer. The authors call for tests of completeness, local content, interviewer-assisted administration, test-retest reliability, and responsiveness. No documented routine use or effect is reported.
- **Reuse and evidence relations:** The psychometric sample is independent of the cognitive-interview sample. This is **documented independent** evidence. The English source version is reused for translation. The wider project data used for a separate EQ-5D-Y comparison do not support a finding in this paper.
- **Source uncertainty:** The classroom-distance wording was changed by the team but was not tested with participants. This is a transfer limit for that wording. The exact amount and type of instruction help in the psychometric component are not reported. No material source conflict was found.

## 5. `10.1007/s11136-026-04230-y`

- **Aim, contribution, and status:** The paper examines how dementia proxies interpret the EQ-HWB-9 recall period and reporting perspective. The study family is **Measurement-property study**. The exact assessment targets are recall-period interpretation and adherence to the requested proxy perspective. The paper reports completed primary research.
- **Components and relations:** One qualitative think-aloud component administers experimental proxy items and uses an integrated thematic analysis. Recall and perspective are distinct assessment targets within the same component.
- **Evidence, population, and flow:** Nineteen adult paid or unpaid carers in Australia were respondents and evidence providers. People with dementia were the referents. Recruitment used a convenience sample from an online dementia course. Of 39 contacted people, 19 were interviewed. Most respondents were women and had high education. The intended use concerns proxy assessment for people with dementia.
- **Instruments and administration:** The target of measurement-property assessment was an experimental 2022 Australian English EQ-HWB-9 proxy version. Nine EQ-HWB-9 items and three positive items from the longer EQ-HWB were administered. The recall period was seven days. The exact numbered proxy form and requested perspective are not reported. Interviews used Zoom, a paper copy, think-aloud work, and a semi-structured interview. Sessions lasted 49 to 69 minutes.
- **Domain methods and analysis:** The analysis used integrated thematic coding. It combined inductive line coding with deductive item coding. Two researchers coded the first five interviews, and one researcher coded the remainder with team review. A saturation grid showed no new themes after ten interviews. A lived-experience group reviewed the analysis, and respondents received a study summary.
- **Outcomes and findings:** Two recall strategies occurred. Some proxies extended the period to include salient events just outside seven days. Others used earlier health to interpret the current period. Observable items supported closer adherence. Wording about difficulty, change, frequency, and a new normal caused variation. All respondents used observed proxy-report information. Nearly half also tried to infer the person's internal view. No respondent used only that inferred view. Pain and control produced notable perspective conflicts.
- **Interpretation, limits, and implications:** The authors interpret recall and proxy perspective as active reasoning tasks, not simple instruction compliance. They propose clearer wording and instructions. They also suggest that the two proxy perspectives can provide complementary evidence, but the study does not test an outcome benefit. Digital recruitment, high literacy, Australian English, and a mostly female sample limit transfer. Think-aloud methods can change response behavior. Dementia severity and self-report ability were not reported. Future work should test wording, adherence, proxy groups, and cultures.
- **Product, use, and effect:** The paper does not produce a completed instrument version. It evaluates an experimental form. It gives method and wording implications, but it reports no documented routine use or effect.
- **Reuse and evidence relations:** The experimental proxy form reuses EQ-HWB-9 items and three longer-form items. The paper does not report reuse of a prior evidence sample. Recall and perspective results use **documented same evidence**.
- **Source uncertainty:** The exact requested proxy perspective and numbered proxy form are not reported. Characteristics of the people with dementia are also not reported. No material count conflict was found.

## 6. `10.1007/s40271-025-00787-x`

- **Aim, contribution, and status:** The paper tests the introduction of routine quality-of-life collection in two Australian residential aged-care facilities. It studies workflow feasibility, resident and proxy completion, staff experience, burden, barriers, facilitators, and possible care use. The study family is **Implementation study**. The paper reports completed primary research.
- **Components and relations:** A co-design and preparation component precedes a two-site pilot collection component. A staff-evaluation component uses experience from the pilot and informs implementation recommendations. Facility A used a digital workflow. Facility B used paper and later entry. Resident self-report, staff proxy-report, and family proxy-report are linked administration arms.
- **Evidence, population, and flow:** The sites were two interested facilities from one not-for-profit provider in Melbourne. The resident evidence set included 103 consenting residents or holders of power of attorney. Available assessments included 90 resident self-reports, 101 staff proxy reports, and 49 family proxy reports. Seven resident and seven staff duplicate assessments resulted from an administrative error. Six staff members supplied qualitative evidence. Residents were the referents for all instrument forms. Staff and family relationships were recorded as proxy roles.
- **Instruments and administration:** All roles received Australian English EQ-5D-5L. Residents used the interviewer-administered self form with a current-day recall. Staff and families used proxy version 2 with the proxy-person perspective. A randomized second measure was QOL-ACC, ASCOT SCT-4, or an experimental 25-item EQ-HWB. The second measures had self and proxy forms as applicable. Facility A used REDCap on tablets. Facility B used paper. Researchers later entered paper data. Staff completed proxy forms before researcher-administered resident interviews, often while the resident was present. Researchers administered family proxy forms by telephone.
- **Implementation methods and workflow:** Staff training and a workshop informed the workflow. Staff checked resident eligibility, completed a proxy form, and supported access. Researchers conducted the resident interview and staff evaluation, and they contacted family proxies. Feasibility methods covered completion, missingness, engagement, understanding, required support, proxy difficulty, and staff thematic evidence. The workflow did not include a fully specified result display, flag rule, review duty, or required action route.
- **Product and state:** The paper reports an **implementation workflow** for routine quality-of-life collection. The development fact is a completed local pilot. Supporting evidence is the two-site pilot and six staff interviews. Documented use is pilot use only. The implementation state is **pilot**. Governance and wider availability are not reported.
- **Findings and meaning:** EQ-5D-5L had high attempted completion and low missingness in most reported summaries. The experimental EQ-HWB was longer and more complex. Staff needed to repeat or explain items for some residents. Barriers included time, interruptions, cognition, illness, hearing, language, limited proxy knowledge, emotional items, and interview effects. Staff reported that the process increased knowledge of residents and sometimes changed their approach to care.
- **Limits, implications, use, and effect:** The paper documents pilot collection and limited staff-reported care responses. It does not establish scaled routine use or a measured resident-health effect. The two facilities, incentives, consent requirements, capacity selection, research support, and one provider limit transfer. People with cognitive or language barriers were underrepresented. Timing and repeat completion were not measured accurately. The authors propose dedicated staff time, training, a quality-of-life champion, clear action routes, and resources for wider use. Future work should test repeated collection and integrated workflows.
- **Reuse and evidence relations:** Self and proxy forms for the same residents are **documented same evidence** at the referent level, but not all roles have an assessment for every resident. The co-design work and pilot are part of a larger project. The paper reports the present components, but it does not make later quality-improvement work part of this pilot.
- **Source uncertainty:** Missingness summaries in the abstract and role-specific table use different denominators and can appear inconsistent. The assessment counts do not equal 103 for each role because participation differed. Duplicate assessments are an administrative error, not repeat completion. Exact denominators are not reported for every feasibility statement.

## 7. `10.1007/s11136-026-04223-x`

- **Aim, contribution, and status:** The paper develops a 12-item short form of QI-Disability and assesses selected score and item properties. The study families are **Instrument development or revision** and **Measurement-property study**. The paper reports a completed secondary analysis.
- **Components and relations:** A pooled-data preparation component precedes an algorithmic item-selection component. A psychometric component tests the selected short form in the same pooled evidence. The selection component produces QID-12, and the property component supplies same-sample supporting evidence.
- **Evidence, population, and flow:** Parents and caregivers supplied 1,699 unique, complete proxy records about children aged 3 to 18 years with intellectual disability. Severity ranged from mild to profound. The sources were The Kids studies, Simons Searchlight, and Inchstone. The paper removed duplicate records across sources. Countries, recruitment, and administration differed across sources and were not always reported. Parents or caregivers were respondents and evidence providers, and children were referents. The exact requested proxy perspective is not reported.
- **Instruments and administration:** The 32-item parent-report QI-Disability was the source instrument and target of revision. The QID-12 short form was the product. Administration varied across reused data sets, with online collection in at least some sources. The exact form, language, mode, and country are not reported for every contributing record.
- **Development and property methods:** A genetic algorithm used 500 random seeds. It selected 12 items while retaining at least one and no more than three items from each of six domains. The objective was maximum explained variance in the long-form total. Pearson correlation tested association with the long-form total. Cronbach alpha assessed internal consistency. A partial-credit Rasch model assessed item fit, person separation reliability, unidimensionality, targeting, and category thresholds.
- **Product and supporting evidence:** The paper produced QID-12, an **instrument or version** with 12 items and six domains. The development fact is a completed named output. Its intended use is brief overall quality-of-life screening, monitoring, and research for children with intellectual disability. The authors do not recommend it as a replacement for domain-level detail. Supporting evidence is same-sample correlation, internal consistency, and Rasch analysis. Independent validation, governance, and availability are not reported.
- **Findings and meaning:** QID-12 correlates strongly with the long form and has acceptable internal consistency and Rasch item fit. It better targets the lower quality-of-life range. Three response-category thresholds are disordered. The shared items and same selection sample partly explain the high long-form correlation. The authors interpret the short form as a practical brief overall measure.
- **Limits, implications, use, and future work:** No independent cohort tested the selected items. Heterogeneous source data had limited harmonized covariates. Some administration and country facts were unavailable. The disordered thresholds need more study. The authors call for independent validation, different settings, responsiveness tests, and comparison with the long form. No documented routine use or effect is reported.
- **Reuse and evidence relations:** The analysis reuses multiple named data sets. Overlap checks and duplicate removal produce a **documented partial overlap** relation before deduplication and a unique pooled analytic set after it. Item selection and psychometric testing use **documented same evidence**.
- **Source uncertainty:** Cronbach alpha is 0.85 in the abstract and discussion but 0.84 in the main results. The Martin-Löf result is reported as greater than 0.99 in the abstract and 0.12 in the main results. These are unresolved summary-versus-main-text conflicts. Administration and national scope are not reported for all source data.

## 8. `10.3390/cancers16111952`

- **Aim, contribution, and status:** The paper assesses the internal responsiveness of EQ-5D-5L and EORTC QLQ-C30 during the first year after curative breast-cancer surgery. The study family is **Measurement-property study**. The paper reports a completed secondary analysis of routine PROM data.
- **Components and relations:** One longitudinal complete-case component has preoperative, six-month, and 12-month time points. The same respondents completed both instruments at each retained time point. A chemotherapy subgroup analysis uses the same component.
- **Evidence, population, and flow:** Women with invasive breast cancer who received curative surgery at Erasmus MC in the Netherlands from November 2015 to January 2022 were respondents, referents, and evidence providers. Exclusions included palliative treatment, recurrence, proton therapy, no treatment, and incomplete repeated data. Of the reported source flow, three duplicate records, 27 nonresponders, 118 other exclusions, and 219 incomplete cases were removed. The complete-case analysis included 333 women.
- **Instruments and administration:** Dutch EQ-5D-5L and EORTC QLQ-C30 version 3 were administered measures and targets of responsiveness assessment. EQ-5D-5L outcomes were the descriptive-system profile, EQ VAS, and a utility scored with the Dutch value set. EORTC outcomes included global health, summary, functional, and symptom scales. The hospital patient-data platform collected PROMs. The exact self-completion medium, support, and recall periods are not reported in the paper text.
- **Property methods and comparisons:** Paired tests compare baseline with six and 12 months. Effect size uses the baseline standard deviation, and standardized response mean uses the change-score standard deviation. The paper compares changes with an external 0.08 EQ-5D threshold and a five-point EORTC threshold. These thresholds are interpretation criteria, not external anchors. The study assesses internal responsiveness. It does not assess external responsiveness against an independent clinical change anchor. Instrument, time, and chemotherapy are the main comparison axes.
- **Outcomes and findings:** Outcomes are health profiles, EQ VAS, directly scored utility, scale outcomes, and change scores. Most overall effect sizes and standardized response means were below 0.5. Changes were larger at six months and moved toward baseline by 12 months. EQ-5D utility showed more change than the EORTC global and summary outcomes. Some EORTC symptom and function outcomes had larger changes in the chemotherapy subgroup. Mean changes for the main summary outcomes were often below the selected thresholds.
- **Interpretation, limits, and implications:** The authors interpret responsiveness as dependent on time, treatment subgroup, population stability, and instrument content. Complete-case selection limits generalization. The study has no independent change anchor. The population mainly had early, stable disease, and the change thresholds were not specific to Dutch breast cancer. The authors propose more time points, treatment-specific analysis, and breast-specific PROM comparisons. The paper documents routine PROM data collection, but it does not study implementation or a care effect.
- **Reuse and evidence relations:** The paper reanalyzes routine patient-data-platform records. The two instruments provide **documented same evidence** at the respondent and time-point level. The Dutch value set is reused as a scoring source.
- **Source uncertainty:** The paper describes the EQ VAS direction as 0 for best health and 100 for worst health. This conflicts with the instrument's use and the reported outcomes in the paper. The issue remains unresolved in the source record. Exact administration support and recall periods are not reported.

## 9. `10.1007/s40271-020-00490-z`

- **Aim, contribution, and status:** The paper examines telephone or video cTTO as an alternative to personal interviews and gives practical method guidance. The study families are **Preference-method study** and **Conceptual or methodological guidance**. The paper reports a completed secondary method analysis and method guidance.
- **Components and relations:** A personal interview component precedes a remote Tele-TTO component because COVID-19 stopped in-person collection. A comparative analysis uses both components. A guidance component uses the comparison and interviewer experience to produce recommendations.
- **Evidence, population, and flow:** Dutch general-population adults valued hypothetical EQ-5D-5L states. There were 36 respondents in personal interviews and 113 in Tele-TTO interviews from March to May 2020. Recruitment used age and sex quotas through a marketing agency. The same author interviewed both groups. The samples are independent, but allocation was not randomized and the periods were sequential.
- **Instruments and administration:** Dutch EQ-5D-5L health states were the source of health-state descriptions and the target of valuation. Both modes used interviewer-operated, proprietary EQ-VT-based software. Each respondent valued six states in random order. Personal interviews occurred face to face at the same table. Tele-TTO used Zoom video, audio, and screen sharing on a laptop or tablet. Respondents gave spoken choices. Telephone audio was a backup when video failed.
- **Domain methods and analysis:** Both components used cTTO with conventional and lead-time procedures and a 10-year frame. The purpose was cardinal health-state valuation and a test of remote administration feasibility. Analyses compared mean and median values, variance, response clustering, and logical inconsistency. Tests included t, rank, variance, and chi-square methods. The administration comparison is bundled with period, recruitment experience, and interviewer learning. It does not identify an isolated effect of remote contact.
- **Outcomes, product, and findings:** Outcomes were cTTO values and data-quality indicators. The paper found no systematic differences in central values, variance, or clustering. Strict logical violations were less frequent in Tele-TTO. Tele-TTO respondents were younger and more often unmarried. Remote work offered flexibility and reminders, but it required more effort and had technology and rapport limits. The paper produces **reporting or method guidance** for remote cTTO. The development fact is completed paper-local guidance. It is not a formal endorsed protocol.
- **Interpretation, limits, implications, and future work:** The authors consider Tele-TTO feasible but do not establish equivalence. Sequential, non-random samples, one interviewer, a small personal group, COVID-19 timing, and interviewer experience confound the comparison. The study did not collect full signup, response, or respondent-experience data. The authors propose randomized comparisons, device and software tests, respondent and interviewer experience measures, and hybrid mode choice. No routine use or downstream effect is reported.
