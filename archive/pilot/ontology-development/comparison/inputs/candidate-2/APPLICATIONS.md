# Round 1 paper applications

The papers appear in batch order. Each application uses the ontology in `ONTOLOGY.md`. The descriptions give enough result detail to identify each contribution. They do not reproduce all study results.

## 1. 10.1007/s40258-021-00639-3

**Contribution and status:** Completed primary valuation study. It develops a national value set.

**Objects:** The focal objects are EQ-5D-5L health states, Danish adult population preferences, and the model that assigns utility values. Composite time trade-off (cTTO) and discrete-choice experiment (DCE) data are inputs. Earlier Danish EQ-5D-3L and crosswalk value sets are comparators.

**Question and use:** Which elicitation data and model can produce a logically consistent Danish EQ-5D-5L value set for quality-adjusted life-year estimation and national health-care priority setting?

**Evidence source and target:** The evidence source is 1,014 included adult preference respondents. They also reported their own health, but their main source role was to value hypothetical EQ-5D-5L states. The target is the valuation of health states reported by people in Denmark. Recruitment started from a register-based national sample and later added a market research panel. The design targeted balance by age, gender, education, and region. The paper found slight under-representation of younger adults and people with the lowest education.

**Design and analysis:** This was a cross-sectional, face-to-face, computer-assisted EQ-VT 2.1 study. Each respondent completed ten cTTO and seven DCE tasks. The study used interviewer training, continuous quality control, blocked health-state designs, and a feedback module. It compared cTTO-only, DCE-only, and hybrid model families. Model selection emphasized logical consistency. Sensitivity analysis restored cTTO observations that respondents had marked as incorrectly ordered.

**Output and scope:** The paper selected a heteroscedastic censored hybrid model. This model produced the paper's final Danish EQ-5D-5L value set. The output was ready for the decision use stated by the paper, but the paper also noted unresolved theoretical and anchoring questions for hybrid DCE and cTTO models.

**Limits and transparency:** A change in recruitment source, small sample imbalances, and the long interview burden limit the representativeness claim. The paper states that data and code are available from the corresponding author on reasonable request. EuroQol supported methods and quality control, and one author disclosed EuroQol membership.

## 2. 10.1007/s11136-020-02688-y

**Contribution and status:** Completed systematic review with quantitative pooling.

**Objects:** The focal object is the measurement-property evidence for the EQ-5D-5L descriptive system. The review does not evaluate valuation methods as its main object.

**Question and use:** What evidence supports the use and interpretation of EQ-5D-5L across adult populations, conditions, and settings, and where are the evidence gaps?

**Evidence source and target:** The evidence sources are 99 publications from 32 countries, found for 2011 through January 2019. Included populations covered general populations and many patient groups. Publications, not individual participants, are the direct evidence units.

**Design and analysis:** Two reviewers screened records. The review extracted missing response, distribution, test-retest reliability, validity, and responsiveness. It pooled the proportion in best health, mean utility index, and correlations by random-effects methods when the evidence permitted pooling.

**Output and scope:** The output is a measurement-property evidence profile. It supports broad reliability and validity, but it keeps item-level instability, ceiling effects, and limited responsiveness evidence visible. It also identifies weak coverage of satisfaction and cognition or communication.

**Limits and transparency:** The review excluded experimental versions, most application studies that did not explicitly assess psychometrics, and valuation methodology. Evidence was concentrated in Western Europe, other OECD countries, and East Asia. EuroQol funded the review, and all authors reported EuroQol membership.

## 3. 10.1007/s11136-025-03983-2

**Contribution and status:** Protocol and active resource description. It describes infrastructure and data collection, not a completed analysis of population health or instrument performance.

**Objects:** The focal object is EQ-DAPHNIE, a multi-country population survey resource. EQ-5D-5L, bolt-ons, EQ-HWB, PROMIS-10, ASCOT or ICECAP-A, WHO-5, OPQOL-brief, PHQ-2, GAD-2, and an anchoring vignette are measured objects within the resource.

**Question and use:** How can a standardized survey infrastructure produce comparable population health data and support instrument evaluation across countries?

**Evidence source and target:** The source population is adults aged 18 years or older from online panels. The target is the general adult population of each country. The project used quotas for age, sex, income, residence, and, where relevant, language. Quota sampling and post-stratification target population balance, but the design is not probability sampling.

**Design and analysis:** The resource uses cross-sectional online surveys with a target of 4,500 completed responses per country. It standardizes a core questionnaire, uses language adaptation, randomizes the order of several measures, duplicates selected questions for quality checks, and includes a response-scale heterogeneity vignette. A pilot and two country rounds were complete or in progress as described in the paper. The paper reports no main outcome analysis.

**Output and scope:** The output is a governed, reusable, multi-country dataset design for population norms, cross-country comparison, and instrument comparison. Future longitudinal follow-up is proposed but is not part of the current resource design.

**Limits and transparency:** Web access and panel membership can exclude parts of the population. Cultural interpretation can reduce cross-country comparability. The cross-sectional design does not support change analysis. Data access is restricted to EuroQol members or collaboration with a member. All authors except one reported EuroQol membership.

## 4. 10.1017/s0266462326103602

**Contribution and status:** Completed cross-sectional use and practice study with quantitative and qualitative survey data.

**Objects:** The focal objects are utility instruments, preference-elicitation methods, sources of health-state preferences, data-quality problems, and research priorities in health technology assessment (HTA).

**Question and use:** What do HTA practitioners currently use, what problems do they see in health-related quality-of-life and health-state utility data, and what research do they need?

**Evidence source and target:** The source is 238 individuals from 65 HTA agencies in 45 countries. Respondents gave personal views based on their work. They were not official agency representatives. The intended target is global HTA practice across six regional groupings. Purposive and network recruitment through the study team reached a specialized population, but it did not produce an agency-level probability sample.

**Design and analysis:** The anonymous online survey used closed questions and open text. Country summaries were combined into regional summaries. Research priorities were scored at country, region, and global levels. Open responses received structured content analysis.

**Output and scope:** The output is a practice and research-priority landscape. EQ-5D and time trade-off were the most prominent instrument and method. Foreign preference data were often used where local data were limited. Sample representativeness, small samples, health-state mismatch, and mixed methods within one model were common concerns. Recent value sets, measures for children, and broader health and social care outcomes were high priorities.

**Limits and transparency:** Several countries had very few responses. Network recruitment can over-represent people familiar with EuroQol. Eligibility could not be verified. The data do not support agency-level reanalysis or official agency positions. EuroQol funded the work, and most authors reported EuroQol membership.

## 5. 10.1007/s11136-019-02115-x

**Contribution and status:** Completed multi-phase instrument development study. The product is a self-report descriptive system that still requires psychometric and valuation work.

**Objects:** EQ-5D-Y-3L is the origin object. Candidate four-level and five-level descriptive systems are comparison objects. The final focal artifact is EQ-5D-Y-5L in German, Spanish, Swedish, and UK English. Severity labels and response-category count are the components under study.

**Question and use:** Can the youth descriptive system use more response levels while it remains understandable and feasible for children aged 8 to 15 years?

**Evidence source and target:** Children and adolescents aged 8 to 15 years in Germany, Spain, Sweden, and the United Kingdom supplied language and usability evidence. Early work used general-population school and community samples. Later cognitive work included healthy participants and, in three countries, participants with health conditions. The target is youth self-report. The samples were convenience samples and were not nationally representative.

**Design and analysis:** Phase 1 combined a review of existing measures, focus groups, and 255 age-adapted sorting or response-scaling interviews. Prespecified severity-spacing rules and participant language guided label selection. Phase 2 used cognitive interviews to test comprehension, feasibility, and preference between four and five levels. National teams then harmonized meaning across languages without requiring literal word equivalence.

**Output and scope:** Most participants preferred five levels. The paper produced the language-specific EQ-5D-Y-5L self-report descriptive system. It did not establish reliability, validity, responsiveness, a proxy form, or a value set.

**Limits and transparency:** Recruitment of children with health conditions was difficult. The UK procedure differed from the other countries. Language-specific evidence justified some non-literal cross-language differences. EuroQol funded the study, and all but one author reported EuroQol membership.

## 6. 10.1007/s40273-022-01216-9

**Contribution and status:** Completed primary valuation study that produces a national youth value set.

**Objects:** The focal artifact is the Chinese EQ-5D-Y-3L value set. DCE and cTTO are preference inputs. A mapped DCE mixed-logit model and hybrid models are competing model strategies. The `A3` term represents an extra effect for the worst state, `33333`.

**Question and use:** Can an expanded cTTO design and joint modeling improve the EQ-5D-Y-3L value set for pediatric quality-adjusted life-year estimation in China?

**Evidence source and target:** The source is 1,476 adults from the Chinese general population. Independent samples completed DCE tasks (1,058) or cTTO tasks (418). Adults valued the health of a hypothetical 10-year-old child. Thus, the preference source is adult, the described person is a child, and the target is the valuation of child and adolescent health states. Quotas covered gender, age, education, and urban or rural registration. Recruitment used purposive and snowball methods across selected regions, so it was non-probability recruitment.

**Design and analysis:** The study used face-to-face EQ-VT interviews. The DCE included 150 choice sets. The expanded cTTO design included 28 states. cTTO interviewers received training and formal quality control. Model comparison used coefficient significance and monotonicity plus prediction error for observed cTTO means.

**Output and scope:** The paper selected a hybrid model with the `A3` term. The term allowed the model to represent the observed gap between `33333` and other severe states. This model is the paper's Chinese EQ-5D-Y-3L value set.

**Limits and transparency:** cTTO collection covered fewer regions than DCE collection. The relationship between each adult and the imagined child was not standardized. Ethical and behavioral differences between valuing child and adult life remain unresolved. Data are available from the corresponding author on reasonable request. EuroQol funded the study, and several authors reported EuroQol membership.

**Source conflict:** The abstract and methods state that recruitment covered 14 regions or provinces and cities. The “Key Points for Decision Makers” section states “4 different regions.” The paper does not resolve this difference.

## 7. 10.1007/s11136-025-04003-z

**Contribution and status:** Completed secondary measurement-property analysis.

**Objects:** The focal objects are frequency and severity response scales for separate pain and discomfort items in the experimental EQ-HWB. The EQ-5D-5L combined pain or discomfort severity item is a comparator.

**Question and use:** Do frequency and severity response scales supply the same information, and which scale is more useful for long or short generic health-related quality-of-life instruments?

**Evidence source and target:** The source is existing cross-sectional survey data from 504 caregiver and care-recipient dyads, for 1,008 adults. Analysis was at the individual level, not the dyad level. Health conditions were self-reported. The target is pain and discomfort measurement across varied adult health conditions.

**Design and analysis:** The source survey used an online caregiver panel, linked dyadic completion, randomized measure order, attention checks, and completion-time checks. The secondary analysis used correlations, Shannon indices, graded-response item response theory, differential item functioning with scale type as the group, and ordinal regression by condition.

**Output and scope:** Frequency and severity were strongly related but not interchangeable. Frequency used response categories more evenly and was more sensitive at lower trait levels. Severity had stronger discrimination at higher levels. The paper supports both scales in a long measure and gives frequency priority for a short measure, subject to the intended population and use.

**Limits and transparency:** Cross-sectional data do not show change or causation. Conditions were self-reported. Cultural and language transfer requires new evidence. Data are available from the corresponding author on reasonable request. EuroQol funded the work, and two authors disclosed EuroQol membership or a related consultancy role.

## 8. 10.1007/s11136-025-04038-2

**Contribution and status:** Completed qualitative conceptual and content evaluation.

**Objects:** The study develops a Chinese lay quality-of-life framework and compares it with the EQ-HWB conceptual framework. It tests only the comprehensiveness part of content validity in this paper.

**Question and use:** Does the EQ-HWB framework cover the quality-of-life concepts that Chinese healthy people, patients, and informal caregivers identify?

**Evidence source and target:** Thirty Chinese adults took part: ten healthy people, ten patients, and ten informal caregivers. Quotas covered age, gender, education, health or caregiving group, and urban or rural registration. Participants came from two regions. They are qualitative informants, not preference respondents. The target is the use of EQ-HWB in the Chinese context.

**Design and analysis:** Face-to-face semi-structured interviews used a translated and piloted topic guide. Two coders used deductive codes from EQ-HWB candidate items and inductive codes from transcripts. The team applied five explicit exclusion rules before it grouped 187 retained codes into 57 sub-themes and eight themes. The framework was then compared with EQ-HWB.

**Output and scope:** The output is an eight-theme Chinese quality-of-life framework. Seven themes align with EQ-HWB, while `mindset` is additional. The paper concludes that the differences do not materially reduce EQ-HWB comprehensiveness. This conclusion applies to comprehensiveness, not to relevance or comprehensibility.

**Limits and transparency:** The exclusion rules removed material conditions, external events, future outcomes, and other concepts. These rules define the framework's scope and affect any content-validity conclusion. The sample had limited regional and severe-illness coverage. Saturation was judged subjectively. Some Chinese concepts had no direct English equivalent. Qualitative data are not public because of privacy, but anonymized excerpts can be requested. EuroQol funded the work and reported no role in the study.

**Source conflict:** The comparison section reports “alignment rates of 68% (18/57) for sub-themes.” The fraction `18/57` is about 32%, not 68%. The supplied paper does not show which value is correct.

## 9. 10.1016/j.jval.2025.02.001

**Contribution and status:** Completed systematic review of analysis practice.

**Objects:** The focal objects are the statistical methods used for EQ-5D dimension responses, EQ VAS, and utilities in randomized clinical trials. Treatment effects are not the review's focal result.

**Question and use:** How are postbaseline EQ-5D outcomes analyzed by trial arm, and which method and reporting problems can guide future analysis guidance?

**Evidence source and target:** The evidence units are 2,125 unique randomized trials. Searches covered MEDLINE and EMBASE from inception through November 2021 and ClinicalTrials.gov in August 2023. The target is EQ-5D analysis in randomized trials.

**Design and analysis:** Reviewers screened in duplicate, joined multiple reports to one trial, and classified data by EQ-5D output, numerical or categorical format, and single or multiple postbaseline measurements. Methods were grouped as descriptive, bivariate, multivariable, or survival analyses. The review did not appraise clinical risk of bias because it described methods and did not estimate treatment effects.

**Output and scope:** The output is an analysis-practice map. Utilities were the most analyzed EQ-5D output. Linear fixed-effect models were most common for one postbaseline measurement, and linear mixed-effect models were most common for repeated measurements. Few reports examined model assumptions, adjusted for baseline EQ-5D, or explicitly addressed missing data. Dimension responses received much less analysis than utilities or EQ VAS.

**Limits and transparency:** Sparse reporting can make method features appear absent when authors did not report them. Long time coverage combines changing practice. Publication and outcome-reporting bias can remain even with a registry search. The paper classifies an EQ-5D outcome as exploratory when the source did not call it primary or secondary; this is a review rule, not necessarily the trial's planned endpoint status. EuroQol funded the work and the paper states that the funder had no role.

## 10. 10.1007/s10198-025-01770-x

**Contribution and status:** Completed primary measurement-property comparison with a seven-day retest.

**Objects:** EQ-5D-Y-3L, EQ-5D-Y-5L, and CHU9D descriptive systems are focal comparators. EQ VAS is a focal score. PedsQL, the PIP-Kids pain classification, and a pain rating scale are reference measures. The study does not test utility scores.

**Question and use:** How do three youth health-related quality-of-life instruments perform in Brazilian children and adolescents with and without self-reported musculoskeletal pain?

**Evidence source and target:** The source is 356 schoolchildren aged 8 to 18 years in São Paulo: 181 with and 175 without the study's self-reported musculoskeletal pain definition. A total of 231 participants were stable and supplied retest data. The target is Brazilian-Portuguese youth self-report, especially in musculoskeletal pain.

**Design and analysis:** Participants completed paper measures at baseline and after seven days. The order of the two EQ-5D-Y forms was randomized. Assessed properties were missing response and completion, floor and ceiling effects, item-level test-retest reliability and agreement, EQ VAS reliability and measurement error, construct validity against a priori hypotheses, and known-group validity. The analysis stratified the pain and no-pain groups.

**Output and scope:** The output is a conditional measurement-property profile. All three measures distinguished children with and without pain. EQ-5D-Y-5L met more prespecified construct hypotheses than several comparisons. Descriptive-system test-retest reliability was poor to moderate. Performance was generally better in the pain group than in the no-pain group. These results support use with explicit reliability and population qualifications.

**Limits and transparency:** Pain was self-reported, heterogeneous, and not clinically diagnosed. Pain classification used a one-month recall period, while the focal measures used “today.” Consecutive similar forms can cause recall or response effects. The study did not test comprehension time, responsiveness, utility scores, or criterion validity against a gold standard. Data are available on request under ethics conditions. EuroQol and other agencies funded the work; the paper reports an employee and a member relationship with EuroQol.

## 1. 10.1016/j.jval.2025.01.003

**Contribution and status:** Completed primary valuation study. It develops the first UAE EQ-5D-5L value set.

**Objects:** The focal objects are UAE adult preferences for EQ-5D-5L states and the models that assign utility values. cTTO and DCE responses are model inputs. cTTO-only, DCE-only, and hybrid models are comparators.

**Question and use:** Which data and model can produce a UAE EQ-5D-5L value set for local cost-utility analysis, health technology assessment, population health assessment, and health-system use?

**Evidence source and target:** The analysis includes 1,005 adults. UAE nationals and expatriates with at least five years of residence could take part in Arabic or English. Two-stage quotas covered the seven emirates, age, and sex. Recruitment used professional and personal networks, snowball methods, public places, posters, and social media. The target is the UAE general adult population, but the recruitment was not a probability sample.

**Design and analysis:** Interviewers completed face-to-face or online EQ-VT interviews. Each respondent completed ten cTTO tasks and seven DCE pairs. Seven interviewers passed repeated training and data-quality review. The study compared ten model forms. Selection used logical consistency, parsimony, AIC, BIC, RMSE, MAE, and cross-validation. Sensitivity analyses tested flagged and inconsistent observations.

**Output and scope:** The paper selected a hybrid heteroskedastic Tobit model censored at −1. This model produced the paper's final UAE value set. The output supports the stated local uses, subject to the sample and language conditions.

**Limits and transparency:** The five-year residence rule for expatriates has no conclusive empirical basis. The study used the UK English EQ-5D-5L because no UAE English version was available. Language, national status, employment, and lived experience can be confounded. The changing expatriate population also raises a time-validity question. EuroQol and Novartis funded the study, and the paper states that funders had no role in the work.

**Source conflict:** The abstract gives a mean age of 39 years, SD 10.8, 44.5% female, and 11% UAE nationals. The Results section gives a mean age of 32.1 years, SD 11.4, 44.4% female, and 11.4% UAE nationals. The paper does not resolve these differences.

## 2. 10.1016/j.jval.2024.05.016

**Contribution and status:** Completed valuation-method evaluation with two primary study components. It compares outputs from DCE with duration (DCEd) against cTTO and EQ-VT outputs. It does not present a new national value set as its main product.

**Objects:** The focal object is the agreement of EQ-5D-5L state values across valuation methods. Nonlinear and linear DCEd mixed-logit models are focal comparators. cTTO heteroskedastic Tobit, latent-scale DCE, and hybrid EQ-VT models are reference methods. The correction for nonlinear time preference is a material model component.

**Question and use:** Can a self-complete DCEd method produce values like the more costly interviewer-led cTTO or EQ-VT method and become a feasible valuation alternative?

**Evidence source and components:** Both components sampled adults in Trinidad and Tobago. The EQ-VT component included 1,079 home interviews. Each person completed ten cTTO tasks and twelve DCE pairs. The DCEd component received 1,581 completed surveys and retained 970 after quality exclusions. Each retained respondent completed eighteen split-triplet tasks. The components used different people, modes, recruitment periods, and demographic mixes.

**Design and analysis:** The EQ-VT component used trained interviewers and formal quality control. The DCEd component used an online survey, response-time and IP-address checks, and three design updates. The study compared values for all 3,125 states with correlations, scatterplots, Bland-Altman plots, mean differences, and mean absolute differences. It also compared predictions with observed means for the 86 cTTO design states.

**Output and scope:** Nonlinear DCEd values had high agreement with the EQ-VT model values. The linear time model produced a materially different scale. The output is a conditional method-comparison profile: DCEd is a promising substitute only when the model accounts for nonlinear time preference. One country comparison does not establish general interchangeability.

**Limits and transparency:** The final DCEd analysis excluded 611 people. Public-place recruitment failed its quality checks, and the study discarded those data. The method cannot always separate inattention from a strong preference for quality or length of life. The two components differed in sample composition and were about six months apart. EuroQol funded the work and reported no role in the study. The article states that supplementary material contains the OpenBUGS model code, but that supplement was not a separate input to this run.

## 3. 10.1186/s12955-023-02177-z

**Contribution and status:** Completed primary longitudinal measurement-property evaluation.

**Objects:** EQ-5D-5L dimension responses, the Hong Kong-value-set index, and EQ VAS are focal objects. ThyPRO-39 is the disease-specific reference measure. Patient-rated change at six months is the responsiveness anchor.

**Question and use:** Does EQ-5D-5L have adequate reliability, convergent validity, and responsiveness for health assessment and economic evaluation in adults with Graves' disease?

**Evidence source and target:** A convenience cohort of 125 adults with relapsed Graves' disease was recruited from three Hong Kong public hospitals. A total of 101 completed one month and 100 completed six months. The target is adult Graves' disease care in Hong Kong, not all thyroid disease.

**Design and analysis:** Participants completed EQ-5D-5L and ThyPRO-39 at baseline, one month, and six months. The reliability analysis used 64 people who reported unchanged health at one month. The study assessed ceiling effects, convergent validity, dimension agreement, index and VAS test-retest reliability, and responsiveness within patient-rated improved, unchanged, and worsened groups. It used prespecified correlation and reliability thresholds.

**Output and scope:** The output is a property-specific profile. The study found moderate-to-strong convergence, high dimension agreement, moderate index and VAS reliability, and responsiveness for worsening. It did not show responsiveness for improvement. A baseline ceiling effect limits detection of improvement.

**Limits and transparency:** Responsiveness results rely on small change groups, including 21 people who worsened. The one-month retest is long enough for clinical change to remain possible. Loss to follow-up and a convenience sample limit transfer. The data are restricted to authorized users. EuroQol funded the study, and the authors declared no competing interests.

## 4. 10.1007/s11136-025-04150-3

**Contribution and status:** Completed qualitative conceptual and content evaluation that supplies input to instrument development. It evaluates EQ-TIPS version 2.0 but does not produce a revised instrument version.

**Objects:** Experimental EQ-TIPS version 2.0 is focal. Its six dimensions, three response levels, “Today” recall period, “age-appropriate” wording, proxy instructions, and construct definition are component objects. EQ-5D-Y and EQ-5D are context objects for measurement across age groups.

**Question and use:** Is EQ-TIPS content understandable, relevant, and sufficiently complete for proxy-reported health-related quality of life in children aged 0 to 3 years, and what changes need further testing?

**Evidence source and target:** Thirty-three expert stakeholders from 15 countries took part. The three purposive groups included 17 EuroQol measurement experts, 11 pediatric health and development experts, and five pediatric instrument developers. The target is young children and their proxy reporters. Neither children nor caregivers supplied data in this study.

**Design and analysis:** Three online focus-group consultations used group-specific guides and breakout rooms. Two researchers used deductive and inductive thematic analysis. Facilitators and the wider research team checked the analysis. The consultations were iterative, so later groups did not receive an identical procedure.

**Output and scope:** Experts generally accepted the six dimensions and short format. They recommended tests of sleep and emotion, age-specific examples instead of “age-appropriate behavior,” clearer construct definition, and safeguards against measurement of caregiver spillover. They did not agree on whether communication and social interaction overlap or whether the age range needs more than one form. These are candidate changes, not a final EQ-TIPS revision.

**Limits and transparency:** Recruitment drew on the team's networks. The instrument-developer group was small and mainly from Europe and Canada. Breakout rooms helped participation but reduced whole-group consensus. Parenting experience and lived experience were not recorded. The study did not include caregivers, people with lower literacy, or direct child observation. Qualitative data are not public. EuroQol funded the work, and five authors reported EuroQol membership.

**Source conflict:** The paper states that it invited 44 experts and that 11 did not attend. The listed invited group counts are 21, 13, and 9, which total 43. The listed final counts are 17, 11, and 5, which total 33 and imply ten non-attendees. The paper does not resolve the arithmetic conflict.

## 5. 10.1186/s41687-025-00985-z

**Contribution and status:** Completed translation and cultural adaptation study with a small primary cognitive-test component.

**Objects and derivation:** The final paper and digital Modern Standard Arabic EQ-5D-Y-5L versions for Egypt are focal artifacts. They derive from UK English EQ-5D-Y-5L. The Egyptian Arabic EQ-5D-Y-3L informed wording consistency. Severity labels, pain wording, instructions, and EQ VAS text are component objects.

**Question and use:** Can a Modern Standard Arabic version preserve the intended meaning and severity order and remain clear to Egyptian children aged 8 to 15 years?

**Evidence source and target:** Eleven Egyptian children formed a convenience sample: six girls, five boys, six without health problems, and five with chronic conditions. The first eight completed card ranking, self-completion, and detailed debriefing. Three additional children tested revised terms and instructions. The observed evidence applies to two governorates and low-to-middle socioeconomic settings.

**Design and analysis:** Two independent forward translations and two independent back translations went through reconciliation and EuroQol Version Management Committee review. Eight children ranked 160 severity cards. Cognitive interviews tested comprehension and examples. Three further interviews tested revised physical-pain wording, an “item” prompt, and VAS instructions. A linguist completed final proofreading.

**Output and scope:** The paper produced a study-final Modern Standard Arabic EQ-5D-Y-5L. Ten of 160 card placements inverted adjacent severity levels. Younger children more often needed a second reading or confirmation, which led the authors to recommend interviewer support for ages 8 to 10. The paper did not report psychometric validation. Use in other Arabic-speaking countries needs local validation.

**Limits and transparency:** The small convenience sample cannot show national or cross-country equivalence. Parents were present during interviews. Arabic dialects and terms without direct equivalents limit transfer. The paper says that separate psychometric work will be published, but that work is not evidence in this application. Data are available on reasonable request. Two authors reported EuroQol membership and identified EuroQol as the funder.

## 6. 10.1016/j.jval.2024.03.2195

**Contribution and status:** Completed secondary measurement-property evaluation of instrument extensions.

**Objects:** Nine existing EQ-5D-5L bolt-ons are focal: breathing, cognition, hearing, self-confidence, skin irritation, sleep, social relationships, tiredness, and vision. The main comparison is EQ-5D-5L alone against EQ-5D-5L with one or more bolt-ons. PROMIS-29, PROMIS Global Health, SF-6D, and the Satisfaction with Life Scale are reference measures.

**Question and use:** Which bolt-ons add distinct and useful health information to EQ-5D-5L in population and condition-group surveys?

**Evidence source and target:** The analysis retained 1,587 adults from a 1,700-person Hungarian online general-population survey after 113 data-consistency exclusions. Soft quotas covered age, gender, residence, and region. Respondents reported physician-diagnosed chronic conditions. The target includes population surveys and candidate selection for later clinical studies.

**Design and analysis:** The fixed-order survey required all responses. The study assessed response distribution, ceiling reduction, divergent and convergent validity, structural validity, known-group validity, and explanatory power. It tested bolt-ons alone and in selected combinations with correlation, PCA, confirmatory factor analysis, relative efficiency, and regression.

**Output and scope:** Bolt-ons reduced the EQ-5D-5L ceiling and improved discrimination in different ways. Sleep and tiredness often added information across conditions. Hearing, vision, breathing, and skin irritation helped in related condition groups. Cognition performed less well than expected. The output supports population- and condition-specific candidate selection, not one universal bolt-on set.

**Limits and transparency:** Few respondents used the most severe bolt-on levels. Fixed item order can cause order effects. Self-reported conditions had no clinical severity measure. Level sum scores can treat different response profiles as equal. The CFA lacked enough related items for several bolt-ons, so one “other physical symptoms” factor does not prove that those constructs are the same. The study did not test responsiveness or value sets for bolt-ons. Both authors reported EuroQol membership. EuroQol and the Hungarian Academy of Sciences supported the work; EuroQol had no reported role.

## 7. 10.1007/s10198-018-0987-x

**Contribution and status:** Completed method evaluation and decision application. It combines secondary patient data with reanalysis of two Markov cost-utility models.

**Objects:** Directly observed EQ-5D-5L utilities are the reference input. Five EQ-5D mapping functions from SF-12 and SF-6D utilities are comparator inputs. Two dialysis Markov models, incremental quality-adjusted life-years, and incremental cost-effectiveness ratios are downstream objects.

**Question and use:** How does the choice between directly collected, mapped, and alternative utility inputs change cost-effectiveness estimates for hemodialysis versus peritoneal dialysis?

**Evidence source and derivation:** The health data came from 75 hemodialysis and 75 peritoneal-dialysis patients in Singapore. Separate ten-year societal Markov models represented patients with and without diabetes. The main chains were `EQ-5D-5L responses → English EQ-5D-5L value set → utility input → Markov model → QALY and ICER`, `SF-12 → five mapping functions → UK EQ-5D-3L values → model`, and `SF-12 items → SF-6D UK values → model`.

**Design and analysis:** Regression produced treatment-specific utility inputs. Each model simulated 10,000 patients, used 3% discounting, and applied 1,000 bootstrap replications to utility uncertainty. The study compared utility differences, incremental QALYs, and ICERs across methods.

**Output and scope:** In both models, mapped EQ-5D and SF-6D produced smaller incremental QALYs and higher ICERs than directly observed EQ-5D-5L. The output is a decision-impact comparison. It shows that the utility method can change a reimbursement result. It does not establish that all mapping methods fail in all settings.

**Limits and transparency:** The study used one small dialysis sample and one decision problem. Mapping functions came from younger or healthier US samples, not dialysis samples. The observed data used EQ-5D-5L, while the mappings predicted EQ-5D-3L. The source instruments also used different recall periods. UK value sets supplied preferences for Singapore data. Other model inputs were held fixed, and transition uncertainty was outside the reported bootstrap. EuroQol partly funded the study; two authors reported EuroQol membership.

**Objects and question:** EQ-5D-3L and EQ-5D-Y health-state wording are focal comparators.
