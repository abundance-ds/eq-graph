# H03 — Online personal utility functions and test-retest reliability

## 1. Identity and study type

- **Publication:** “Test-retest reliability of the Online Elicitation of Personal Utility Functions (OPUF) approach for valuing the EQ-HWB-S”; DOI `10.1007/s10198-025-01769-4`.
- **Study type:** measurement-property or psychometric study; valuation-method study; test-retest reliability study.
- **Source/provenance:** article file `corpus/1508-RA/doi_10.1007_s10198-025-01769-4.md`; extraction date 2026-08-17; manual Markdown extraction; review status: holdout extraction. Abstract, Materials and methods—Sample, Preference elicitation survey, Data analysis, and Results provide the extraction basis.

## 2. Population and samples

- **Target population:** German adults from the general population and adults with diabetes or rheumatic disease (Abstract; Materials and methods—Sample).
- **Recruitment source:** Bilendi online market-research panel; participants received €7.50. Test survey: 6–11 March 2023; retest: 20–30 March 2023, two weeks later (Materials and methods—Sample).
- **Initial and retest samples:** 330 participants completed the initial survey; 257 completed the retest. The original groups had 110 general-population, 110 diabetes, and 110 rheumatic-disease participants (Results—Sample characteristics).
- **Final analytic sample:** 220 matched participants after excluding illogical responses (21 at test, 18 at retest) and 19 unmatched responses; the abstract describes 73 general-population and 147 patient participants (Abstract; Results—Sample characteristics).
- **Subgroups:** general population (GP), diabetes (DM), rheumatic disease (RA), and combined patient sample (RA + DM) (Materials and methods—Sample; Data analysis).

## 3. Research concepts

- Individual and aggregate health-state utility; preference elicitation; reliability and consistency over time; utility decrements; dimension importance; QALY anchoring (Introduction; Materials and methods).
- The EQ-HWB-S has nine dimensions: mobility, daily activities, exhaustion, loneliness, cognition, anxiety, sadness/depression, control, and physical pain (Introduction).

## 4. Instruments and administration

- **EQ-HWB-S:** nine-item short instrument with five response levels per dimension. Role: valued to generate dimension-level utility decrements and value sets (Abstract; Introduction).
- **OPUF / Online elicitation of Personal Utility Functions:** preference-elicitation approach used to value the EQ-HWB-S. Role: survey method, not a separate health-state instrument (Abstract; Materials and methods—Preference elicitation survey).
- **Adapted EQ-VAS:** used in the warm-up task with participants’ own EQ-HWB state; exact version and language are not reported (Materials and methods—Preference elicitation survey).
- **Administration:** open-source online survey platform; two administrations two weeks apart. Respondents were German adults from a general-population or patient panel. Interviewer assistance, language, setting beyond online, and recall period were not reported (Materials and methods—Sample; Preference elicitation survey).
- **Respondent perspective:** participants valued hypothetical EQ-HWB-S states; the warm-up also asked for self-reported state. The paper does not report proxy respondents.

## 5. Methods, protocol, and task design

- **OPUF three-step method:** dimension ranking; dimension swing weights; intermediate-level ratings; then pairwise comparison of worst state `555555555` with dead and an anchoring task (Materials and methods—Preference elicitation survey).
- **Dimension ranking:** nine level-5 dimensions ranked from worst to least bad; the top-ranked dimension was assigned swing weight 100.
- **Swing weights and level ratings:** each dimension’s worst-to-best improvement was scored 0–100 relative to the top-ranked dimension; intermediate levels were rated 0–100 with best and worst anchors.
- **Anchoring:** preference between `555555555` and dead, followed by a 0–100 placement of the preferred state; anchoring values were censored at −1 (Materials and methods—Preference elicitation survey; Data analysis—Anchoring factor).
- **Test-retest design:** the same participants completed the OPUF survey twice, two weeks apart. Illogical responses and indifference in the anchoring task were exclusion criteria (Data analysis—Test-retest).

## 6. Analysis and statistical models

- **Utility model:** additive model. Utility decrements were obtained from level ratings × dimension weights, normalized and multiplied by the anchoring factor; utility was 1 minus the resulting decrement (Data analysis—Estimating the utility decrements).
- **Reliability analyses:** two-way mixed-effects intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) for swing weights, level ratings, anchoring factors, and individual utility decrements; Spearman rank correlation for dimension rankings and health-state rankings; percentage agreement and unweighted kappa for the worst-state/dead pairwise task (Data analysis—Test-retest).
- **Aggregate comparisons:** paired *t*-tests and Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests, with Q–Q plots for empirical distributions of aggregate decrements (Data analysis—Utility decrements and value set).
- **Regression analysis:** linear regression tested age, gender, sample group, and age-by-sample interaction as predictors of cumulative absolute test-retest differences (Data analysis—Impact of other factors on test-retest).
- **Software:** R version 4.3.1. Statistical significance threshold was *p* < 0.05 (Data analysis).

## 7. Products

- **Aggregate EQ-HWB-S value sets:** test and retest aggregate value sets were produced from mean utility decrements. Status: reliable at aggregate group level according to the authors’ conclusion (Data analysis—Utility decrements and value set; Conclusion).
- **Individual personal utility functions:** individual utility decrements were generated, but their test-retest reliability was poor to moderate; the study does not support treating them as stable products (Results; Conclusion).
- No new EQ-HWB-S instrument version or translation was produced.

## 8. Outcomes or measurement properties

- Test-retest reliability of dimension ranks, swing weights, intermediate level weights, anchoring factors, individual utility decrements, and aggregate utility decrements.
- Aggregate utility-decrement distributions and value sets; individual-level agreement and ICCs (Methods—Test-retest; Results—Utility decrements and value set).
- Key reported estimates: 42% selected the same top-ranked dimension; anchoring pairwise kappa 0.64 (95% CI 0.54–0.75); anchoring-factor ICC 0.12 (*p* < 0.05); aggregate overall mean decrement 0.08 in both tests and mean absolute difference 0.004 (Abstract; Results).

## 9. Principal findings and interpretation

1. Only about 36% of participants had significantly correlated dimension ranks, and 42% selected the same top-ranked dimension in both administrations (Abstract; Discussion).
2. Individual swing weights showed poor agreement in 70% of ICC values; intermediate level weights showed poor agreement in 70% and moderate agreement in 30% (Abstract).
3. The worst-state/dead pairwise task had moderate agreement, kappa 0.64 (95% CI 0.54–0.75), but anchoring factors had poor individual reliability, ICC 0.12 (*p* < 0.05) (Abstract; Results—Anchoring).
4. Aggregate utility decrements were similar across test and retest: mean overall decrement 0.08 in each administration and mean absolute difference 0.004. The authors interpret the aggregate value sets as reliable despite unstable individual responses (Results—Utility decrements and value set; Discussion; Conclusion).
5. The authors conclude that OPUF is reliable for aggregate EQ-HWB-S value sets only; individual-level tasks require refinement and further study (Conclusion).

## 10. Limitations and source issues

- Excluding participants with multiple illogical responses or anchoring indifference may overestimate agreement (Discussion—limitations).
- OPUF uses VAS scales and may be affected by response-spreading bias; this was not accounted for in the analysis (Discussion—limitations).
- Online-panel recruitment may select people with better digital literacy; findings came from a German sample and may have limited generalizability (Discussion—limitations).
- Reliability in this study is not directly comparable with conventional TTO or DCE reliability because OPUF assesses consistency across several compositional tasks (Discussion—limitations).
- The paper notes uncertainty about whether inconsistency arises from OPUF, EQ-HWB-S complexity, or online administration; interviewer-assisted and observed self-completion studies are suggested (Discussion; Conclusion).
