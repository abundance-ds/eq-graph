# H08 — Chichewa EQ-5D-Y psychometric evaluation

## 1. Identity and study type

- **Title:** *Psychometric performance of the Chichewa versions of the EQ-5D-Y-3L and EQ-5D-Y-5L among healthy and sick children and adolescents in Malawi* (article title, source title).
- **DOI:** `10.1186/s41687-023-00560-4`; project ID `20190200`.
- **Study type:** measurement-property or psychometric study; instrument evaluation. The paper evaluates Chichewa instrument versions. It does not develop a value set.
- **Source verification:** article file SHA-256 `2b0b92df4d11b6cf42011484f45492f87ed69e4b0c6e74f8fd40ebe4fd6dd74b`; byte count `116878` (manifest and file match).

## 2. Population and samples

- **Target and recruitment population:** healthy and sick children aged 8–12 years and adolescents aged 13–17 years in urban Blantyre, Malawi (Methods, “Participants, recruitment and procedure”).
- **Recruitment sources:** schools for healthy participants and the outpatient department at Queen Elizabeth Central Hospital for sick participants. Convenience sampling was used. Critically ill children and children unable to self-complete because they were not literate were excluded (Methods).
- **Completed sample:** 289 participants aged 8–17 years completed all three questionnaires; mean age 13.6 years and median age 14. Children: 98 (34%); adolescents: 191 (66%) (Results, “Participant characteristics,” Table 1).
- **Health subgroups:** healthy 95 (33%), acute 155 (54%), and chronic 39 (13%) (Table 1). Sex was reported as female 153 (56%) and male 121 (44%), with 15 missing. School-grade data had 10 missing (Table 1).
- **Analytic groups:** children (8–12), adolescents (13–17), combined age groups, and health-condition groups (acute and chronic) were used for psychometric analyses (Methods, “Psychometric analyses”). The empirical-validity analysis excluded respondents missing either EQ-5D-Y version (Methods, “Utility score performance”).
- **Planned sample:** 200 participants were calculated to provide 80% power for the minimum convergent- and discriminant-validity criteria (Methods).

## 3. Research concepts

- Child and adolescent health-related quality of life (HRQoL), feasibility, classification efficiency, informativity, convergent validity, discriminant validity, known-group validity, and empirical validity (Abstract; Methods).
- The study compares response-level versions of the youth descriptive system in a Malawian context, including the effect of age and health condition on performance (Introduction; Methods).

## 4. Instruments and administration

- **EQ-5D-Y-3L:** exact source name; five dimensions (mobility; looking after myself; doing usual activities; having pain or discomfort; feeling worried, sad or unhappy), three response levels, same-day recall, and EQ VAS from 0 (worst imaginable health) to 100 (best imaginable health) (Methods, “The EQ-5D-Y-3L”).
- **EQ-5D-Y-5L:** exact source name; the same five dimensions with five response levels (Methods, “The EQ-5D-Y-5L”). The paper evaluates the Chichewa (Malawi) version.
- **Instrument use:** both EQ-5D-Y versions were self-completed and psychometrically evaluated. They were scored with dimension level-sum scores and utility scores. Utility scores used US adult EQ-5D-3L and EQ-5D-5L value sets because youth value sets were unavailable at the time (Methods, “The EQ-5D-Y-5L”).
- **Comparator instruments:** Chichewa PedsQL™ 4.0 Generic Core Scales (GCS) child self-report for ages 8–12 and teen self-report for ages 13–17; 23 items across physical, emotional, social, and school functioning. A self-rated general-health question with five response categories was also administered (Methods, “Self-rated general health”; “The Pediatric Quality of Life…”).
- **Language and adaptation:** Chichewa versions were administered. Cross-cultural adaptation of both EQ-5D-Y versions was reported elsewhere and included forward and backward translation and cognitive debriefing among children and adolescents aged 8–15; the paper also states that PedsQL adaptation was being prepared elsewhere (Methods, “The EQ-5D-Y-5L”; “The Pediatric Quality of Life…”).
- **Interaction, channel, and setting:** self-completion; questionnaires were completed in clinic or classroom settings and collected by study staff. Interview assistance was not reported (Methods, “Participants, recruitment and procedure”).
- **Order:** EQ-5D-Y-5L was administered before EQ-5D-Y-3L, followed by the age-appropriate PedsQL GCS, to reduce possible order effects (Methods).

## 5. Methods, protocol, and task design

- **Research methods:** convenience-sample questionnaire administration; psychometric assessment; cross-instrument validity testing; response redistribution and Shannon informativity analysis.
- The paper defined problematic missingness as at least 5% per dimension; ceiling as health state `11111`; floor as `33333` or `55555`; and inconsistency as an EQ-5D-Y-5L response more than two levels from the corresponding EQ-5D-Y-3L response (Methods, “General performance and feasibility”; “Redistribution properties…”).
- The paper compared sum and utility scores between known healthy and sick groups and compared them with self-rated general health and PedsQL GCS scores. Empirical validity used relative efficiency with EQ-5D-Y-3L as reference and EQ-5D-Y-5L as comparator (Methods, “Known-group validity”; “Utility score performance”).
- No named study protocol or task design was reported. COVID-19 restrictions resulted in one data-collection wave.

## 6. Analysis and statistical models

- Data analysis used IBM SPSS 26.0.0 for Mac (Methods, “Psychometric analyses”).
- **Analyses:** frequency summaries for dimension responses and missingness; ceiling and floor effects; paired response redistribution; Shannon Index (H′) and Shannon Evenness Index (J′); Pearson correlations for convergent and discriminant validity; t-tests and effect sizes for known-group validity; relative-efficiency statistics for empirical validity; and sensitivity analyses using alternative dichotomisation of external health indicators (Methods).
- **Statistical model/test labels:** Pearson correlation; t-test; one-way ANOVA for school-grade comparisons; relative efficiency (ratio of squared comparator/reference t-statistics); Shannon H′ and J′ indices. No regression model was reported.
- Utility models were inherited from the US adult value sets: the 2005 US EQ-5D-3L MVH value set and the 2019 US EQ-5D-5L composite time trade-off (cTTO) value set. These were scoring sources, not models fitted to this study’s data (Methods, “The EQ-5D-Y-5L”).

## 7. Products

- **Produced/assessed product:** psychometric evidence for the Chichewa EQ-5D-Y-3L and EQ-5D-Y-5L versions among healthy and sick Malawian children and adolescents (Abstract; Conclusion).
- The paper does not produce a Chichewa or Malawian value set. It uses US adult value sets for utility scoring and states that EQ-5D-Y-5L value sets were not available at the time (Methods; Limitations).

## 8. Outcomes or measurement properties

- Missing data, ceiling and floor effects, response redistribution consistency, discriminatory power/informativity (H′ and J′), convergent validity, discriminant validity, known-group validity, and empirical validity (Abstract; Methods).
- Outcomes also included EQ-5D-Y level-sum scores and US-value-set utility scores, PedsQL GCS scores, and self-rated general-health categories (Methods).
- Test–retest reliability and responsiveness were not evaluated (Discussion; Limitations).

## 9. Principal findings and interpretation

- There was little missing data overall (<5% in most dimensions), but missing responses were a problem in younger children, especially for EQ-5D-Y-5L. The EQ-5D-Y-5L generally reduced ceiling effects compared with EQ-5D-Y-3L, but this did not occur in healthy participants. EQ-5D-Y-3L had a higher Shannon H′ index in all five dimensions; its J′ was also higher except for “feeling worried, sad or unhappy” (Abstract; Results, “General instrument performance and feasibility” and “Discriminatory power”; Table 4; Discussion).
- Convergent-validity correlations met the prespecified threshold of ≥0.4 at scale level for EQ-5D-Y-3L with teen PedsQL GCS and EQ-5D-Y-5L with child PedsQL GCS; results were mixed for dimensions/subscales (Results, “Convergent validity”).
- There was no significant gender difference and little association with age in the intended analyses. School-grade differences were significant among adolescents and all respondents, but not children (Results, “Discriminant validity”).
- Known-group validity was supported, with generally larger effect sizes in adolescents (>0.5); the child EQ-5D-Y-3L effect size was −1.15 versus 0.23 for EQ-5D-Y-5L (Results, “Known-group validity”).
- EQ-5D-Y-5L was 31–91% less efficient than EQ-5D-Y-3L for detecting differences in self-reported general health and 5–44% less efficient for the PedsQL total score. The authors interpret EQ-5D-Y-3L as particularly suited to ages 8–12 and EQ-5D-Y-5L to ages 13–17 in this setting (Abstract; Results, “Empirical validity”; Conclusion).

## 10. Limitations and source issues

- COVID-19 restrictions allowed only one data-collection wave, so test–retest reliability and responsiveness could not be assessed (Discussion; Limitations).
- Convenience recruitment was in urban Blantyre; generalisation to Malawi or sub-Saharan Africa is limited. Younger children may have had sub-optimal reading skills, and critically ill or non-literate children were excluded (Methods; Discussion).
- The EQ-5D-Y-5L had no available youth value set at the time; adult US value sets were used for childhood states. This limits interpretation of utility and empirical-validity results (Methods; Limitations).
- The source reports “EQ-5D-Y” in the sample description while the study evaluates the 3L and 5L versions. No separate recruited, completed, or analytic counts beyond the reported 289 completed participants were provided.
- Table 3 labels the all-participant column `N = 298`, while the Results participant count and Table 1 report 289. Table 6 labels the combined group “Age 7–17 years”, while recruitment and the principal age groups are 8–12 and 13–17 years. These source conflicts remain unresolved (Results, “Participant characteristics”; Tables 1, 3, and 6).

## 12. Extraction fit

- The proposed concepts fit the paper well: exact youth instrument versions, language, respondent age groups, self-completion, comparator instrument, psychometric properties, and separate tests are searchable.
- The paper’s use of adult value sets to score youth instruments needs a clear distinction between the instrument, the scoring/value-set source, and the model. It does not fit naturally as a study-fitted statistical model.
- Adaptation is only partly in scope: the paper evaluates already adapted Chichewa versions and refers the adaptation procedure to another source. A separate “adaptation reported elsewhere” status is useful.
