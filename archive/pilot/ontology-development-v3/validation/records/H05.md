# H05 — Appropriateness of the EQ-HWB for Use in Residential Aged Care: A Proxy Perspective

Source publication: Engel et al., *The Patient* 17(6), 2024; DOI 10.1007/s40271-024-00715-5.
Source location: article abstract, sections, tables, and conclusion.
Extraction date and method: 2026-08-17; manual extraction from the assigned article file.

## 1. Identity and study type

- Study: qualitative examination of the appropriateness of the EQ Health and Wellbeing measure (EQ-HWB) for proxy reporting in residential aged care.
- Study types: content-validity study; qualitative or stakeholder study.
- Setting: three not-for-profit residential aged care facilities in Melbourne, Victoria, Australia.
- Funding: EuroQol Research Foundation, project 150-RA. The article states that the authors' views are their own.
- Source: abstract; “Sample and Recruitment”; Funding.

## 2. Population and samples

- Target population: residents of residential aged care whose health and well-being may be reported by a proxy. Residents were not recruited directly.
- Recruitment population: family members or aged care staff recruited by convenience sampling from three facilities. Recruitment took place August–November 2022. Eligibility was age over 18 years and ability to read and speak English.
- Completed participant sample: 29 proxies: 9 family members and 20 aged care staff. Family participants were five daughters/sons, two sisters, and two partners. Staff included personal care workers, lifestyle coordinators, nurses, a manager, and a customer service officer.
- Analytic sample: qualitative data from the 29 proxies; the paper does not state a separate analytic count. One interview had two family members present.
- Important proxy distinctions: family visiting frequency varied; staff had different roles and employment durations. Staff were asked to select one resident and keep that resident in mind.
- Source: “Sample and Recruitment”; “Procedure”; Table 1; Results.

## 3. Research concepts

- Resident health and well-being and quality of life in residential aged care.
- Proxy-report feasibility and accuracy, including adherence to the proxy-person perspective, disagreement with a resident's own assessment, choice of proxy type, and response strategies.
- Appropriateness, comprehensiveness, clarity, and content validity of the EQ-HWB for this setting.
- Source: abstract; Introduction; Results, Themes 1 and 2.

## 4. Instruments and administration

- Instrument: “EQ Health and Wellbeing (EQ-HWB)”; preferred label: EQ-HWB. The study used the experimental 25-item long/profile measure, English version for Australia, with wording identical to the original UK version.
- Form and use role: proxy version 2; administered and evaluated for appropriateness/content validity.
- Respondent: family proxy or aged care staff proxy.
- Perspective: “proxy-person perspective”, defined as how the proxy thinks the person would describe their own health and well-being if they could tell the proxy. The paper contrasts this with the “proxy-proxy perspective”, based on the proxy's own impression.
- Interaction and channel: interviewer-assisted cognitive think-aloud completion in a qualitative interview; face-to-face in a facility or another place selected by the participant, with one interview by Zoom.
- Setting: residential aged care facilities or another participant-preferred place.
- Language: English instrument and interviews.
- Recall period: EQ-HWB “last 7 days”.
- Instrument features discussed: items on seeing, hearing, mobility, activities, personal care, sleep, relationships, cognition, emotions, control, pain, and discomfort; items 19–21 are positively worded while most other items are negative.
- Source: Introduction; “Procedure”; Results, Theme 2, “Recall Period” and “Layout and Instructions”.

## 5. Methods, protocol, and task design

- Research methods: qualitative cognitive think-aloud interviews and semi-structured interviews.
- Task: proxies completed all 25 EQ-HWB proxy-version-2 items while speaking aloud about the reasons for each response. Interview questions covered item understanding, response options, instructions, recall period, appropriateness in residential aged care, ability to proxy-complete, and adherence to the proxy-person perspective.
- Interview details: all sessions were audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim; duration 8–60 minutes, average 26 minutes. A topic guide was used. Data saturation was discussed by the two interviewers after each interview.
- Named protocol: not reported. Task block, item order, and other experimental task-design details: not reported.
- Analysis method: thematic analysis combining deductive coding guided by interview questions and inductive coding for new themes. Transcripts were coded line by line, then grouped into themes and sub-themes. The first two transcripts were analysed by three people; the remaining transcripts were coded by two people. Disagreements were discussed with a third person. NVivo was used.
- Source: “Procedure”; “Analysis”; Results Table 2.

## 6. Analysis and statistical models

- Analysis: deductive and inductive thematic analysis of interview transcripts.
- Coding framework: initially developed from the first two transcripts and then applied and refined for the remaining transcripts.
- Statistical model: not reported or applicable. No quantitative model, hypothesis test, or score comparison was used for the current analysis.
- Source: “Analysis”.

- Produced product: qualitative evidence on EQ-HWB content and use in residential aged care, including recommendations for further wording, example, response-option, recall-period, layout, and instruction work.
- Product status: evidence and recommendations produced; no revised EQ-HWB version was produced in this study.

## 9. Principal findings and interpretation

1. Proxies generally viewed the EQ-HWB as covering important domains for residential aged care, but they identified ambiguity, double-barrelled items, inappropriate examples, and perceived repetition. They recommended modifications to item wording and examples. (Source: abstract; Results, Theme 2; Conclusions.)
2. Some proxies did not consistently maintain the proxy-person perspective. They reported difficulty when the resident had severe cognitive impairment or dementia, and they sometimes disagreed with what the resident might report. (Source: Results, Theme 1, “Adhering to the Proxy Perspective” and “Acknowledging Disagreement”.)
3. Proxy ability varied by item and proxy type. Observable physical domains were easier than psychological domains; family proxies often lacked information about sleep and pain, while staff could refer to medication records when answering pain-related questions. Participants stated that staff who knew residents well might provide more accurate reports, but staff knowledge also varied. (Source: Results, Theme 1, “Feasibility/Ability” and “Choice of Proxy Type”.)
4. The 7-day recall period was often considered too short by family proxies, while one staff proxy considered it consistent with other aged-care assessments. Proxies used heuristics and sometimes selected midpoint responses when uncertain, which the authors describe as central-tendency bias. (Source: Abstract—Results; Results, Theme 1, “Response Process (Finding Evidence)”; Results, Theme 2, “Recall Period”; Discussion.)
5. The authors interpret self-report as the default for residential aged care, with proxy reporting sometimes necessary for residents who cannot self-report. They recommend further work on proxy perspectives, proxy selection, and the EQ-HWB's wording and examples. (Source: Key Points for Decision Makers; Discussion; Conclusions.)

## 10. Limitations and source issues

- Convenience sampling was used rather than purposive sampling.
- The study collected proxy views only and could not compare proxy responses with residents' own assessments.
- The authors state that focusing interviews and analysis on problems with the measure may have produced views that were not sufficiently balanced by positive comments.
- The study examined the EQ-HWB only; the authors call for head-to-head qualitative and quantitative comparison with other generic and aged-care measures.
- The paper does not report a separate analytic-participant count, a named protocol, or a revised instrument as an output. One interview included two family members.
- No direct source conflict is reported for the extracted study facts.
- Source: Discussion, final paragraph; Declarations; Results.
