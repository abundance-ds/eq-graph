## Aim and setting

The study extends a corrective approach for time trade-off (TTO) utilities to **composite time trade-off (cTTO)**. The motivation is that the linear QALY model assumes no discounting of future life years and no loss aversion, while both can bias utilities upwards. The extension covers lead-time TTO for health states considered worse than dead (WTD), and is intended for EQ-5D valuation. Data were collected in the Netherlands in March 2020. The study began with computer-assisted personal interviews on a university campus (36 interviews through 13 March); COVID-19 restrictions led to 114 digital interviews through Zoom. The sample was 150 general-public respondents recruited by a marketing company to broadly reflect age, sex and education, without strict quotas; complete loss-aversion and discounting data were available for 147. Interviews took about one hour and participants received EUR 30. [Source: Abstract; EXPERIMENT—Sample and data collection strategy; Table 1]

## Instruments, tasks and protocol

The interview used the EQ-VT cTTO protocol. The demographic part included age, sex, income, subjective life expectancy, religion, beliefs about life after death and euthanasia. Participants completed the EQ-5D-5L descriptive system and EQ VAS (0 = worst, 100 = best imaginable health). The cTTO tutorial used a wheelchair example and a top-down titration procedure. Two practice states were 21211 and 35554. Six randomly ordered study states covered mild to very severe health: 11211, 13313, 35332, 22434, 24443 and 55555. Conventional TTO used 10 years in impaired health and yielded BTD values as T/10. Lead-time TTO used 10 years in full health followed by 10 years in the impaired state and yielded WTD values as (T−10)/10. [Source: NOTATION AND PRELIMINARIES; EXPERIMENT—Design; cTTO introduction and main cTTO task]

Loss aversion was measured with the non-parametric method of Abdellaoui et al. using three chained indifferences. The loss-aversion coefficient λ was measured with reference points of 10 and 20 years; the gauge gain was 5 years. Discounting, defined as curvature of life-duration utility L(T), was measured with the direct method using 20-year profiles that alternated full health and chronic back pain (EQ-5D-5L 21211). Five indifference points estimated L(T) at 1/8, 1/4, 1/2, 3/4 and 7/8; linear interpolation supplied non-parametric corrections. Area under the normalized curve (AUC) classified L(T) as concave (>0.5), linear (=0.5) or convex (<0.5). An exponential form was also fitted by non-linear least squares. A validation task explained QALYs and asked respondents whether each elicited utility was exactly right, should be higher or should be lower; respondents could adjust it on a −1 to 1 slider. [Source: EXPERIMENT—Elicitation of loss aversion and discounting; Validation task]

## Corrective specifications and analysis

The prospect-theory extension uses a reference point and λ for losses in life duration. For cTTO, two reference-point assumptions were tested: **correction based on constant alternative** (the unchanged alternative is the reference point) and **correction based on maximum BTD time** (10 years is the reference point for both TTO and lead-time TTO). Significance was α = 0.05; repeated paired tests also report Bonferroni-adjusted p values. Analyses compared personal and digital data, described λ and discounting, compared elicited, respondent-confirmed and corrected utilities by paired t tests/Wilcoxon tests, and classified whether respondent changes followed the corrective direction. [Source: CORRECTIVE APPROACH FOR CTTO; Data analysis]

## Findings and interpretation

Of 150 interviews, two ended before the discounting/loss-aversion tasks and one ended for religious objection to life-year trade-offs. There were 894 cTTO state observations. Digital and personal interviews showed no difference in quality indicators or cTTO utilities. Non-trading (utility 1) occurred for 134 states (15%) and all-in trading (−1) for 118 (13%); 40 respondents assigned at least one state the same value as 55555, although only 15 did so after excluding non-trading.

Loss aversion was common: with a 10-year reference point, 82% were loss averse, 14% gain seeking and 4% loss neutral; with 20 years, 73%, 13% and 14%, respectively. Mean λ did not differ between reference points (paired t(146) = 0.07, p = 0.94); median λ was 2.00 (10-year reference) and 1.88 (20-year reference). Median discounting was close to linear (AUC 0.50; ρ = 0), but individual heterogeneity was large: L(T) was concave for 37%, linear for 13% and convex for 49%. [Source: RESULTS—Descriptive statistics; Table 3]

Confirmed utilities were generally lower than elicited utilities, but the confirmed mean was higher for 22434 and the difference was not significant for 24443; 38–69% left each utility unchanged, and changes were more often downward. Mean elicited versus confirmed values were 0.96 versus 0.95 (11211), 0.80 versus 0.76 (13313), 0.52 versus 0.47 (35332), 0.23 versus 0.26 (22434), −0.21 versus −0.15 (24443), and −0.68 versus −0.72 (55555). Corrected utilities were generally lower than elicited and confirmed utilities (all p < 0.03 except state 35332 under constant-alternative correction, p = 0.053). The correction direction agreed with respondent changes for a significant majority in four of six states. The two reference-point approaches agreed for mild states but differed for severe states, especially 24443 and 55555. [Source: RESULTS—Elicited, confirmed and corrected cTTO utilities; Tables 4–5; Figure 1]

The authors conclude that correction has a downward effect, driven mainly by loss aversion rather than average discounting. They caution that correction can “overcorrect”, adds measurement error, removes the cTTO lower bound of −1, and models loss aversion only for life duration. The normative benchmark remains unclear. Future work should identify respondents’ reference points and assess psychological and normative implications.
