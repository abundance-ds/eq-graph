# Measuring quality of life in autistic children and young people: comparing the performance of common generic health related quality of life instruments

## Aim and population

The study compares measurement performance of five generic paediatric HRQoL instruments in autistic children: PedsQL, EQ-5D-Y-5L, EQ-5D-Y-3L, CHU9D and KIDSCREEN-27. Attributes were acceptability, feasibility, test-retest reliability, floor/ceiling effects, convergent validity and known-groups validity. Subgroups were reporter type (child self-report versus caregiver proxy), age (5–12 versus 13–18) and gender (male versus female). Data came from the Australian Paediatric Multi-Instrument Comparison (P-MIC) Study, conducted November 2021–August 2022 through Pureprofile. Eligibility was caregiver report of a health-professional-diagnosed ASD in a child aged 5–18 (Abstract; Methods—Participants).

The analysis included 510 children; 327 (64.1%) self-reported and 183 (35.9%) were proxy-reported. Mean age was 10.6 (range 5–18), 70.2% were male, and caregivers were 83.7% female. The initial online survey took 15–30 minutes; a four-week follow-up took about five minutes. Children over seven could self-report if their caregiver considered them capable; otherwise the caregiver completed all instruments. All questions were required and there were no missing data. KIDSCREEN-27 was not repeated at follow-up because of survey burden and lived-experience feedback (Methods—Data collection; Results—Sample characteristics).

## Instruments and scores

PedsQL (Pediatric Quality of Life Inventory 4.0 generic core) has 23 five-level items, proxy and self forms for ages 8–12 and 13–18, one-month recall, and physical, emotional, social and school functioning domains; score range 0–100, with higher scores better. EQ-5D-Y-5L has five five-level items and EQ-5D-Y-3L five three-level items; both are proxy/self measures referring to “today,” with mobility, looking after self, usual activities, pain or discomfort, and worried or sad. Their level-sum score (LSS) ranges are 5–25 and 5–15, respectively, and lower scores indicate better HRQoL. CHU9D has nine five-level “today” items (worried, sad, pain, tired, annoyed, schoolwork or homework, daily routine, activities and sleep), LSS 9–45, lower better. KIDSCREEN-27 has 27 five-level items, one-week recall, five domains (physical wellbeing, psychological wellbeing, autonomy and parent relations, peers and social support, school environment), score 27–135, higher better. Main analyses used total score/LSS rather than utility scores; sensitivity analyses used available EQ-5D-Y-3L and CHU9D value sets (Methods—Instruments).

## Methods

Stata 18 was used. Acceptability was a five-point very difficult–very easy rating. Feasibility was REDCap completion time; times over 10 minutes were top-coded and medians/IQRs reported. Test-retest reliability used a two-way mixed-effects, single-measure, absolute-agreement ICC, excluding children whose health changed. ICC <0.5 was poor, 0.50–0.74 moderate, 0.75–0.90 good and >0.90 excellent. Ceiling or floor effect was ≥15% at the best or poorest possible level for all items. Convergent validity used Spearman correlations with PedsQL as the reference because no gold standard exists. Known groups were mainstream versus specialist school (proxy for ASD severity), without versus with a special healthcare need (SHCN), and normal versus “abnormal” or “of concern” Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire (SDQ) behavioural difficulties. Independent t-tests required p<0.05 and Cohen’s d >0.5; d 0.2–0.49 was small, 0.5–0.79 moderate and ≥0.8 large (Methods—Psychometric analyses).

## Results

EQ-5D-Y-5L had the highest “easy” or “very easy” acceptability (63.1%); KIDSCREEN-27 the lowest (54.7%). Median completion time ranged from 32.1 seconds (EQ-5D-Y-5L) to 123.5 seconds (KIDSCREEN-27). Moderate test-retest reliability was observed for PedsQL (ICC 0.56, 95% CI 0.39–0.69), EQ-5D-Y-5L (0.60, 0.47–0.71) and EQ-5D-Y-3L (0.58, 0.43–0.69). CHU9D was poor (0.42, 0.22–0.58). No total-sample ceiling or floor effects were found. EQ-5D-Y-3L had subgroup ceiling effects in ages 13–18 (19.1%), males (15.6%) and self-report (15.0%); no floor effects were found (Results—Acceptability to Floor and ceiling effects).

PedsQL correlations with other instruments were strong: EQ-5D-Y-5L 0.63–0.66, EQ-5D-Y-3L 0.63–0.66, CHU9D 0.63–0.66 and KIDSCREEN-27 0.53. For school setting, EQ-5D-Y-5L alone reached moderate known-groups validity (d=0.51, p<0.001); PedsQL, EQ-5D-Y-3L and CHU9D had significant but small effects (d 0.32–0.41), and KIDSCREEN-27 did not differentiate (p=0.598). For SHCN, PedsQL (d=0.64) and CHU9D (d=0.54) had moderate effects; EQ-5D-Y instruments and KIDSCREEN-27 had small effects. Every instrument differentiated SDQ normal versus “abnormal”/“of concern” groups with large effects; PedsQL was highest (d=1.24). Utility-score sensitivity results were largely unchanged (Results—Known-groups validity; Sensitivity).

## Interpretation and limitations

PedsQL and EQ-5D-Y-5L had the strongest overall performance. The CHU9D showed good convergent validity and SHCN discrimination but poor reliability. KIDSCREEN-27 had positive, strengths-based framing and good convergent validity but poorer acceptability and longer completion. Instrument choice depends on intended use: shorter instruments support low burden; preference-accompanied EQ-5D-Y-3L and CHU9D support utility and QALY analyses. The study did not assess responsiveness because too few children reported health change. Online-panel selection, unverified ASD diagnosis, possible self-report verification limits and small subgroup samples restrict generalisability. School setting may be an imperfect ASD-severity proxy. The authors suggest interviewer administration to support self-report for younger children or those with literacy needs (Discussion; Conclusion).
