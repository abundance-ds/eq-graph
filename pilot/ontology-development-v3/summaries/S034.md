## Purpose and scope

The paper reviews existing patient-reported outcome measure item-selection criteria and adds requirements specific to preference-based measures used for economic evaluation. Patients complete the measure, but a representative general-public sample usually values health states for QALY scoring. The authors present 22 criteria across accurate completion, domain coverage, current QoL/interpersonal comparability, and suitability for valuation. Criteria 1–12 are universal questionnaire-design concerns, 13–19 are especially important for preference-based measures, and 20–22 relate specifically to valuation. Application is iterative, evidence-based and subject to discussion when criteria conflict. [Source: Abstract; Item selection criteria; Table 1]

## Criteria 1–12: full and accurate completion

Items should have an appropriate reading level, be short, avoid double negatives, ambiguity, jargon, excessively colloquial terms and multiple questions in one item. They should avoid excessively personal or ethically inappropriate questions, items irrelevant to some respondents, reliance on knowledge outside the individual’s experience, and value-laden terms. Examples include avoiding “I felt I had no control – none of the time”, compound “anxiety or depression” when the respondent may answer only one component, and questions about employment or family that may not apply to everyone. The authors also note that intrusive or negative items can cause missing data or leave vulnerable respondents distressed. [Source: Table 1; Accuracy and completion]

## Criteria 13–19: coverage and comparability

Criterion 13 says items should not be too extreme or too mild; with few items per domain, item-response theory can test coverage across the full latent construct, including poor QoL. Criteria 14–19 support interpersonal and intertemporal comparability required for QALYs: avoid differential item functioning; comparisons with other people, expectations or “usual”; comparisons over time; items that do not fit the specified recall period; and response options without clear order. DIF can arise when groups with the same latent construct interpret an item differently. Examples include gender differences for crying and terms such as “satisfied”, “life” or “bad temper” that may draw on personal norms or periods outside the stated time. [Source: Table 1; Ensure items cover the full range; Ensure items are suitable for measuring QALYs]

## Criteria 20–22: valuation suitability

Criterion 20 requires every valued item to represent an important life domain for which respondents would trade an improvement against other domains. Criterion 21 says an item should not attribute a problem to a circumstance, because “because of my pain I am unable to see my friends” leaves unclear whether pain or social activity is being valued. Criterion 22 requires response options to point in the same direction; switching whether “often” is good or bad across items can confuse respondents and affect quality-adjustment values. The paper discusses positive versus negative framing: positive items may support completion and mood, but mixed directions can harm validity and scoring. Items involving effects on other people (for example “I am a burden to others”) can also blur whether the respondent’s feeling or the actual burden is valued. [Source: Table 1; Ensure items are suitable for measuring QALYs]

## Development process and methods

The criteria were derived from existing PROM lists, presentations and discussions with researchers and advisors. Item selection follows a conceptual framework identifying independent domains, usually with one item per domain because valuation tasks commonly include only 8–9 items. Evidence should combine qualitative work with patients, carers, social-care users and general-public valuers, psychometric/DIF/IRT or Rasch analyses, and translation work. The authors stress that item wording must be clear when shown alone as part of a health-state description, not only in the questionnaire context. [Source: Background; Item selection criteria]

The authors also discuss translation and cultural context: literal translation is not enough; each criterion should be evaluated in qualitative interviews to support consistent interpretation. Conflicts are expected. For example, a domain may be relevant but ethically sensitive, or positive framing may support mood but create scoring-direction problems. [Source: Other considerations; Conclusion]

## Implications and limitations

Applying criteria before valuation can reduce problems in state design and preference elicitation. The paper does not report a participant study, sample, statistical model or new value set; evidence consists of methodological synthesis and expert discussion. It states that criteria require judgment informed by intended questionnaire completers and preference respondents. The central use case is a preference-based measure whose values are anchored to dead (0) and full health (1), with QALYs assuming interpersonal and intertemporal comparability.
