## Aim and procedure

The study translated and culturally adapted the EQ-5D-Y-5L into Modern Standard Arabic for use in Egypt and potentially other Arabic-speaking countries. The UK English EQ-5D-Y-5L was the reference, supplemented by the Egyptian Arabic EQ-5D-Y-3L. The work followed EuroQol Group translation guidelines approved by the Version Management Committee (VMC), with ethics approval CL 3060 from Cairo University Faculty of Pharmacy (Methods).

The four stages were independent forward translation, independent back translation, cognitive debriefing, and final proofreading. Two professional native Arabic translators produced paper and digital forward versions; the research team reconciled them and sent a report of controversial words and proposed decisions to the VMC. Two native English translators unfamiliar with the source back-translated the Arabic version. The team compared translations with each other and the UK source, then submitted changes to VMC reviewers (Methods, “Forward translation” and “Back translation”).

## Cognitive debriefing and population

Face-to-face interviews followed VMC guidance: card ranking exercise, child self-completion of the translated questionnaire, and structured detailed debriefing. The intended age quotas were 8–9, 10–11, and 12–15 years, with health-condition and sex variation. Eleven Egyptian children aged 8–15 years were recruited by convenience sampling between January 2023 and January 2024 from Cairo and Menoufia, different schools, and low-to-middle socioeconomic settings. Six were healthy and five had chronic conditions; six were girls and five boys; mean age was 10.6. Eight children completed all three cognitive steps, and three additional children completed self-completion and debriefing only (Results; Table 1).

In the card ranking exercise, eight children ranked five severity cards for four dimensions (160 card rankings; “usual activities” was omitted because its qualifiers duplicate other dimensions). Ten rankings (6.25%) were incorrect, mainly confusing “some problems” and “a lot of problems” or levels 2 and 3 for pain or discomfort and worried, sad, or unhappy. The children later demonstrated the intended hierarchy (Results, “Ranking exercise”).

## Instrument and wording findings

The EQ-5D-Y-5L is a self-complete generic child HRQOL instrument with walking about, looking after myself, doing usual activities, having pain or discomfort, and feeling worried, sad, or unhappy; each dimension has “no problems,” “a little bit of a problem,” “some problems,” “a lot of problems,” and “cannot/extreme problems.” EQ VAS is 0–100. The Arabic translation retained consistent qualifiers across the first three dimensions and used simpler, non-literal qualifiers for the final two where literal Arabic terms were difficult to distinguish (Background; Results, “Forward and back translation”).

The back translation required one digital-source modification: the Arabic term for “item” back-translated as “element” or “value,” so it was replaced. Cognitive interviews identified ambiguity in “having pain or discomfort”: children could interpret pain emotionally despite “in my body.” VMC and the team changed the term to one that usually denotes physical pain. Five children did not understand the translated “item”; four suggested “question,” which was tested and accepted in three additional interviews. Mean questionnaire completion was 5.2 min (SD 2.18); all found it clear and easy, while one 9-year-old found it too long. Six needed confirmation for general or VAS instructions. The authors recommend interviewer-based administration for ages 8–10 (Results; Table 3; Discussion).

The final Standard Arabic version was judged acceptable, but regional dialect differences and complex grammar require local validation. Psychometric performance was not reported in this study and was planned separately. The authors state that the version may support comparison across Arabic-speaking studies after local adaptation (Discussion; Conclusion).
