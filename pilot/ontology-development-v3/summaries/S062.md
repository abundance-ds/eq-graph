## Aim, setting, and participants

The study adapted the UK English EQ-5D-Y-3L (Y-3L) and EQ-5D-Y-5L (Y-5L) for Singapore English and assessed the relevance and comprehensiveness of the EQ-5D-Y descriptive system (DS) in Singaporean children. The study followed Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research and COSMIN content-validity guidance. Ethical approval came from the National Healthcare Group Domain Specific Review Board and the National University of Singapore Institutional Review Board (Methods).

For adaptation, six paediatricians and two primary school teachers formed the local expert panel. Cognitive debriefing took place in June–August 2020 with 11 children: six tested Y-3L and five tested Y-5L. The mean age was 9.8 years (SD 1.7). For content validity, 14 children aged 8–15 years were interviewed in December 2021–January 2023. Eight were healthy and six had illness; five had a chronic condition and two had an acute condition, with one child in both categories. The sample was 50% female and included Chinese, Malay, and Indian children. Healthy children were recruited through parents and social media; children with chronic disease were referred by a paediatrician. Purposive sampling sought variation in age, gender, ethnicity, and chronic disease. Saturation was declared after no new themes emerged in the last three transcripts (Methods > Study participants; Results > Content validation; Tables 2–3).

## Instruments and methods

The Y-3L DS has five dimensions: **mobility**, **looking after myself**, **doing usual activities**, **experiencing pain or discomfort**, and **feeling worried, sad, or unhappy**. It has three response levels. The Y-5L uses the same dimensions with five severity levels. Both include EQ VAS from 0 (the worst health you can imagine) to 100 (the best health you can imagine) (Background).

The adaptation followed EuroQol cultural-adaptation and translation guidance, with local expert review. Cognitive interviews used verbal probes and paraphrasing. For Y-5L, children also ranked severity labels shown in random order on separate showcards. The content-validity interview had two parts. First, open questions elicited direct and indirect experiences of poor health without a health questionnaire. Second, children completed the Singapore English Y-3L and discussed comprehensiveness and relevance. The Y-5L was not used in this part because response levels were not the focus. Framework analysis coded spontaneous concepts into Physical health, mental well-being, social relationship, medical conditions & treatment, and health promotion knowledge and behaviours. Two independent coders coded transcripts line by line; content analysis examined the second part (Methods > Cross cultural adaptation; Content validation; Data analysis).

## Adaptation findings

The final Singapore English versions made minor changes to the UK English wording. “Washing and dressing myself” became “showering or wearing clothes myself”. “Looking After Myself” became “Taking Care of Myself”. “Walking about” became “Walking around” after expert review and “Walking” after interviews to prevent confusion with walking in circles. The instruction changed from “Under each heading” to “Under each question”. EQ VAS instructions added “Then” to clarify that children must mark the line and write the number. For “HAVING PAIN OR DISCOMFORT”, examples were added: “aches, breathlessness, itching, feeling like vomiting”. All changes were endorsed by the EuroQol Research Foundation (Results > The Singapore english Y-3L and Y-5L; Table 1).

All children ranked Y-5L severity levels correctly. The adaptations were understandable to local children as young as 8 years. Examples of discomfort came from the EuroQol standard list and were retained only when all interviewed children understood them.

## Content-validity findings

The five EQ-5D-Y dimensions were spontaneously elicited. Mobility was linked to injury and walking. Taking care of myself was linked to showering and wearing clothes. Usual activities included school, play, and sport. Pain or discomfort was understood mainly as physical and was viewed as an indicator of illness. Feeling worried, sad, or unhappy was viewed as an important mental-health dimension. Most children found the DS relevant and sufficient.

The broader framework grouped poor-health concepts into Physical Health, Mental Well-being, and Social Relationship. Missing or under-covered concepts included relationships with friends and family, appetite, sleep, and additional mental-health content. Children suggested bolt-on items for social relationship and appetite. One child interpreted Usual activities outside a health context, showing a possible response issue (Results > Content validation; Fig. 1; Table 4).

## Implications and limitations

The study provides Singapore English Y-3L and Y-5L versions with local wording and examples. It supports the relevance and comprehensiveness of the Y-3L DS in Singapore, while identifying social relationship and appetite as possible local bolt-ons. Psychometric performance, construct validity, test–retest reliability, and responsiveness were not tested. EQ VAS content validity was not assessed. Children aged 8 may have limited experience with poor health, relative importance was not studied, and the sample was small (Discussion; Study limitation; Conclusion).
