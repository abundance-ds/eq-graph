## Aim and participants

The study examined how five adult respondent perspectives change EQ-5D-Y-3L value sets and relative importance (RI) of dimensions, using the Online elicitation of Personal Utility Functions (OPUF) tool and DCE. UK adults were randomised to: 4-year-old child, 10-year-old child, child of unspecified age, another adult, or own health. Online Prolific recruitment used age, gender and ethnicity quotas. Target sample sizes were 300 for each arm except 1000 for the 10-year-old child arm. Final sample was 2080 after 121 respondents were dropped for extreme anchoring responses (Study Overview; Perspectives; Results).

EQ-5D-Y-3L contains mobility, usual activities, self-care (labelled “looking after myself”), pain or discomfort and feeling worried, sad or unhappy, each with no, some or a lot of problems. The OPUF survey order was randomised. OPUF involved dimension ranking, swing weighting (most important dimension fixed at 100), level rating (0–100), and an anchoring-VAS task comparing 33333 with being dead. The DCE followed the EQ-5D-Y-3L valuation design with 15 pairwise tasks per participant (OPUF Tool; DCE Methodology).

## Analysis

OPUF produced individual-level disutilities from dimension weights, level ratings and anchoring. Five social utility functions were created and anchored to full health/dead. Responses were censored at −1; respondents rating 33333 or dead at 1 in the anchoring-VAS task were dropped. RI was the dimension’s contribution to the total disutility of 33333 versus 11111, with bootstrap 95% confidence intervals. DCE used conditional logit with ten level dummies. DCE coefficients remained unanchored, so RI was calculated from relative coefficients; Delta-method confidence intervals were used. A pooled DCE model added perspective interactions with the 10-year-old child arm as baseline (Analysis).

## Findings

OPUF utility for 33333 was 0.202 (4-year-old child), 0.262 (10-year-old child), 0.198 (unspecified child), 0.183 (other adult) and 0.158 (adult self). The percentage choosing dead over 33333 was 18.6%, 17.7%, 25.0%, 25.4% and 31.8%, respectively. Thus child-perspective sets were narrower and assigned higher worst-state values than adult-perspective sets (Table 3).

Across OPUF perspectives, pain or discomfort was most important and looking after myself least important. Child perspectives placed less importance on looking after myself and more on pain or discomfort than adult perspectives. DCE showed the same direction, with wider RI variation: looking after myself was least important for all child perspectives and less important for the 4-year-old child than older child perspectives; mobility was least important in adult perspectives. Pain or discomfort was most important in every perspective and less important for adults; worried, sad or unhappy was also less important for adults (Results; Figs. 2–3).

The paper concludes that perspective affects both scale and relative dimension importance. OPUF utilities were higher for child perspectives than for another adult or self. The authors note that OPUF lacks a temporal component, so the effect cannot be explained only by willingness to trade life-years; death aversion may contribute. Limitations are separate samples per perspective, possible underlying sample differences, online self-completion and previous concerns that interviewer-led OPUF may improve data quality. The paper recommends stakeholder agreement on perspective before valuation studies because perspective can affect QALYs and reimbursement decisions (Discussion; Conclusion).
