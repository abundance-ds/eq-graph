## Aim and data

The study directly compares EQ-VT-derived EQ-5D-5L value sets with crosswalk-based 5L sets at country level (Abstract; Aims). It identified 25 TTO- or DCE+TTO-based EQ-5D-3L sets and 32 EQ-VT 5L sets, yielding 19 paired sets from 18 countries; the USA contributed two pairs. Each 3L set was converted to 5L with the Van Hout crosswalk algorithm and compared with the corresponding national EQ-VT set. Three parallel studies (Hungary, Romania, USA) had 3L and 5L valuation data from the same respondents and were analysed separately to reduce population and methodological differences.

## Comparison methods

The analysis compared ranges, means, 55555, percentage of negative values, and five isolated one-level-5 states (51111, 15111, 11511, 11151, 11115). Severe states were also examined using level sum score (LSS) >15. Pearson, Spearman, and Lin concordance correlations, mean absolute difference (MAD), *t* tests, scatterplots, and Bland–Altman plots were used. Publication-year gap was a proxy for time between 3L and 5L valuation studies. The study applied both sets to the 7,933-person multi-instrument comparison (MIC) dataset and tested mean utility differences, severe-state differences, and responses reporting “unable to walk about” (Methods).

The conceptual comparison concerns three crosswalk assumptions: sound underlying 3L methods, continuing validity of old preferences, and representativeness of the European response-mapping population. A fourth issue is wording: the 3L mobility extreme is “confined to bed,” while 5L uses “unable to walk about.” The authors hypothesize that respondents may value these labels differently even if self-report response mapping is accurate.

## Findings

Spearman correlation ranged 0.831–0.989 and was below 0.9 in 11 of 19 pairs; Lin concordance ranged 0.556–0.978. The average MAD was 0.149, below 0.1 in only five pairs. Differences in percentage negative ranged +22.5 to −18.8 percentage points, and range differences +42.7 to −18.4%. EQ-VT had a lower 55555 in ten pairs and a wider range in nine. For states with one level 5 and four level 1s, no country preserved the ranking of the five dimensions when moving from crosswalk to EQ-VT (Results).

Bland–Altman plots showed mobility level-5 states as a separate group in 15 pairs, including a completely separate band in ten. Reverse-crosswalk analyses in the three parallel countries similarly separated mobility level-3 states. Longer time gaps tended to be associated with lower correlations; excluding parallel pairs, the correlation between gap and Spearman rho was −0.50. In the MIC dataset, means differed significantly for all 19 pairs. EQ-VT means were higher in 14 pairs and lower in five. Severe-state means differed significantly for every country; among “unable to walk about” respondents, 13 pairs had lower crosswalk means, four lower EQ-VT means, and two no significant difference (Results).

## Interpretation and implications

The findings indicate poor agreement and support the conclusion that crosswalk and EQ-VT sets should not be used interchangeably. The mobility label change appears to have a substantial effect even when 3L and 5L data come from the same people and similar methods. Other drivers may include TTO versus cTTO/DCE, QC, changing mental-health and usual-activity preferences, old valuation samples, and the European crosswalk population. Differences can alter HTA comparisons, mean utilities, severe-state utilities, and potentially reimbursement decisions.

The authors recommend direct EQ-5D-5L valuation where possible. Crosswalks remain an interim option for very small populations where EQ-VT is infeasible. They suggest testing whether using the 5L mobility wording in 3L improves comparability and note nonlinear-time-preference DCEd as a possible resource-saving alternative. The main limitation is the small number of country pairs, which prevents estimating the relative contribution of time, labels, methods, and modelling. Funding and data/code declarations are reported in the paper; no conflict is reported.
