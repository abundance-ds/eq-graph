## Aim, setting, and population

The study examined health status, QoL, happiness, and life satisfaction among people living beside the polluted and frequently flooded Ciliwung River in Bukit Duri, South Jakarta, Indonesia. The Ciliwung sample included 204 respondents aged 17+ who lived beside the river and spoke Bahasa Indonesia. Three comparison samples were used: a socio-demographically matched non-riverbank control (*n* = 204), Jakarta inhabitants (*n* = 305), and Indonesian general population (*n* = 1041) (Abstract; Methods, “Respondents”). Riverbank respondents were recruited door-to-door with help from the Ciliwung Merdeka organization; non-response could not be counted reliably. General-population data came from multi-stage stratified quota sampling; matched controls were selected by gender, age group, education, and monthly income.

## Instruments and administration

Four measures were collected in face-to-face home interviews by trained interviewers. The official Indonesian Bahasa Indonesia EQ-5D-5L version measured health status with mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort, and anxiety/depression, each with no, slight, moderate, severe, and unable/extreme problems. EQ-VAS used a 20 cm vertical scale with “the best health you can imagine” and “the worst health you can imagine”. The Indonesian value set converted profiles to an index; EQ-VAS was transformed to 0–100. WHOQOL-BREF (Indonesian version) measured physical, psychological, social relationships, and environment domains on transformed 0–100 scores with a 4-week timeframe and was self-administered on paper. Happiness used an 11-point Happiness Thermometer for today, past month, and whole life. Life satisfaction used Cantril’s Self-Anchoring Striving Scale for now, five years ago, and five years ahead. A 0–10 financial-situation VAS ranged from poorest to richest imaginable (Methods, “Procedure”; “Measures”). Respondents were encouraged to read the questionnaires themselves; interviewers read items aloud when literacy, education, or eyesight caused difficulty.

## Analysis

EQ-5D-5L levels 2–5 were combined as “any problems” and compared with level 1 using chi-square tests. Means/SDs were calculated for EQ-VAS, index, WHOQOL-BREF domains, happiness, life satisfaction, and financial situation. Group comparisons used t-tests or Wilcoxon rank-sum tests after Shapiro-Wilk normality checks. One-way MANOVA tested domains simultaneously; multiple linear regression controlled for gender, age, education, income, ethnicity, religion, and marital status. Cohen’s *d* quantified effect size; *p* < 0.05 was significant (Methods, “Analysis”).

## Principal findings

Compared with matched controls, Ciliwung respondents had significantly lower physical WHOQOL-BREF QoL and felt less happy today, but significantly higher life satisfaction at all three timepoints, perceived financial situation, and EQ-VAS. EQ-5D-5L index scores were similar: Ciliwung 0.91 (SD 0.15), matched control 0.91 (0.11), Jakarta 0.90 (0.12), general population 0.91 (0.11). EQ-VAS was 81.74 (15.39) for Ciliwung versus 78.85 (13.24) matched control, 77.50 (13.15) Jakarta, and 79.41 (14.03) general population (Results, “Comparison between groups”, Table 2).

WHOQOL-BREF physical score was 63.31 (11.56) for Ciliwung versus 69.66 (10.60) matched control; effect size was moderate (0.57). Ciliwung social and environment domains were also lower than Jakarta/general population. Ciliwung’s lower actual income contrasted with higher perceived financial situation. The paper reports fewer anxiety/depression problems and higher perceived health despite polluted, flood-prone conditions (Results; Discussion).

## Interpretation, implications, and limitations

Possible explanations include adaptation/resilience to yearly floods, relative comparison with similarly poor neighbours, and social capital: long-standing community ties, mutual support, local work opportunities, and community organizations. The authors suggest relocation to better housing may improve physical QoL and happiness, but may not improve perceived health, life satisfaction, or financial situation. They recommend post-relocation quantitative follow-up and qualitative interviews for policy evaluation (Discussion; Implications; Conclusion).

Limitations include cross-sectional design, possible recruitment/organizational bias, uncertainty about relocation tension during data collection, and inability to infer relocation effects. The riverbank sample was matched on selected demographics but remained lower education/income than Jakarta and general-population samples. The paper reports no clinical diagnosis or value-set construction; its EQ-5D-5L index is an outcome in a comparative population study.
