## Aim and rationale

The paper introduces direct elicitation of personal utility functions (PUFs), asking respondents to specify relative importance of EQ-5D dimensions, levels, and interactions rather than valuing a subset of health states and inferring the rest (Abstract; Introduction). The method is deliberative: respondents see feedback, reflect, and may change earlier responses. The pilot tested feasibility and acceptability and showed how individual PUFs could be aggregated into a social utility function (SUF), or value set.

## Design, setting, and sample

Initial testing used convenience samples in England and Australia, followed by a 30-person professional pre-pilot. The main pilot recruited adults from the south of England by door-knocking. Eligibility was age 18+, informed consent, and no interviewer-judged cognitive impairment preventing completion. Four trained research-agency interviewers conducted one-to-one home interviews in December 2015. A one-day training course and instruction booklet were used; the Excel tool was not checked until fieldwork ended. Seventy-six interviews were completed, lasting 25–90 minutes (mean 46, median 45) (Methods; Results, “Sample”).

## Instrument and tasks

The study used a three-level simplification of EQ-5D-5L: levels 1, 2, and 3 corresponded to no, moderate, and extreme problems (the original 5L levels 1, 3, and 5). The seven sections were:

- **A, warm-up:** self-reported current and worst-ever EQ-5D health state and EQ VAS.
- **B, dimension ranking:** rank five dimensions by which problems respondents least wanted; ties were allowed.
- **C, dimension rating:** rate each worst-to-best dimension “swing” on 0–100, with the most important at 100; visual bar/pie feedback supported deliberation.
- **D, level rating:** allocate 100 points between extreme-to-moderate and moderate-to-no improvement for each dimension; ties were allowed.
- **E, paired-comparison validation:** two tailored comparisons, one easier and one close in expected PUF, generated by an algorithm from sections C/D.
- **F, location of dead (LOD):** up to five TTO-type choices between 10 years in a state and immediate death, using bisection to locate dead within 15–16 ranked states.
- **G, interactions:** two comparisons tested whether the value of an improvement changed when another dimension had moderate problems (Methods, “Survey instrument”).

## Analysis and findings

Descriptive means, medians, standard deviations, frequencies, and correlations were used. Ranking ties were treated by average-rank (AVG) and skipped-rank (EQ) methods. PUF weights were established on 0–1 scales, anchored at dead using section F, and aggregated by mean or median after excluding one outlier. Excel, Stata, and R were used.

Pain/discomfort and mobility were most important overall; usual activities was least important (section B). Section C mean ratings were PD 90.9, MO 87.0, UA 80.8, AD 82.1, SC 80.3; implied ranking correlated with section B at 0.899 (AVG) or 0.883 (EQ). Nine respondents failed to assign any dimension 100, 15 assigned multiple dimensions 100, and two gave anxiety/depression a rating of 0 (Results, Tables 2–3). In section D, the median intermediate-improvement rating was 50 for four dimensions; 64.5% mixed equal and unequal ratings, 10.5% gave at least one improvement 0, and 10.5% gave at least one 100 (Table 5).

The first tailored paired comparison generally selected the higher estimated utility state; the second, designed to be close, split evenly. In LOD, 18 respondents (23.7%) never chose immediate death, implying dead below all EQ-5D states; two (2.6%) never chose 10 years in a presented state, leaving dead above the mildest tested state. Interaction comparisons favored improvements without other problems for 72.4% and 75.0% (section G), suggesting context-dependent improvement value. Feedback was mixed: some preferred deliberative tasks, while others found point allocation, hypothetical states, interaction tasks, diagrams, cards, length, and repetition difficult.

The SUF used 60 respondents because one interviewer failed to activate the tailored Excel macro. The SUF minimum was −0.667; 11112 had value 0.85. Mean level-2 decrements ranged 0.1536–0.1801 and level-3 decrements 0.3146–0.3653. Ranking was PD, MO, AD, SC, UA, with caution due to the small pilot (Results, “Using PUF data”).

## Implications and limitations

The PUF approach appears feasible and may complement state-based methods. Limitations include validating with the very state tasks criticised, duration dependence of the dead anchor, inability to construct PUFs when dead lies below all states, difficult interaction questions, interviewer influence, rudimentary Excel, 45-minute interviews, and normative assumptions about interpersonal comparability and mean versus median SUF. The full EQ-5D-5L remains untested. Funding was EuroQol Research Foundation project 2014170; no conflict statement beyond the paper’s declarations was reported.
