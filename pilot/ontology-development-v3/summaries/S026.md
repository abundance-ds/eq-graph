## Aim and context

This paper assesses digital interviewer-assisted TTO through videotelephony (**tele-TTO**) as an alternative when geographical, financial or situational constraints limit face-to-face computer-assisted personal interviews (CAPI). It reviews possible advantages and disadvantages, then reports lessons from a Netherlands project that changed mode during the COVID-19 outbreak. It is not a randomized comparison: personal interviews occurred first and tele-TTO later, and the project did not collect sign-up, response-rate, cancellation or respondent-experience data. The author therefore treats comparisons as cautious evidence. [Source: Abstract; Introduction; Tele-TTO Applied in Practice]

Tele-TTO can remove travel for interviewer and respondent, support recruitment in remote or rural areas, reduce travel cost, and allow flexible scheduling. Possible disadvantages are partial loss of visual cues, more difficulty building rapport, audio/video disruptions, distracting respondent environments, additional preparation, digital-literacy selection effects, more no-shows and lower response. Loss of visual cues may make it harder to assess engagement or understanding; lack of proximity may also make some respondents more willing to disclose socially undesirable views. [Source: Possible Advantages of Tele-TTO Interviews; Possible Disadvantages of Tele-TTO Interviews]

## Project design and administration

Data collection ran March–May 2020 in the Netherlands. A marketing agency used quota sampling for age and sex. The first 36 interviews were face-to-face; after public-facility closures were announced on 13 March, 113 remaining interviews were completed by tele-TTO. The EQ-VT-based proprietary software used composite TTO in random order for six EQ-5D-5L states: 11211, 13313, 35332, 22434, 24443 and 55555. Personal interviews placed respondent and interviewer at one table. Tele-TTO used Zoom audio/video and shared screen; the interviewer operated the software and respondents stated preferences verbatim. Conventional TTO used a 10-year impaired-health duration; lead-time TTO used 10 years full health plus 10 years impaired health. [Source: Tele-TTO Applied in Practice; Appendix A]

The project had practical changes. A day-before telephone reminder, added to email, appeared to reduce no-shows and allowed questions about videotelephony and preparation. Short-notice tele-TTO cancellations could sometimes be replaced immediately. Audio problems were managed by dropping video while retaining audio and screen share, or by telephone audio with computer video. The author found tele-TTO more demanding for the interviewer because of fewer visual cues, rapport difficulties and connection problems, and recommends fewer consecutive interviews or more break time. [Source: Lessons Learned from Tele-TTO]

## Comparison and analyses

Appendix B compared personal (n = 36) and tele-TTO (n = 113) observations for central tendency, variance, clustering and decision quality. Means used t tests; medians used Wilcoxon tests; variances used Levene and Brown–Forsythe tests; modal clustering and distributions used chi-squared tests. Quality indicators followed the EQ-5D-5L quality-assurance work of Alava et al., including non-trading, all-in trading, logical consistency and distinct-value counts. [Source: Appendix B—TTO Method and Data; Decision Outcomes; Decision Quality]

No mean or median differences were observed for any state (all mean p > 0.155; all median p > 0.164) or for all states combined. Variance did not differ except for state 35332 on Levene’s test (p = 0.04); combined variance tests were non-significant. Clustering differences were non-significant. For personal versus tele-TTO, the proportion of utilities equal to 1 was 10.6% versus 14.0%, and equal to −1 was 16.7% versus 14.5% (chi-squared p = 0.37). Data-quality indicators were generally similar; strict logical-consistency violations were 3.7% versus 0.9% (p = 0.01), while non-trading was 16.7% versus 14.4% (p = 0.49) and all-in trading 10.6% versus 14.0% (p = 0.25). [Source: Appendix B—Central Tendencies and Variance; Clustering; Decision Quality; Tables 2–4; Figures 2–3]

## Selection effects, limitations and implications

Tele-TTO respondents were younger (mean 40.30, SD 14.89) than personal respondents (50.25, SD 15.55; p = 0.001), and less often married (21% vs 41%; p = 0.02). Education, parental status, household income and religion did not differ. This indicates possible selection effects linked to the “digital divide”, internet access and digital literacy. Mode was not randomly assigned, and COVID-19 timing, interviewer learning and respondents’ growing videotelephony experience could confound the comparison. Respondent and interviewer rapport was not measured. The paper suggests an explicit comparative study, evaluation of alternative devices/software, mobile-phone adaptations such as larger fonts, and hybrid choice of mode. It concludes that tele-TTO appears feasible, flexible and low-cost with mostly similar outcomes, but may introduce selection effects and process burdens.
