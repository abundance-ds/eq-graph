# Is EQ-5D-5L Better Than EQ-5D-3L Over Time? A Head-to-Head Comparison of Responsiveness of Descriptive Systems and Value Sets from Nine Countries

## Aim and data

The study compared responsiveness of EQ-5D-3L and EQ-5D-5L descriptive systems and value sets. It used a German rehabilitation sample and a Polish stroke sample. The German sample had 230 baseline and 224 follow-up observations, with 225 participants in the paired analysis. It was a multicentre longitudinal study of rehabilitation patients, measured at rehabilitation start and about 4–6 weeks later before discharge. The Polish sample had 112 stroke patients, measured one week after stroke and about four months later. It was a single-centre observational longitudinal cohort. Paper-and-pencil EQ-5D-3L and EQ-5D-5L were completed at both times (Methods > Paired 3L–5L Descriptive Data; Table 1).

The German sample was mostly female (69.6%), mean age 57 years, and included spine/back disease, arthropathies, affective disorders, neurotic, somatoform and stress-related disorders, and inflammatory arthropathies. The Polish stroke sample had mean age 70.6 years, 51.8% female, and included cerebral infarction and intracerebral hemorrhage. In rehabilitation, 3L/5L order was randomised and retained. In stroke, 5L was always first. Stroke analyses used patient and proxy reports (18.7% proxy).

## Instruments, value sets, and analyses

EQ-5D-3L and EQ-5D-5L both have mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort, and anxiety/depression. For comparison, 3L levels were recoded to 0, 2, and 4, while 5L levels were recoded to 0, 1, 2, 3, and 4. Nine pairs of country-specific value sets were applied: Canada, China, England/United Kingdom, Germany, Japan, The Netherlands, Korea, Poland, and Spain. US 3L/5L value sets derived from the same sample and protocol were also used as a methodological comparison. Crosswalk values were not the main basis for the new comparisons.

Cross-sectional analysis compared level sum scores (LSS), health profiles, and standardised differences with Wilcoxon signed-rank tests. Descriptive responsiveness used changed level responses (“moves”), improved patients, Paretian Classification of Health Change (PCHC), and probability of superiority (PS). Value responsiveness used standardised response mean (SRM) and standardised effect size (SES). Relative efficiency was the ratio of 5L to 3L SRM or SES. Improvement anchors were self-rated health in rehabilitation and modified Rankin Scale (mRS) or Barthel Index (BI) in stroke (Methods > Analysis).

## Main findings

- Cross-sectional results consistently showed more reported health problems on 3L than 5L. Standardised 3L–5L differences ranged from -0.029 (rehabilitation baseline mobility) to 0.087 (rehabilitation follow-up pain/discomfort). Overall differences were 0.022–0.040, with the largest differences for pain/discomfort, anxiety/depression, and usual activities (Results > Descriptive Cross-Sectional Results; Table 2).
- Descriptive responsiveness favoured 5L for moves. Average moves per respondent were 0.22 (3L) versus 0.46 (5L) in rehabilitation and 0.34 versus 0.58 in stroke. PCHC classified 50% (3L) versus 60% (5L) of rehabilitation patients as improved, but 51% (3L) versus 44% (5L) of stroke patients as improved. PS was modestly higher for 5L: rehabilitation 0.57 versus 0.59 and stroke 0.59 versus 0.63 (Results > Descriptive Responsiveness; Fig. 1).
- Value responsiveness was better for 5L across all value sets in rehabilitation. In stroke, 3L was generally better. SRM and SES were medium to large for improved subgroups. Relative efficiency was moderately to strongly in favour of 5L for rehabilitation and slightly to moderately in favour of 3L for stroke (Results > Responsiveness of Values; Fig. 2; Table 3).
- The “confined to bed” 3L mobility level was important. It was rarely used in rehabilitation, which reduced 3L responsiveness. In stroke, 21% used it at baseline and 7% at follow-up, which increased 3L value responsiveness. The direction of 3L bias changed over time and by disease severity (Results; Discussion).

## Interpretation and limitations

The authors conclude that descriptive-system differences are the main driver of 3L–5L responsiveness results. 3L tends to overestimate problems, which can underestimate or overestimate QALYs depending on condition and severity. For moderate conditions improving toward mild states, 3L may underestimate QALY gains. For severe stroke-like conditions, 3L may overestimate gains. The authors judge 5L to produce more accurate health and QALY estimates in these data.

Limitations include possible order effects in stroke, one anchor in rehabilitation, small stroke improvement subgroups, proxy reports, two countries and two disease settings only, and country-specific translations and response behaviour. A more refined system than 5L could also complicate valuation (Discussion; Conclusion).
