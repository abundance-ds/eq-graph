# A qualitative study to understand public views on the relative value of health gains for children and young people in Australia compared to adults

## Aim and design

The study examined why people may assign different social value to health gains for children and young people versus adults. It used think-aloud, semi-structured interviews and thematic analysis. Forty-one Australian participants were interviewed online over 27 March–20 July 2023: adolescents (n = 7), non-parents (n = 11), parents of healthy children (n = 8), and parents of children with health conditions (n = 15). Interviews averaged 45 minutes and were conducted by three female interviewers using Zoom and shared screens. Recruitment used a commercial qualitative panel for adolescents/non-parents and the QUOKKA Pediatric Multi-Instrument Comparison Study frame for parents. [Source: Abstract; Methods—Recruitment and participants; Data collection]

The survey had six components: consent/introduction video, seven PTO questions, comprehension feedback, reasons for PTO answers, three attitudinal questions about healthcare prioritisation, and demographics. Four PTO tasks concerned 2- or 5-year life extension; three concerned mental health, mobility, and pain/discomfort. Participants chose between interventions for an adult aged 40 or 55 and a younger person in one of 13 age categories from one month to 24 years. Half used an unforced arm with “no preference”; the rest had forced choices. Interviewers probed differences between PTO and attitudinal answers and presented alternative viewpoints. [Source: Methods—Survey design; Figures 1–2]

## Analysis and themes

Thematic analysis used a framework-analysis process. Three researchers independently coded two transcripts, double-coded five more, discussed and revised codes, and one researcher applied the final framework. Nine themes were grouped into decision-making on PTO questions and attitudinal questions. [Source: Methods—Qualitative research approach and data analysis; Results—Table 1]

Participants interpreted life extension through expected life stages and experiences, awareness of death, and the difference between two and five years. Quality-of-life choices considered usual activities (school, work, sport), coping/adaptation, social expectations and self-confidence, and the ability to understand the condition. Views about coping differed: 19 participants saw adults as better able to cope and prioritised children, while eight saw children as better supported or adaptable and prioritised adults. Twenty-six considered effects on family; nine considered productivity, taxation or broader social contribution. [Source: Results—Themes 1–3]

Decision patterns included personal experience, emotion, life experiences, calculating age after treatment, proportional gain and “fair innings” reasoning. Ten participants had emotional responses, especially to babies or children in pain. Fifteen used “fair innings” arguments that older adults had experienced more life; older adults’ family, work and societal roles could instead support prioritising them. Ten participants were uncomfortable making trade-offs; five unforced-arm participants, all over 40, chose “no preference” throughout or for life extension. [Source: Results—Theme 4; Decision making criteria]

Some participants considered long-term consequences despite instructions not to, and five had difficulty imagining severe pain. Four could not imagine a one-month-old with a mental-health condition. These difficulties are relevant to interpreting PTO responses. Attitudinal answers often differed from PTO because “children” and “adults” were broad categories, while PTO specified ages. Some participants viewed the resource constraint as a reason to increase resources rather than prioritise by age. In attitudinal questions, 10 supported equal Medicare access, two prioritised children, and others used largest-gain or ability-to-pay criteria. [Source: Results—Themes 5–9]

## Findings, implications and limitations

The qualitative patterns help interpret the quantitative PTO study: one-month to two-year-olds were less favoured for averting mental-health problems, all child ages were more favoured for averting pain, 55-year-olds led to more child prioritisation than 40-year-olds, and younger respondents more often prioritised younger people. Participants re-evaluated health gains according to activities, coping, understanding, anticipated experiences, family impact and fairness. Adolescents focused on school, sport, travel and future experiences; older participants more often stressed adults’ contribution to family, work and society. Parents of children with health conditions generally prioritised younger people, but some prioritised adults because children had support or parents were needed as caregivers. [Source: Discussion—Summary]

The authors report selection bias from qualitative-research volunteers, unequal subgroup sizes, Australian cultural specificity, all-female interviewers and limited formal reflexivity. Sensitive discussions of challenging views were light. The meta-level question of whether PTO captures relative age weights was not answered. Data are not publicly available as full transcripts; only consented quotes and the minimal dataset in the paper/supporting files are available. [Source: Data Availability; Discussion—Strengths and limitations; Conclusion]
