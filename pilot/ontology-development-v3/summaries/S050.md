## Aim, setting, and sample

The study examined socioeconomic inequalities in health-related quality of life (HRQoL) during the early COVID-19 pandemic across China, Italy, the Netherlands, Sweden, the UK, and the US. It compared education, annual household income, and work status as SES indicators. It is a secondary analysis of the first POPCORN wave: web surveys ran 22 April–5 May 2020 in China, Italy, Netherlands, UK, and US and 26 May–1 June in Sweden. Dynata recruited adults aged 18–75 years from voluntary panels; data were anonymous and no missing questionnaire items were possible (Abstract; Methods, “Study design and study population”).

Official instrument translations were used when available; otherwise, questionnaires were translated into each country’s official language and back into English with translation software and bilingual native-speaker checks (Methods, “Data collection and outcome measures”).

Of 19,432 completers, 1,825 without income data were excluded. The analytic sample was 17,607: China 3,146, Italy 2,866, Netherlands 2,736, Sweden 2,839, UK 2,964, and US 3,056. Median age was 43; 43.5% reported chronic conditions; 81.7% were classified employed and 18.3% unemployed (Results, “Study population”).

## Instruments and variables

The primary outcome was EQ-5D-5L Level Sum Score (LSS), summing five dimensions: mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort, and anxiety/depression. Each has levels 1 “no problems” through 5 “extreme problems”; LSS ranges 5 (“full health”) to 25 (“worst possible health state”). LSS was selected instead of country-specific utility values to avoid heterogeneity from national value sets and valuation methods. EQ VAS was secondary: a vertical 0–100 scale of health today, anchored by “the worst health you can imagine” and “the best health you can imagine” (Methods, “Data collection and outcome measures”).

Education was recoded by ISCED-97 into low (1–2), middle (3–4), high (5+). Income was country-specific bottom 20%, middle 60%, and top 20%. Original work-status responses were “in work: employee,” “in work: self-employed,” out of work >1 year, out of work <1 year, looking after others, student, retired, and unable to work; these were collapsed to Employed (employee, self-employed, student, retired) versus Unemployed (out of work, looking after others, unable to work) (Methods, “SES indicators”).

## Analysis and findings

Country comparisons used Kruskal–Wallis; SES comparisons used Mann–Whitney U tests. Multiple linear regressions for LSS and EQ VAS were fitted for the pooled sample and each country, adjusted for age, sex, and chronic disease status, with all SES indicators retained. No formal multiple-testing correction was applied (Methods, “Statistical analysis”).

Overall median LSS was 6 (IQR 3). China had the best median (5); Italy, Sweden, and US had 7; Netherlands and UK had 6 (Results, “Health outcomes”). Low education had pooled median LSS 7 versus 6 for high education. The largest education gap was UK (2 LSS points); Netherlands and US differed by 1. Italy showed a non-significant reversal; China and Sweden had equal medians but significant rank differences. Low income had higher LSS in Netherlands, Sweden, UK, and US, but not China or Italy. Unemployed respondents had pooled median LSS 8 versus 6 employed; unemployment differences were significant in all countries. EQ VAS generally supported lower scores for unemployed and higher scores for high-income respondents (Tables 2–3).

Regression showed lower education associated with poorer LSS in Netherlands, Sweden, and pooled data; UK and Sweden showed some U-shaped education patterns, China a reverse association, and Italy/US no association. Chronic disease and unemployment were the strongest and most consistent predictors for both outcomes. Limitations include cross-sectional design, income non-response, online-panel selection, collapsed work categories, cross-country cultural reporting differences, pandemic timing, and use of LSS rather than utility values (Discussion; “Strengths and Limitations”).
