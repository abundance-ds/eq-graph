# The reliability and validity of the isixhosa version of the euroqol toddler and infant populations (EQ-TIPS) health related quality of life instrument

## Aim, design, and sample

The study tested validity and reliability of the isiXhosa EQ-TIPS for South African children aged 0–36 months. A cross-sectional non-probability design assessed validity. Longitudinal data from crèche children assessed test–retest reliability. Caregivers had to be proficient in isiXhosa. Children in intensive care, terminally ill children, and premature children who had not reached corrected age were excluded (Methods > Participants).

The total sample was 181 caregiver–child pairs: 63 inpatients, 63 outpatients, and 55 crèche children. Most caregivers were mothers. The crèche group had more grandmothers and older children. The crèche group provided 52 second EQ-TIPS administrations one week after baseline; six children who changed health were excluded, leaving 46 for test–retest analysis. The study was conducted in a children’s hospital, crèches, and toddler play groups in Cape Town. Ethical approval was HREC 012_2019 (Methods > Procedure; Results > Descriptive statistics; Table 1).

## Translation, instruments, and scoring

The isiXhosa EQ-TIPS was translated using the EQ-5D-Y protocol. Two translators independently forward- and back-translated the measure. A consensus version was tested in cognitive interviews with 10 caregivers of children aged 0–36 months. No changes were made because no comprehension problems were reported.

EQ-TIPS has six dimensions: **movement**, **play**, **pain**, **relationships**, **communication**, and **eating**. Each has three levels: no problems, some problems, and a lot of problems. The EQ VAS measures general health from 0 (worst health imaginable) to 100 (best health imaginable). Health states use six digits, such as 111223. There are 729 (3^6) possible states. EQ-TIPS had no preference-based score at the time of the study. A level sum score (LSS) was used, from 6 (no problems on every dimension) to 18 (a lot of problems on every dimension), with level labels treated as numeric. The instrument was intended to support future preference weights (Methods > Measures > EQ-TIPS).

Concurrent validity used the Ages and Stages Questionnaire, Third Edition (ASQ), the FLACC (Face, Leg, Activity, Cry, Consolability) pain scale, and a self-designed dietary information questionnaire. ASQ domains were communication, gross motor, fine motor, problem solving, and personal social. FLACC scores pain behaviour from 0–2 on each item. The dietary questionnaire had eight yes/no items and a 0–8 total score (Methods > Measures).

All instruments were self-completed by caregivers in a standardized research pack; no caregiver training was required. Hospital caregivers completed the pack in a private room or at the child’s bedside, and crèche caregivers returned the sealed pack after three days (Methods > Procedure).

## Analysis

Age groups were 0–12, >12–24, and >24–36 months. Feasibility used missing values and ceiling/floor distributions. Known-groups validity used chi-square results and Kruskal–Wallis tests for dimensions, EQ VAS, and LSS across inpatient, outpatient, and crèche groups and age groups. Spearman’s Rho tested correlations with ASQ, FLACC, and dietary scores. Multiple regression examined the effect of dimension levels on EQ VAS; outliers above 2 standard deviations were excluded. Test–retest reliability used Cohen’s weighted Kappa for dimensions and intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC) for EQ VAS and LSS. Analyses used IBM SPSS Statistics Version 28 and TIBCO Statistical Version 13. Significance was p≤0.05 (Methods > Data management and analysis).

## Findings

- There was one missing descriptive-system response. No floor effects were found in hospital groups. Problems were most frequent for pain and eating. Pain differed across the three health groups (χ²=37.21, p<0.001), and LSS differed across groups (KW-H=25.9, p<0.001). The 0–12-month group reported more problems than the >24–36-month group (KW=28.52, p=0.004). EQ VAS did not discriminate health groups (KW-H=3.92, p=0.141) (Results > Known groups; Fig. 1–2).
- Concurrent validity was weak to moderate and significant for hypothesised relationships. Pain correlated moderately with FLACC (r=0.43, p<0.001). Eating correlated with dietary information (r=-0.37, p<0.001), and other hypothesised correlations were weak but significant. Negative correlations reflect higher EQ-TIPS problem levels against better ASQ or dietary scores (Table 3).
- Regression showed EQ-TIPS dimensions explained only 4% of EQ VAS variance, increasing to 5% after excluding nine outliers. No coefficient significantly detracted from EQ VAS. This indicates weak evidence for the VAS as understood in this sample (Table 4).
- Test–retest reliability in 46 stable crèche children was significant for movement (weighted κ=0.54), play (κ=0.37), pain (κ=0.20), and eating (κ=0.29). Relationships and communication were not significant (κ=-0.04 for each). EQ VAS reliability was ICC=0.72; LSS ICC=-0.10 and was not significant (Table 5).

## Implications and limitations

The authors conclude that isiXhosa EQ-TIPS is feasible, valid, and reliable for very young children in South Africa, with caveats for EQ VAS and some dimensions. Future work should test age-related differences, responsiveness, stable clinical groups, and understanding of “general health” and EQ VAS. The crèche group had higher age and more grandparent proxies, independent completion was difficult for some caregivers, and missing data were concentrated in that group. Age and health group were confounded. The study did not have a preference-based score, and test–retest changes were not recorded. Results may not generalise beyond isiXhosa-speaking Cape Town caregivers (Discussion; Conclusion).
