# A Systematic Review of Studies Comparing the Measurement Properties of the Three-Level and Five-Level Versions of the EQ-5D

## Aim and search

The review consolidated evidence on EQ-5D-3L and EQ-5D-5L measurement properties. It searched PubMed, EMBASE, PsycINFO (EBSCO), and the EuroQol Research Foundation website. Searches covered English and German publications from 2007 to January 2018. Eligible studies were empirical adult studies or conference papers that compared final 3L and 5L versions. Experimental versions, non-human studies, other languages, non-empirical reports, and papers without full text were excluded (Abstract; Methods > Data Sources, Search Strategy, Study Selection).

Twenty-four studies were included. Two researchers independently screened records and a third reviewer resolved differences. Data extraction covered sample, country, design, outcome measures, distributional properties, informativity, inconsistencies, responsiveness, and test–retest reliability. A nine-item critical appraisal tool assessed objective, population, eligibility, design, sample size, response rate, data collection, outcome parameters, and analysis (Methods > Screening; Quality Assessment).

## Measures and definitions

EQ-5D has five dimensions: Mobility (MO), Self-Care (SC), Usual Activities (UA), Pain/Discomfort (PD), and Anxiety/Depression (AD). The 3L uses no, some/moderate, and extreme/unable-to levels. The 5L adds slight and severe levels and changes the mobility top level from “confined to bed” to “unable to walk about”. The systems describe 243 and 3,125 health states, respectively. The EQ VAS was not the main review focus.

The review examined missing values, ceiling and floor effects, Shannon’s H′ and J′ informativity indices, inconsistencies, responsiveness, and test–retest reliability. Inconsistency was a paired 3L–5L response differing by more than one level after mapping 3L levels to 5L levels 1, 3, and 5. Responsiveness used effect sizes, standardized response mean, Wilcoxon tests, and probability of superiority. Reliability used ICC, Cohen’s Kappa, weighted Kappa, and percentage agreement (Methods > Distributional Properties; Informativity; Inconsistencies; Responsiveness; Test–Retest Reliability).

## Findings

- **Feasibility and distribution:** Missing values and floor effects were below 5% for both versions. All but three studies had a lower full-health ceiling (“11111”) with 5L. Pooled ceiling in patient samples was 0.23 for 3L and 0.18 for 5L; in population samples it was 0.53 for 3L and 0.43 for 5L (Abstract; Results > Missing Values and Distributional Properties; Fig. 3).
- **Informativity:** Fourteen studies reported Shannon indices. H′ was always higher for 5L. Mean H′ gain ratios ranged from 1.44 for AD to 1.65 for MO. J′ differences were small, with gain ratios from 1.02 for SC to 1.16 for MO. The 5L therefore captured more information without a relevant loss in evenness (Results > Informativity; Fig. 4).
- **Inconsistency:** Eighteen studies reported inconsistencies ranging from 0 to 10.6%, usually below 5%. UA had the highest mean inconsistency (4.1%), and MO the lowest (2.5%). Orthopaedic settings had the highest range (6–10.6%). Low inconsistency supports logical redistribution from 3L to 5L (Results > Inconsistencies; Fig. 5).
- **Responsiveness:** Evidence was mixed and based on only three studies. German rehabilitation data found 5L better on dimension-level probability of superiority. Chinese hepatitis B data found very small, mostly non-significant differences. Polish stroke data found 3L more responsive than 5L when the 5L index used a crosswalk. Crosswalk scoring may underdetect gains and complicate interpretation (Results > Responsiveness; Table 5).
- **Reliability:** Six papers studied test–retest reliability. Index ICC was 0.52–0.83 for 3L and 0.69–0.93 for 5L. Weighted Kappa ranges were 0.31–0.70 for 3L and 0.33–0.69 for 5L. Mean percentage agreement was higher for 3L (0.877) than 5L (0.773). There was no clear dimensional pattern of better reliability; index results tended to favour 5L (Results > Test–Retest Reliability; Table 6).

## Interpretation and limitations

The review supports use of both versions. It finds moderately better distribution and informativity for 5L, especially where discrimination among milder health states matters. Responsiveness and reliability require more evidence. Choice should also consider purpose, respondents, language, administration mode, and value-set availability. The 3L had more available value sets at the time of review; crosswalk was interim for 5L.

Heterogeneity in populations, languages, value sets, order, questionnaires, designs, and analyses limits generalisation. There were no agreed reporting standards for preference-based measures. Findings do not include youth instruments or experimental versions (Discussion; Limitations; Conclusion).
