## Aim and design

The study developed an EQ-5D-Y descriptive system with more than three severity levels and tested comprehensibility and feasibility in children and adolescents aged 8–15 years. It ran in Germany, Spain, Sweden, and the UK between May 2014 and June 2018. Phase 1 generated labels, rated their severity, and constructed draft 4L and 5L versions. Phase 2 used cognitive interviews to test both versions. A common protocol was used, with minor country-specific changes (Abstract; Introduction; Methods).

## Phase 1

The existing child HRQoL instruments, disease-specific instruments, dictionaries, and thesauruses were reviewed. Labels had to describe quantity or intensity of health problems. Frequency terms were excluded. Each country held two focus groups, one for ages 8–10 and one for ages 11–15. Children discussed personal illness experiences and pictures, ranked candidate labels, and identified labels they liked, disliked, or did not understand. Thematic content analysis informed country-specific label pools.

The initial pools contained 79 labels in Germany, 67 in Spain, 50 in Sweden, and 37 in the UK. A total of 255 individual sorting and response-scaling interviews were completed: Germany 64, Spain 72, Sweden 60, and UK 58. Younger children aged 8–10 used a five-face “smiling face” scale. Participants aged 11–15 rated labels on a 0–10 visual analogue scale. Labels were selected using median, mode, standard deviation, and participant comments. Labels needed to cover the full severity continuum and to be understood in everyday language (Methods > Phase 1; Results > Sorting and response scaling interviews; Tables 2–5).

## Phase 2 and harmonization

Draft EQ-5D-Y-4L and EQ-5D-Y-5L versions were tested in individual or group cognitive interviews. Germany had 33 participants, Spain 35, Sweden 32, and the UK 20. Germany, Spain, and Sweden included healthy children and children receiving treatment for a health condition. The UK used two school focus groups and did not recruit children with current illness. Participants completed both versions in varied order, discussed ease of use, paraphrased items, and explained response choices. The UK used written comprehension tasks. The teams translated and compared language versions and discussed discrepancies.

## Findings and product

Children found both versions feasible and easy to understand. The 5L was preferred by 68–88% of participants per country. The final product was the self-report **EQ-5D-Y-5L**, with five severity levels in each of the five dimensions: mobility, looking after myself, doing usual activities, having pain or discomfort, and feeling worried, sad or unhappy. It defines 3,125 possible health states, compared with 243 for EQ-5D-Y-3L. The 5L retained the lower and upper anchor wording where comprehension was good, while intermediate labels were selected from child language and country fieldwork (Abstract; Results > Phase 2; Discussion).

The final English labels included “not”, “a little bit”, “quite”, “really”, and “extremely” for the emotional dimension. The first three dimensions used wording such as “a little bit of a problem”, “some problems”, “a lot of problems”, and “extreme problems”. Country versions were comparable but not always literal translations. For example, German “große” was preferred to “viele” because participants found the latter unusual. The study did not treat language versions as exact lexical equivalents (Results > Harmonization; Table 7).

## Interpretation, uses, and limitations

The study concludes that children aged 8–15 can take part in label-generation, sorting, response scaling, and cognitive interviews. The EQ-5D-Y-5L is a short, comprehensible, and feasible self-report measure. The expanded response structure was intended to reduce ceiling effects and improve sensitivity. The authors state that psychometric properties, comparison with EQ-5D-Y-3L, proxy versions, and feasibility in health-state valuation still require testing. The paper does not report a value set.

Convenience samples were used in all countries and are not nationally representative. Recruitment of children with health conditions was difficult. The UK and Spain protocols differed slightly. The paper reports a self-report version only; proxy development and valuation studies were future work. Language-specific wording differences may affect cross-country comparisons (Discussion; Conclusion).
