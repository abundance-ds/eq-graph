# Psychometric properties of the EQ-5D-5L: a systematic review of the literature

## Aim and search

The review summarised published evidence on EQ-5D-5L distribution, missing values, reliability, validity, and responsiveness in adults. It focused on the five-item descriptive system because studies did not always identify the EQ VAS version. PUBMED (MEDLINE), PsycINFO, EMBASE, and the EuroQol website were searched using “EQ-5D”, “EQ-5D-5L”, “5L”, “EuroQol”, and “5 Level”. Publications up to January 2019 were screened in two stages. Eligible studies used the official EQ-5D-5L, were in English or German, involved humans aged 18 years or older, and evaluated measurement or psychometric properties (Abstract; Methods > Literature search).

Of 889 identified publications, 99 were included. They represented 32 countries, 32 general-population studies, and 58 patient-study settings. The most common patient groups were musculoskeletal/orthopedic problems and cancer (*n*=8 each), followed by lung/respiratory diseases (*n*=7). Duplicate datasets were extracted once. Two reviewers screened records and senior researchers resolved disagreement (Results; Methods > Data extraction).

## Methods and outcomes

The review extracted sample, design, EQ-5D-5L version, distribution, ceiling and floor, missing values, test–retest reliability, convergent and known-groups validity, discriminant validity, and responsiveness. Missing values ≤5% and floor ≤15% were treated as acceptable. Reliability used Cohen’s Kappa for dimensions and ICC for index values. Responsiveness used standardized effect size (SES), standardized response mean (SRM), anchor-based change, and minimally important difference where available.

Because studies were heterogeneous, pooled outcomes were limited to the proportion at full health (“11111”), mean index values, and correlations. Random-effects models with inverse-variance weighting were used. Correlations used Fisher’s *z* transformation and proportions used logit transformation. R with the “meta” package was used for analysis (Methods > Data extraction; Analysis).

## Distribution and reliability

Missing values were unproblematic in all 17 studies reporting them. Floor effects were unproblematic in 43 of 48 studies. Pooled full-health reporting was 23% for patient samples, ranging from 2% in musculoskeletal disease to 36% in cancer; general population studies reported 48% and healthy populations 41%. Self-care usually had a large ceiling, except in groups expected to have self-care limitations, such as stroke, spinal cord injury, or post-caesarean patients. Index means were lower in disease groups and lower socioeconomic or sociodemographic groups (Results > Distribution properties; Fig. 2–3).

Nine papers studied test–retest reliability. Eight found excellent index ICC and the remaining study found ICC=0.70. Item-level agreement was less stable. Five studies found fair agreement for selected dimensions. Agreement tended to be lowest for Pain/Discomfort and highest for Mobility. The review states that index scores are stable over time even when individual dimensions are less stable (Results > Reliability).

## Validity

Five papers addressed content validity. Research professionals found Self-Care too narrow and Usual Activities too broad in one qualitative study. Patients with spinal cord injury generally found EQ-5D-5L relevant but identified condition-specific gaps. A qualitative asthma study found limited relevance to asthma except for physical limitations while also valuing generic coverage.

Thirty-three papers studied convergent validity. Pooled index correlations were strongest with multi-attribute utility instruments (rho=0.756), physical or functional measures (rho=0.582), and pain/discomfort measures (rho=0.595). Correlations were poor with satisfaction (rho=0.335) and cognition/communication (rho=0.259). At dimension level, Pain/Discomfort correlated most strongly with pain measures (rho=0.636). Anxiety/Depression had moderate correlation with mental (rho=0.461), emotional, and social measures. All items correlated poorly with cognition/communication and vitality/fatigue/sleep measures. The index generally distinguished disease groups, disease severity, symptoms, age, and education, but not consistently spinal deformity, medication frequency, gender, health-service use, or marital status (Results > Validity; Fig. 4; Table 2).

## Responsiveness

Fifteen studies examined change over time. All used SES and/or SRM. Five stratified results by improved, stable, or deteriorated status using an anchor. Four reported minimally important differences. Index values usually had moderate effect sizes in groups expected to improve after treatment. The largest effects were observed shortly after childbirth. Compared with other instruments, EQ-5D-5L generally performed as well or better. Two dimension-level studies found greater sensitivity than 3L, but one Anxiety/Depression study found both versions limited (Results > Responsiveness; Table 4).

## Interpretation and limits

The authors conclude that EQ-5D-5L is a reliable and valid generic instrument across broad adult populations and settings. Missing data and floor effects are not major concerns. Large population ceilings remain because the instrument focuses on limitations and symptoms rather than positive health, energy, or well-being. Responsiveness is acceptable in the reviewed studies but needs more rigorous methods, common anchors, and minimally important difference definitions.

Limitations include exclusion of experimental versions and application studies that did not explicitly report psychometric properties. The review did not assess valuation methods, although value-set choice can affect index values and economic results. Most evidence came from Western Europe, OECD countries, and East Asia; more evidence is needed in other regions and in clinical, health-services, and surveillance settings (Discussion; Study limitations; Conclusion).
