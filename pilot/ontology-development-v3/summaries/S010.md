# The effect of duration and time preference on the gap between adult and child health state valuations in time trade-off

## Aim and participants

The study tested whether the adult–child difference in cTTO values changes when the gauge duration increases from 10 to 20 years, and whether perspective-specific time preferences explain the difference. Personal video interviews were conducted with 151 adults recruited to represent the English adult population by age, gender and education. Dynata provided participants, who received panel points equivalent to about £30. Interviews used Zoom or Google Meet in English during autumn 2021 COVID-19 restrictions; the session lasted up to one hour (Design and Participants).

The EQ-5D-Y-3L describes mobility, looking after oneself, doing usual activities, pain or discomfort and feeling worried, sad or unhappy, with three levels. Four states were valued: 22222, 32211, 32223 and 23232. Each respondent completed four randomized TTO blocks: adult versus 10-year-old child perspective crossed with 10-year versus 20-year duration. A wheelchair warm-up and two practice tasks preceded cTTO. EQ-VT iteration used initial bisection, then one-year or six-month titration. For 20-year TTO, duration was 20 years but lead time stayed 10 years, so the minimum uncorrected value was −0.5 rather than −1 (TTO Operationalization).

## Time preference and analysis

Time preference used the direct method. Respondents compared two 30-year profiles containing full health and state 32211 in reversed order, from both perspectives. Area under the curve (AUC) was bounded 0–1; 0.5 means no discounting, above 0.5 positive discounting and below 0.5 negative discounting. TTO utilities were corrected using the perspective-specific discount function and linear interpolation. Paired t-tests compared perspectives and durations. Mixed-effects regressions used respondent random effects and dummies for perspective, duration and health state plus demographics (Time Preference Measurement; Analysis).

## Findings

Children produced more no-trade responses than adults for 10-year TTO (18.8% vs 13.7%, p<0.02), but the 20-year difference was not significant (15.1% vs 14.1%). All-in trading was 2.0% adult and 1.8% child at 10 years, and 1.3% adult and 2.6% child at 20 years. There was no significant duration effect on all-in trading, zero trading, all-states-equal responses or dominance violations. Weak dominance violations were about 25.2–30.9%; strict violations 6.4–7.5% (Data Quality; Table 2).

Mean AUC was 0.502 for adults and 0.489 for children (paired p=0.14), and neither differed significantly from 0.50. Sixty respondents had negative discounting for both perspectives and 39 had positive discounting for both (Time Preference). Uncorrected child-perspective utilities were higher for all four states in both durations, but within-subject comparisons were not significant. In the mixed regression, the child-perspective coefficient was +0.028 and highly significant for uncorrected TTO, while it was −0.001 and not significant after time-preference correction. The 20-year coefficient was not significant (Table 5).

The conclusion is that increasing duration to 20 years did not reduce the adult–child gap. Correcting for perspective-specific time preference removed the regression gap, suggesting the difference is partly caused by time preference rather than health-state utility. Limitations include video rather than physical interviews, the unequal 10-year and 20-year lower bounds, no validity checks for the time-preference task and inability to separate “self versus other” from “adult versus child” effects. The direct method may be affected by sequence effects and can produce negative discounting (Discussion; Conclusion).
