# Adding a fatigue item to the EQ-5D-5L improves its psychometric performance in the general population

## Aim, design, and participants

The study tested whether an additional fatigue item adds unique information to the generic EQ-5D-5L. A web survey recruited Dutch general-population adults aged 18–75 through Survey Sampling International, 29 June–31 July 2017, with quotas intended to represent sex, age, and education. The sample was part of CENTER-TBI, with ethical approval from Leids Universitair Centrum. Of 3027 respondents, mean age was 44.7 years, 50.2% were male, and 52.0% reported at least one chronic health condition (31.2% one; 19.9% two or more). Analyses were stratified into 1453 respondents without a chronic condition (“healthy group”) and 1574 with one or more (“disease group”). [Abstract; Methods—Participants; Results—Respondents and Table 1]

## Instruments and analysis

EQ-5D-5L asks about health today in mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort, and anxiety/depression, each with five levels from “no problems” to “extreme problems”; the Dutch value set produced a utility score anchored at 0 (as bad as dead) and 1 (full health). EQ VAS is 0–100. The Rivermead Post-Concussion Symptoms Questionnaire (RPQ) has a fatigue item asking, “Do you (i.e., over the last 24 h) suffer from fatigue?” with 0 (not experienced at all), 1 (no more of a problem), 2 (a mild problem), 3 (a moderate problem), and 4 (a severe problem), recoded to 1–5. EQ-5D-5L + Fatigue combined the five EQ-5D-5L dimensions with this recoded item. [Methods—Measures]

The study compared ceiling and unique profiles; Shannon Index (H′) and Shannon Evenness (J′) for informativity; Spearman correlation of level-sum scores with EQ VAS for convergent validity; domain dependency using chance-adjusted extreme-versus-no-problem cross-tables; and univariate/multivariable regression of EQ VAS on dimension levels. Models included the original five dimensions, the six-item set, and all combinations of five of six items, for the full sample and both chronic-condition subgroups. Significance was *p*<0.05; analyses used IBM SPSS Statistics 25. Hypotheses predicted lower ceiling, greater H′, comparable J′, improved convergence and explanatory power, and larger gains in the chronic-condition group. [Methods—Data analyses and Hypotheses]

## Findings

- **Distribution:** Mean EQ-5D-5L utility was 0.83; 48% experienced some fatigue. Full-health profile 11111 occurred in 1116/3027 (37%) with EQ-5D-5L, versus 744 (25%) with + Fatigue. Observed profiles increased from 368/3125 (12%) to 636/15,625 (4% of the enlarged theoretical space). EQ VAS declined with fatigue: 84.3 at fatigue level 1, 75.9 at level 3, and 54.7 at level 5. [Abstract; Results—Distributional effects; Table 3]
- **Informativity:** H′ rose from 4.90 to 6.44 and J′ from 0.422 to 0.462. In the healthy group H′/J′ increased 2.58/0.222 to 4.05/0.291; in the disease group 6.41/0.552 to 7.90/0.567. [Results—Informativity]
- **Convergent validity:** Spearman correlation with EQ VAS was −0.606 for EQ-5D-5L and −0.621 for + Fatigue. In the disease group it was −0.331 versus −0.369; in the healthy group −0.614 versus −0.637. All were strong in the full/healthy samples and moderate in the disease subgroup under the stated criteria. [Results—Convergent validity]
- **Domain dependency:** Severe/extreme fatigue with no problems on another domain occurred in 5.5–20.1% (highest with self-care 20.1% and mobility 12.4%). The reverse pattern was only 0.2–0.7%. All five EQ-5D-5L domains were dominant over fatigue. [Results—Domain dependency; Table 4]
- **Explanatory power:** Multivariable EQ VAS R² increased from 0.387 to 0.416 when fatigue was added. Increase was 0.156 to 0.175 in the healthy group and 0.355 to 0.396 in the disease group. The authors report the largest practical gain among the extra dimensions, especially with chronic health conditions. [Results—Explanatory power; Table 5]

The authors conclude that + Fatigue improves discriminatory power, informativity, convergent validity, and explanatory power, with substantially larger effects in respondents with chronic health conditions. They identify open questions about whether the best construct is “energy”, “tiredness”, or “fatigue”, the wording and terminology, and tariff modelling. Limitations include use of a post-concussion RPQ item in a general population, a different recall period (EQ-5D-5L today versus fatigue over 24 h), web-panel non-response information not being available, and no condition-specific sample. [Discussion; Conclusions]
