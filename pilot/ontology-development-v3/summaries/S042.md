# Feasibility of Routine Quality-of-Life Measurement in Residential Aged Care: Results from a Pilot Study in Australia

## Aim, setting, and sample

The study examined feasibility, barriers, and facilitators of routine quality-of-life (QoL) measurement in residential aged care. It was conducted at two not-for-profit facilities in Melbourne, Victoria, Australia. All residents were eligible if consent was provided; consent could be from a resident or power of attorney (PoA). Data collection occurred February–July 2023 at Facility B and May–August 2023 at Facility A (Abstract; Methods, Sections “Sampling and Recruitment” and “Procedure”). Of 103 consenting participants, 90 self-reports, 101 staff proxy reports, and 49 family proxy reports were collected. Some residents had duplicate assessments because of an administration error.

## Instruments, versions, and administration

All participants were asked to complete the Australian English interviewer-administered EQ-5D-5L: mobility, self-care, usual activities, anxiety/depression, and pain/discomfort, each with no, slight, moderate, severe, and extreme problems/unable. EQ-VAS ranged 0 (“the worst health you can imagine”) to 100 (“the best health you can imagine”), with recall “TODAY”. Residents self-reported; aged care staff and relatives used proxy version 2 with the proxy-person perspective (how they think the resident would rate their own health), not proxy-proxy perspective. Facility A used REDCap/tablets; Facility B used paper. Staff received training and completed proxy before interviewing the resident (Methods, “Procedure” and “QoL Measures”).

A randomly assigned second instrument was also used: QOL-ACC, ASCOT, or experimental 25-item EQ-HWB. QOL-ACC has five older-person QoL dimensions; ASCOT has eight social-care domains and SCT-4 self-report; EQ-HWB extends beyond health to well-being. This paper focuses on the EQ-5D-5L feasibility while reporting instrument-specific missingness and staff experience.

## Analysis and feasibility indicators

Quantitative data from REDCap and paper forms were merged in STATA; descriptive statistics used percentages and means/SD. Feasibility covered missing data, resident engagement and understanding, staff/relative proxy difficulty, and qualitative barriers/facilitators. Six staff data collectors were interviewed after piloting. Interviews were audio-recorded, transcribed, imported into NVivo, and thematically analyzed with team review (Methods, “Qualitative Interviews…” and “Analysis”).

## Results

Most residents (94%; 82/87 evaluated) completed EQ-5D-5L; non-completion was attributed to difficulty comprehending questions (5%) or being unwell (1%). Staff rated residents very engaged (34%) or somewhat engaged (41%); 97% of assessments were private. EQ-5D-5L had few missing values: 1% all items missing for staff, 14% certain items missing for family, and 8% all items plus 2% certain items missing for residents (Results, Tables 4–5). Staff had to repeat/explain EQ-5D-5L questions in 64% of evaluations, yet 43% rated question understanding good and 45% response-option understanding good.

Proxy burden differed. Forty-one percent of staff found proxy reporting somewhat difficult, compared with 8% of relatives; 73% of staff thought residents would rate their own QoL better than staff did, while 54% of relatives expected agreement. Most staff (84%) and relatives (90%) did not think another proxy was needed. Staff reported time fit within schedule in 88% of assessments; 12% reported conflict (Results, Table 3).

Qualitative themes were perceived benefits, conflicting priorities, resident-related challenges, measurement-driven issues, choice of proxy, strategies, and biases (Results, “Qualitative Interview Findings”). Benefits included person-centred care and understanding residents. Barriers included time pressure, competing duties, dementia/cognitive impairment, hearing or language problems, residents being unavailable/unwell, negatively worded questions, and staff uncertainty about feelings. Staff suggested dedicated staff/time, quiet rooms, morning assessments, repeated approaches, care-plan review, and using a proxy who knows the resident. They reported possible interviewer bias, social desirability, and shortcuts when workload was high.

## Implications and limitations

Routine QoL collection is feasible but needs clear protocols, training, resources, and integration into care and reporting workflows. The authors suggest resident-level use, defined action points, self-report support, bias safeguards, and a designated “QoL Champion”. Limitations include convenience selection of two facilities from one not-for-profit provider, incentives, consent-based exclusion of residents with severe impairment, language barriers, limited interpreter funds, staff-led social desirability bias, inability to assess repeated timepoints, and incomplete administration-time recording (Discussion, “Implications” and “Limitations”; Conclusions).
