## Aim, setting, and participants

The study sought US stakeholder input for a country-specific EQ-5D-Y-3L valuation study, supplementing the International Valuation Protocol with US context (Abstract; Introduction). Fourteen stakeholders were purposively identified through investigator networks across pediatric clinical/research, HTA bodies, third-party payers, caregivers/educators, academic HEOR, consulting, pharmaceutical, and medical-device backgrounds. All 14 consented; two Younger Populations Working Group members and three investigators also attended (Methods; Results, Table 2). A 2-hour online roundtable took place on 7 April 2022. Thirteen of 14 returned a pre-meeting survey; 10/13 had HRQoL-measure experience, 7/13 used health utilities or estimated QALYs, and 8/13 were very or extremely familiar with HTA (Results).

## Instrument and valuation context

EQ-5D-Y-3L is a generic preference-based child/adolescent HRQoL measure with five child-friendly dimensions: mobility; looking after myself; doing usual activities; having pain or discomfort; and feeling worried, sad, or unhappy. Each has three levels from no problems to a lot of problems, producing 243 states. It is intended for self-completion at ages 8–15 and proxy completion at ages 4–7 (Introduction). The international protocol recommends adult general-population respondents, a hypothetical 10-year-old framing, DCE and cTTO tasks, and anchoring on 0 (dead) and 1 (full health). The discussion examined whether to use children/adolescents, adults, or both; adult-own versus 10-year-old perspective; and DCE versus cTTO (Introduction; Methods, Table 1).

## Procedure and analysis

Before the meeting, participants received written instrument and objective information, videos on EQ-5D descriptive systems, and a five-item survey. The first 30 minutes gave background on HRQoL, the instrument, valuation methodology, and youth-valuation challenges. The remaining 90 minutes used eight semi-structured prompts. The session was recorded and transcribed. Investigators independently reviewed the transcript, chat comments, and follow-up emails, categorized responses, then met one week later to agree on distilled themes. This was qualitative thematic distillation, not a formal consensus or quantitative preference study (Methods).

## Findings by theme

- **Willingness to pay:** Several stakeholders supported paying more per QALY for children because of potential future societal contribution, avoiding undervaluation, or improving access to innovation. Others warned of double counting because QALYs already include longer expected life, insufficient evidence on long-term benefits, and a precedent for paying more for selected subgroups (Results, “Willingness-to-Pay…”).
- **Sources and perspective:** Most supported direct inclusion of children or adolescents. Older adults may not identify with a 10-year-old, and adult responses may vary with parenthood, number/age/health of children, caring experience, or religion. Adolescents were viewed as closer to the target age, capable of valid responses, and aligned with intended youth use. HTA-oriented participants favoured adults because adults vote/pay taxes and make resource decisions; they accepted own or 10-year-old framing with reservations (Results, “Sources of Preferences…”).
- **Sampling and value-set selection:** Most favoured adolescent preferences alone or alongside adults, possibly weighted to US population representation. Adult surveys should collect parenthood and relevant child-illness experience. Stakeholders generally opposed multiple US value sets because users could be confused or select the set yielding favourable QALYs (Results, “Sampling Approach…”).
- **International protocol and future study:** Stakeholders did not reach consensus. The discussion was designed to explore feasibility, validity, and priorities, not to reach agreement. The investigators incorporated stakeholder input by including adolescents for DCEs and adding adult background questions in the subsequent US study. The paper notes adolescent DCEs alone give latent values and do not anchor at dead/full health (Discussion).

## Limitations and implications

The sample was small (*n* = 14), purposive, network-based, and not geographically representative. Some expertise was overrepresented; no formal Delphi or structured content analysis was used. Prompts focused on QALY estimation and cost-effectiveness, not all possible EQ-5D-Y-3L uses. Participants had varied baseline knowledge despite preparatory material. The authors recommend stakeholder engagement as a country-specific complement to, not a replacement for, international protocol guidance, and suggest further consultation during and after valuation studies (Discussion; Conclusions).
