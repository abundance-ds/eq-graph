## Aim and design

The study explored whether the multi-item EQ-5D-5L adds discriminatory value in health-inequality analyses compared with the single-item EQ VAS. It examined education-related socioeconomic status (SES), and the roles of chronic health conditions and work status, in Italy, the Netherlands, and the United Kingdom (UK). The survey ran 29 June–31 July 2017 as part of CENTER-TBI (EC grant 602150); ethical approval was from Leids Universitair Centrum—Commissie Medische Ethiek (P14.222/NV/nv) (Abstract; Materials and Methods, “Participants”).

## Population and measures

Survey Sampling International recruited adults aged 18–75 years from existing internet panels. Samples were selected to represent each country by age, gender, and educational level. Only complete questionnaires were analysed. There were 10,172 respondents: Italy 3,026, Netherlands 3,027, and UK 4,119. Mean age was 44.5 years (SD 15.3), 49% had middle education, 26% low and 25% high education, 52% were employed, and about half reported at least one chronic condition; depression or anxiety disorder was most frequent (19%) (Results, “Respondents”; Table 1).

Education was coded with ISCED-97 as low (ISCED 0–2), mid (3–4), or high (5–6), and used as the SES proxy. Work status categories were employed, unemployed, looking after others, student, retired, and unable to work. Chronic conditions were self-reported and recoded as presence/absence and number. The EQ-5D-5L descriptive system has mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort, and anxiety/depression, each with no, slight, moderate, severe, and extreme problems. Its equal-weight level sum score (LSS) ranges 5–25, with 25 worst health. The authors transformed it to a 0–100 tLSS for comparison with EQ VAS: `100 − [(LSS − 5) × 5]`. They used LSS/tLSS rather than utility scores because no EQ-5D-5L value set was available for Italy; applying the Dutch or UK set would confound profile differences with country preference differences. EQ VAS ranges 0 (worst imaginable health) to 100 (best imaginable health) (Materials and Methods, “Measures” and “Statistical Analysis”; Discussion).

## Analysis and findings

Descriptive, ANOVA, Kruskal–Wallis, univariate regression, and country-specific multivariable stepwise regression with backward elimination were used. The multivariable models entered sex and age first, then education, work status, income, and number of chronic conditions; significance was p < 0.05 (Materials and Methods, “Statistical Analysis”).

Italy had the best mean tLSS (90.7, SD 11.8) and EQ VAS (77.6, SD 17.4); the UK had the worst (tLSS 84.8, SD 18.9; EQ VAS 71.4, SD 21.6) (Results, “Health Outcomes According to Country,” Figure 1). In the UK and Netherlands, low education was associated with worse tLSS and EQ VAS, while high education was better. Italy showed a different pattern: tLSS was best for middle education and no clear EQ VAS education gradient. Education-group differences were generally slightly larger for tLSS than EQ VAS (Table 2). Inability to work and increasing numbers of chronic conditions were strong, consistent predictors. Model R² was 31.6–54.3% for tLSS versus 17.9–30.6% for EQ VAS, highest in the UK (Results, “Factors Associated…”).

The authors conclude that both measures show education-dependent health inequalities, but tLSS discriminates slightly better. Chronic conditions and inability to work largely cancel education effects in regression, apart from a small residual UK education effect. Limitations include online-panel selection, cross-sectional inference, self-report, extra burden of a multi-item score, and privacy restrictions on data access (Discussion; Conclusion).
