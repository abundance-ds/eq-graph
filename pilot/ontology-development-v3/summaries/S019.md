## Aim and design

The study asks whether discrete choice experiments with duration (DCEd) produce results similar to cTTO/EQ-VT for EQ-5D-5L (Abstract; Introduction). Both studies were conducted in Trinidad and Tobago but in different general-population samples. The EQ-VT sample had 1,079 face-to-face respondents, each completing ten cTTO and 12 duration-free DCE tasks. The DCEd sample had 1,581 initial online/public-place respondents; 611 were excluded by quality rules, leaving 970, each completing 18 split-triplet tasks.

## Preference tasks and sampling

EQ-VT cTTO compared 10 years in an EQ-5D-5L state with variable full-health years; EQ-VT DCE compared two states with no duration. The standard EQ-VT cTTO design used 86 states in ten blocks; DCE used 20 blocks of 12 pairs with overlap on two dimensions. Interviewers had four days of training and EQ-VT QC.

DCEd split triplets first compared two EQ-5D-5L states A and B with equal duration, then compared B with full-health state C for fewer years; three triplets used immediate death for C. Durations were 1–15 years or six months, with A/B equal and C shorter. Data were collected online and by recruiters in public places; respondents needed a sufficiently large screen. Respondents averaging under 12.5 seconds per split-triplet were excluded. Public-place IP groups were removed when speeding/flatlining indicated invalid data. The design began near-orthogonal, then was updated three times using 200-person batches and TPC-QD software (Methods).

## Modelling and comparison

EQ-VT data were modelled with heteroskedastic Tobit cTTO, mixed-logit DCE, and hybrid heteroskedastic Tobit models. DCEd used mixed-logit models with linear and nonlinear time preferences; a separate immediate-death parameter was estimated. All 3,125 state values were compared with scatterplots and Bland–Altman plots. Mean observed cTTO values for the 86 design states were also compared. Sensitivity analyses used 10- and 15-second cutoffs.

## Findings

The nonlinear DCEd model ranked level-5 dimensions in the same order as EQ-VT: pain/discomfort largest, then mobility, anxiety/depression, self-care, and usual activities. Its 55555 value was −0.543; the linear model produced −1.214. The EQ-VT models gave 55555 values −0.611 (heteroskedastic Tobit), −0.686 (rescaled mixed-logit DCE), and −0.563 (hybrid). Nonlinear DCEd values correlated with EQ-VT cTTO at 0.954, rescaled DCE at 0.973, and hybrid models at 0.973. Mean differences between DCEd and cTTO, rescaled DCE, and hybrid estimates were 0.041, 0.011, and 0.036; MADs were 0.083, 0.051, and 0.060 across all states (Results).

For the 86 observed states, MAE was 0.081 for DCEd versus 0.042 cTTO, 0.053 rescaled DCE, and 0.044 hybrid. A population mean discount rate of 23.5% (95% CI 21.7–25.3) was estimated in the nonlinear DCEd model. The linear model preserved dimension order but produced a very different scale, showing that correcting nonlinear time preferences is essential.

Quality exclusions were material: 206 online respondents were speeders, 263/405 public-place respondents were speeders, and 302 public-place respondents flatlined on B/C. Public-place collection was discontinued. The final DCEd sample had more education and different ethnicity than EQ-VT, and the samples were collected about six months apart (Results; Discussion).

## Interpretation, limitations, and product

The authors conclude that nonlinear DCEd can be a cheaper, online alternative to EQ-VT with similar values. DCEd is sensitive to flatlining and cannot easily distinguish strong longevity preference from inattention; a garbage-class model is not available. Samples were different, in-person data were discarded, and only one country was studied. The result does not support linear DCEd.
