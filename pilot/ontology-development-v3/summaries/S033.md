# Content Validity of the EQ-HWB in Caregivers of Children with Health Conditions

## Aim and sample

The study examined relevance, understanding and comprehensiveness of the experimental 25-item EQ-HWB and 9-item EQ-HWB-S in caregivers of children with health conditions, and tested possible wording, response-direction and item-order modifications. Twenty-one caregivers were drawn from the Australian QUOKKA Pediatric Multi-Instrument Comparison Study hospital sample. The children had autism spectrum disorder (ASD), prematurity, rare genetic conditions and combinations; ages ranged 1–18 years (mean 9.7). Caregivers’ mean age was 44.9 (range 31–61), 20 were women and one was a man; 79% reported 81–100% of the child’s care. [Source: Abstract; Method—Sample; Results—Table 1]

## Procedure and analysis

Participants completed one-on-one online Zoom interviews of 30–40 minutes, with a $AU45 honorarium. They thought aloud while answering all 25 Australian-English experimental EQ-HWB items, followed by interviewer probing. They were asked whether the EQ-HWB-S included missing or redundant constructs and whether CarerQol items should be added: fulfilment from care tasks, relational problems and financial problems. The first seven interviews omitted the CarerQol questions because of researcher error. Final questions tested response-option, control-item, positive/negative framing and item-order changes. Interviews were transcribed, checked and anonymised. [Source: Method—Procedure]

Content analysis in NVivo used COSMIN concepts of relevance, understanding and comprehensiveness, with deductive and inductive coding. Both authors independently coded seven transcripts; the first author coded the remaining 14, with differences resolved by consensus. Complete responses required that the question was asked, received more than yes/no, and did not require further probing. [Source: Method—Analysis]

## Content-validity findings

Most items were relevant and understood. Highest relevance concerned sleep, exhaustion, lack of support, memory/cognition, anxiety, frustration, depression, lack of control and having nothing to look forward to, often in connection with caregiving stress and interrupted sleep. Seeing and hearing were less relevant and sometimes confused participants, including uncertainty about aids. Mobility could also be misunderstood when a participant equated “no problems” with not using an aid. Feeling unsafe was relevant to only a few, but most considered it important; pain/discomfort was present but not a major contributor for this sample. Loneliness and anxiety were understood as important caregiver issues. [Source: Abstract; Results—Relevance and Understanding; Discussion]

Participants strongly supported the nine EQ-HWB-S items and would drop one only if forced. One participant wanted support added. CarerQol fulfilment was important to 7 of 12 complete respondents but ambiguous to five. The caregiver–care receiver relationship item was important to six of 10, but four found “demanding” loaded or the question problematic for parents. Financial strain was important to nine of 12; eight described restricted employment or inability to work. Some participants had National Disability Insurance Scheme (NDIS) funding that reduced relevance. [Source: Results—Comprehensiveness; CarerQol items]

## Tested modifications

Changing “only occasionally” to “a little of the time” produced mixed views: among seven responses, three preferred the change, two the original, and two had no preference. Changing “no control over your day-to-day life” to “lacked control over your day-to-day life” was supported by six of 10; one preferred the original. Participants generally preferred the positive framing of EQ-HWB 19 (“feeling accepted”) and EQ-HWB 20 (“feel good about yourself”); negative versions felt emotionally different and might measure different constructs. For EQ-HWB 21, “could do things as wanted” versus difficulty with enjoyable activities, participants were more positive about the change but noted that the concepts differ. Many valued positive items because they balanced difficult questions and could end the questionnaire on a positive note. [Source: Results—Potential Modifications]

Four of seven supported putting activities before mobility in EQ-HWB-S; two were neutral. Seeing/hearing at the start could act as an ice-breaker, but some preferred moving them later. The authors endorse the three EQ-HWB-S changes: “a little of the time”, “lack of control” wording, and activities before mobility. They suggest further work on seeing/hearing relevance and placement, while retaining positive items and considering placing them at the end. [Source: Results; Discussion; Conclusions]

## Limitations and implications

The sample had only one father, and the long instrument limited depth of probing. CarerQol evidence was prompted rather than spontaneously raised. The study supports high content validity for caregivers, but the EQ-HWB remained experimental at publication. Positive/negative item direction may affect preference-based scoring and response accuracy; positive items may also protect participant wellbeing. Funding was reported as open-access funding enabled and organised by CAUL and its Member Institutions. Conflicts included authors’ EuroQol and EQ-HWB working-group or special-interest-group roles and a PharmacoEconomics editorial-board role; no relevant competing interests were declared.
