## Aim, setting, and participants

The study explored staff, resident, and family views on the benefits, challenges, feasibility, validity, and best practice of routine QoL measurement in residential aged care homes (Abstract; Aim and Methods). Two 90-minute staff/family workshops took place at two not-for-profit Melbourne, Australia homes in October and December 2022. Semi-structured interviews were conducted across three homes. The sample included 24 residents, 29 proxies (9 family members and 20 staff), and workshop participants comprising 9 staff and 4 family members (Results, Table 1). Residents were nominated based on cognitive functioning and English ability; proxies could participate regardless of resident participation.

## Design and analysis

Workshop topics were purpose/benefits, resident selection, data collectors, timing, and other considerations, guided by the ISOQOL User Guide for Implementing Patient-Reported Outcomes Assessment in Clinical Practice. Interviews were about 30 minutes for residents and 60 minutes for proxies. All recordings were transcribed verbatim. A qualitative interpretive framework combined deductive parent codes (“benefits”, “barriers”, “validity of data”, “collection and reporting process”) with inductive in-vivo child codes. Two coders used NVivo 1.7.2, met during coding, consolidated themes, and selected quotations (Methods, “Workshop Recruitment…”, “Interview Recruitment…”, and “Analysis”).

The study was part of a broader project examining four QoL measures, but this report uses steps 1–3 before QOL-ACC was added to Australia’s mandatory program. The source does not report an EQ-5D-specific administered instrument for this qualitative phase; it discusses routine QoL measurement and related instruments generally. The proxy interviews’ first part examined the appropriateness of the EQ-HWB for residents, while resident interviews explored four QoL measures; the routine-measurement analysis reported here is not an EQ-HWB or EQ-5D validation study (Methods, “Interview Recruitment and Procedure”; Strengths and Limitations).

## Four themes and findings

1. **Benefits of routine QoL measurement.** Participants said formal measurement could standardize and report QoL to management or government, identify depression or social isolation, prompt practice improvement, provide information and reassurance to families, and support person-centred care. Residents and family members saw potential value for regulation and public comparison of facilities (Results, “Benefits of Routine QoL Measurement”).
2. **Challenges to implementation.** Providers may resist data that could reflect poorly on business performance; staff may view surveys as administrative burden. Limited time, under-resourcing, competing care, resident survey fatigue, and doubts that measurement will change care were repeatedly raised. Some participants thought government mandate was needed (Results, “Challenges to Implementation”).
3. **Best practice.** Most staff recommended integrating QoL into existing care-plan evaluations, alongside clinical, biography, family, and preference information. Registered Nurses were viewed as best placed to organize, oversee, contextualize, and act on results. Administration should be flexible: private, relaxed, and adapted to resident capacity, rapport, timing, and staff schedule. Six-monthly assessment was the most common frequency suggestion, although responses varied (Results, “Best Practice for Collecting Surveys”).
4. **Validity concerns.** Participants warned of “box ticking”, staff answering from professional opinion, provider incentives to manipulate data, resident reluctance from fear of repercussions, and proxy inaccuracy. Family proxies may know the resident well but visit infrequently; staff may know daily function but be conflicted. Participants differed on whether staff or family are the best proxy, particularly for non-verbal or cognitively impaired residents (Results, “Validity Concerns”).

## Interpretation and limitations

The study concludes that routine QoL can support care planning and quality improvement only if integrated into established workflows with adequate time, resources, skilled administration, stakeholder commitment, monitoring, and feedback. External administration may reduce acquiescence bias, while RN-led administration may better connect results to action. Limitations include pre-implementation timing, no discussion of instrument-specific feasibility, workshop peer influence, and resident fatigue after a QoL assessment (Discussion; Strengths and Limitations). The source identifies the Australian National Aged Care Mandatory Quality Indicator Program and QOL-ACC as policy context, but does not report a quantitative value-set or utility analysis.
