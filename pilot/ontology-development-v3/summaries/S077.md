## Aim and sample

The study provided German EQ-5D-5L reference values for the general population aged 65 years and older, including dimension profiles, EQ VAS, and tariff-based index values. It used a subset of the German EQ-5D-5L valuation study. The source valuation study recruited at least 1,000 general-population respondents by quota sampling for age, gender, education, and employment. Computer-assisted personal interviews took place in Berlin, Leipzig, Hamburg, Bielefeld, Munich, Frankfurt, and surrounding areas from December 2014 to March 2015. The elderly subset had no additional restrictions (Abstract; Methods—Data).

The reference subset included 290 respondents aged 65–93 years, mean age 73.1 (SD 5.7), 54.5% women. Age groups were 65–69 (n=86), 70–74 (78), 75–79 (88), and 80+ (38). The authors state that the sample was representative for age, sex, marital status, education, and residence area, but note that valuation participants may be healthier and cognitively more able than institutionalised or frail older adults (Results—Sample characteristics; Discussion).

## Instrument and methods

EQ-5D-5L uses mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain or discomfort, and anxiety or depression, each with levels 1 “no problems” through 5 “unable to/extreme problems,” yielding 3,125 states. EQ VAS is a 0–100 self-rating from “the worst health you can imagine” to “the best health you can imagine.” Index values used the recommended German tariff by Ludwig et al., ranging from 1 to −0.661. Analyses reported level distributions, any-problem prevalence, common profiles, means, SDs, and 95% CIs by age and gender. Non-normal index and VAS values were compared with Kruskal–Wallis, Mann–Whitney U, and chi-square tests (Methods—Variables and Analysis).

## Findings

The mean index was 0.84 (SD 0.22; 95% CI 0.814–0.864) and mean EQ VAS 73.2 (SD 18.5; 95% CI 71.1–75.4). Twenty-one point four percent reported 11111. Ninety-three unique health states occurred; the three most common were 11111 (21.4%), 11121 (14.5%), and 21121 (6.2%). No respondent reported 55555. Pain/discomfort was most frequently problematic (68.3%), followed by mobility (52.1%), usual activities (35.2%), anxiety/depression (27.6%), and self-care (15.5%). Extreme/unable-to responses were below 2% in every dimension (Results—EQ-5D-5L dimensions; Table 2).

Health problems increased almost monotonically with age. Pain/discomfort began at a high prevalence and remained similar after age 70. Women generally reported more mobility, usual-activity, pain/discomfort, and anxiety/depression problems. Anxiety/depression problems were 36.7% in women and 16.7% in men. Mean index values fell from 0.92 at 65–69 to 0.85 at 70–74, 0.82 at 75–79, and 0.68 at 80+. Mean VAS fell from 82.6 (SD 12.6) to 74.3, 68.0, and 61.9 across the same groups (Results—Tables 2, 5–6; Figure 1).

Men had higher mean index than women (0.87 vs 0.82; *p* = 0.027). The oldest group showed a pronounced female decline: women aged 80+ had mean index 0.63 (SD 0.39) versus 0.84 (0.13) for men. Overall VAS was 74.3 for men and 72.3 for women; this difference was not statistically significant (*p* = 0.27). Fifty-four percent had index values ≥0.9, and only three respondents had negative utilities (Results—Index and EQ VAS norms; Tables 5–6).

## Interpretation and limits

The authors argue that broad conventional age bands mask a marked decline after age 80. These reference values can support comparisons with sampled HRQoL, change over time, and burden-of-disease or economic models. The profile suggests age-related problems are concentrated in pain/discomfort and mobility, with fewer self-care problems. Limitations include secondary use of a valuation sample, small total sample, only 38 respondents aged 80+, likely underrepresentation of residential care, no detailed comorbidity data, and possible selection toward healthier participants. Confidence intervals overlap between adjacent age groups, so oldest-old estimates require caution (Discussion; Conclusion).
