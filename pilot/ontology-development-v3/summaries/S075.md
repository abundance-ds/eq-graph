# Feasibility properties of the EQ-5D-3L and 5L in the general population: evidence from the GP Patient Survey on the impact of age

## Aim and data

The study examined missing responses, completion rates, response distributions, and age effects for EQ-5D-3L and EQ-5D-5L in the English general population. It pooled five waves of the General Practitioner Patient Survey (GPPS), a National Health Service England cross-sectional survey. Adults were eligible if aged 18 or older, held a valid NHS registration number, and had been continuously registered with a GP for at least six months. GPPS used proportionately stratified samples from practice registration data; questionnaires were mainly paper-based, with 4–6% online responses and negligible telephone responses. EQ VAS was not included (Abstract; Methods—Data and Variables).

The pooled sample contained 4,358,700 observations: 1,476,395 EQ-5D-3L and 2,882,305 EQ-5D-5L. Data came from 2012 (including the change from 3L to 5L) and 2016–2017. About one third were aged 65 or older and about 60% reported at least one long-term condition; high blood pressure (23%) and arthritis/joint problems (16%) were common (Results—Sample description; Table 1).

## Instruments and analysis

Both versions use mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain or discomfort, and anxiety or depression. EQ-5D-3L has no, some/moderate, and extreme/unable-to levels; EQ-5D-5L has no, slight, moderate, severe, and extreme/unable-to levels. A complete five-dimension profile can be linked to a value set to calculate an index. The study assessed item nonresponse and completion, defined as the proportion with all five descriptive items complete. EQ VAS was absent from the GPPS (Methods—Variables).

Descriptive analyses examined level distributions overall and by age groups 18–24, 25–34, 35–44, 45–54, 55–64, 65–74, 75–84, and 85+. Chi-square tests assessed age associations, and Cramer’s V described effect size. Logistic regression modelled an incomplete descriptive system (at least one unanswered item) using age group, gender, and any long-term condition; predicted probabilities used STATA’s `margins` command with covariates at sample means. STATA 16 was used (Methods—Analysis).

## Findings

Problems were least frequent among young respondents and increased with age. The 5L showed more responses spread across severity levels and a lower ceiling than the 3L. Self-care had the greatest ceiling effect; pain or discomfort was informative. Floor effects were not observed in this general population sample. Anxiety or depression problems were relatively stable across age, while pain/discomfort increased with age (Results—Comparison of response distribution; Tables 2–3).

Dimension-level missingness was low but increased with age, especially after 64 years. For 5L, anxiety/depression missingness rose from 1.98% at age 18–24 to 7.56% at 75–84 and 7.56% in the 85+ category; for 3L it rose from 2.66% to 8.36% and 9.14%, respectively. Other dimensions were generally below 5%. Overall completion was 93.11% for 5L and 91.57% for 3L. Completion fell from 96.01% (5L) and 94.88% (3L) at age 18–24 to 85.92% and 83.14% at 85+ (Table 4). Among respondents with missing items, one missing item accounted for about 60–70% of patterns; complete nonresponse was more common in young adults, not older adults.

Adjusted logistic predictions showed incomplete response probabilities of about 4.3% for 5L at age 18–24 and 5.3% for 3L at age 25–34. Probabilities accelerated beyond age 64 and reached 11.5% (5L) and 13.9% (3L) at age 85+ (Figure 1). At every age, 5L had a lower incomplete-response probability, with the gap reaching 2.4 percentage points at 85+.

## Interpretation and limits

Both versions had good feasibility, but 5L compared more favourably in missing values, completion, and predicted incomplete response, especially in older adults. The authors suggest that improved sensitivity of 5L may help respondents map their health to the available levels. They note possible systematic age-related bias in utility estimates and suggest assisted or interviewer-based administration for limited samples, balanced against interviewer effects, socially desirable responses, and disclosure bias (Discussion; Conclusion).

The study could not compare both versions in the same respondent, lacked EQ VAS, could not control for administration mode or assistance, and could not assess why respondents left items blank. GPPS data may underestimate feasibility problems if respondents received help. Future work should examine paper versus online modes, EQ VAS feasibility, and condition-specific effects.
