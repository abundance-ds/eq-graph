# Developing a quality of life framework from the perspective of laypeople: a qualitative comparison with the EQ-HWB framework

## Aim and sample

The study developed a Chinese quality-of-life (QoL) conceptual framework from lay perspectives and compared it with the EQ-HWB conceptual framework. It addressed the comprehensiveness aspect of content validity. Relevance and comprehensibility were reserved for a separate article. The EQ-HWB is intended for health, public health, and social care, including patients, generic populations, and informal carers (Abstract; Introduction).

Quota sampling recruited 30 Chinese respondents from Guangzhou and Harbin. There were 10 healthy people, 10 patients, and 10 informal caregivers. Quotas sought variation in age, gender, education, medical condition, and rural/urban registered residence (Hukou). No more than 12 respondents could have university education and at least 15 had to be over 45 years. Interviews took place between March and June 2023. Respondents completed face-to-face, semi-structured interviews in quiet public settings and could complete the EQ-HWB independently. Data saturation was reached in the last three interviews according to the Abstract, although the Limitations section says saturation was assessed subjectively (Methods > Participant selection; Results > Participants).

## Data collection and analysis

The interview guide was translated into Chinese and piloted twice with six respondents. “Poor well-being” was difficult to understand in Chinese, so the formal interviews used “QoL”. Respondents described QoL, rated it from 1–10, described poor QoL, and gave examples. The interviewer summarised each interview and checked interpretation with the respondent.

Interviews were transcribed verbatim in simplified Chinese. Two coders used Gale’s thematic framework method and both inductive and deductive coding. Ninety-six EQ-HWB candidate items formed the initial codebook. Codes were excluded if they concerned morality, death, external events, income or economic factors, changes over time, personality traits that could not distinguish good and poor QoL, organ or cellular function, or future outcomes. Codes with similar meanings or opposite phrasing were combined into sub-themes and themes. NVivo 14 supported analysis. The resulting framework was compared with the EQ-HWB framework at theme and sub-theme levels (Methods > Analysis).

## Chinese QoL framework

From 221 initial codes, 187 were retained. The final framework had eight themes: **feeling and emotion**, **cognition**, **self-identity**, **coping**, **physical sensation**, **relationship**, **activity**, and **mindset**. The EQ-HWB has seven common themes; the Chinese framework added **mindset**. Alignment was 68% (18/57) at sub-theme level and 88% (7/8) at theme level (Abstract; Results > Comparison with the EQ-HWB).

Key additional or culturally specific sub-themes included regret, boredom, stress, emotional abilities, cognitive impairment, self-assessment, adaptation, weakness, appetite, appearance, establishing relationships, judgment, betrayal, physical function, sleep, and diet. The **mindset** theme included “life attitude”, “adjust mindset”, and “positive/negative energy”. Respondents used mindset for ways of viewing and approaching difficulties, internal harmony, and effects of emotions or opinions on others. “Positive/negative energy” and “Mindset” were retained as translations because no direct English equivalents carried the same Chinese meaning (Results > Framework themes).

The Chinese framework and EQ-HWB both covered physical, mental, and social aspects. The Chinese respondents did not mention the EQ-HWB “hearing” sub-theme. They placed sleep under activity because they described ability to fall asleep or stay asleep, while EQ-HWB placed sleep under physical sensation. They gave “cope with” a meaning linked to distress and problems rather than daily activity. These differences may affect translation and factor structure in China (Results > Comparison; Discussion).

## Interpretation, use, and limitations

The authors conclude that the EQ-HWB conceptual framework is well represented in the Chinese framework. Missing sub-themes and the additional mindset theme were considered unlikely to damage content validity. The findings support further assessment of EQ-HWB in China and careful translation of culturally specific phrases. The study did not test psychometric performance or preference weights.

The Harbin respondents were young, relatively healthy, and highly educated. Patients were mainly community patients with chronic disease; severe hospital patients were not included. Some terms were difficult to translate. Saturation was subjective. External factors such as economic situation and living conditions were excluded by design, so their absence from the final framework is not evidence that respondents considered them irrelevant (Limitation; Conclusion).
