# Comparing measurement properties of EQ-5D-Y-3L and EQ-5D-Y-5L in paediatric patients

## Aim and sample

The study compared feasibility, ceiling/distribution, redistribution, discriminatory power, convergent validity, test–retest reliability, and responsiveness of EQ-5D-Y-3L and EQ-5D-Y-5L in paediatric patients. Children aged 8–16 years were recruited from five hospitals in Jakarta and Bandung, Indonesia, with good Bahasa Indonesia. Groups were major beta-thalassemia, severe-to-moderate haemophilia, acute lymphoblastic leukaemia (AcLL), and acute illness requiring treatment. The broad severity range was selected to include severe states. The final baseline sample was 286 (38% female; mean age 11.2, SD 2.4): acute illness 43.4%, major beta-thalassemia 23.7%, haemophilia 18.8%, AcLL 14%. Almost half (48.3%) needed assistance, mainly acutely ill children (Results, “Participants”; Table 1).

## Instruments and design

EQ-5D-Y has walking about, looking after myself, doing usual activities, having pain or discomfort, and feeling worried, sad, or unhappy. EQ-5D-Y-3L levels are no problems, some problems, and a lot of problems. EQ-5D-Y-5L levels are no problems, a little bit of problems, some problems, a lot of problems, and cannot/extreme problems; higher scores are worse. Both include VAS 0–100. The in-progress UK English 5L was translated to Bahasa Indonesia with the EuroQol translation protocol and VMC collaboration. PedsQL Generic Core, PedsQL Cancer Module, TranQol, and Haemo-Qol provided convergent comparisons (Methods, “Instruments”).

Children completed paper questionnaires at baseline, retest while health was assumed unchanged, and follow-up after treatment. They were encouraged to self-complete; interviewers gave minimal help (reading aloud and writing answers) only when children reported difficulty. Timing varied by disease; AcLL inpatients were retested next day, outpatients within a week. EQ-5D-Y-5L was always completed first to avoid level-2/4 avoidance if 3L came first. Parents supplied clinical data and completed proxy EQ-5D-Y elsewhere. Analyses assessed missingness, ceiling, logical redistribution, Spearman correlations, Gwet’s AC1 test–retest agreement among children reporting no change, and aligned level changes for responsiveness (Methods, “Study design” and “Analysis”).

## Findings

Neither version had missing responses. At baseline, overall 11111 ceiling was 21.3% for 3L and 17.5% for 5L; the difference was not significant (p=0.88). No significant ceiling reduction occurred for individual dimensions either (Results, “Feasibility and ceiling effect”; Table 2). Most 3L level-1 responses mapped to 5L level 1. Level 2 (“some problems”) usually mapped to 5L level 2 (“a little bit of a problem”). Severe 3L level 3 responses mapped to 5L level 4 for mobility, pain/discomfort, and worried/sad/unhappy, and mostly level 5 for looking after myself and usual activities. Inconsistency ranged 8.7% (mobility) to 16.1% (worried/sad/unhappy) (Results, “Redistribution properties”).

Convergent validity with PedsQL Generic was about equal for both versions, with correlations varying weak to strong by disease group and variance. Among the 44/243 stable retest pairs, 5L showed slightly better stability in all dimensions, at least substantial Gwet’s AC1 >0.61. In the 229 follow-up participants, 95.6% reported improvement; the proportion with aligned positive changes was larger for 5L (Results, “Test–Retest” and “Responsiveness”).

The authors conclude that 5L has no clear overall superiority, but it better captures moderate-to-severe problems, severe-state distinctions, stability, and longitudinal change without reducing feasibility or validity. Limitations include missing completion-time/qualitative feasibility measures, different recall periods from PedsQL, no Indonesian youth tariff, questionnaire order, possible response changes from intervening disease-specific modules, and cognitive demands for younger children (Discussion; Conclusion).
