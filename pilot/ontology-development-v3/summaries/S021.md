# Comparison of online and face-to-face valuation of the EQ-5D-5L using composite time trade-off

## Aim and design

The study tests whether online, unsupervised cTTO can reproduce interviewer-supervised face-to-face (F2F) EQ-5D-5L valuation. It used identical EuroQol 86-state design and cTTO protocol in US samples: 1,134 F2F respondents (11,340 responses) and 501 online respondents (5,010 responses) (Abstract; Methods). Both samples were quota-sampled for age, gender, race, and ethnicity. The F2F study used EQ-VT version 2.0, CAPI interviews in six US metropolitan areas, trained interviewers, aloud reading, five practice states, feedback-module review, and $30 compensation. The online platform was SurveyEngine, modelled on EQ-VT, with automated voice reading, interactive tutorial, forced actions, and a pop-up for tasks under 15 seconds; the feedback module was omitted.

## Preference task and analysis

The cTTO began with conventional TTO for better-than-dead states and switched to lead-time TTO for worse-than-dead states. Ten years in a state were compared with full-health time, with automated ping-pong/titration until approximate indifference. Face validity was assessed by mean and SD by misery score; engagement by trade-offs and time; validity by logical inconsistencies, including 55555. Trading patterns included better-than-dead-only and non-traders.

Each set was modelled with linear regression with respondent-level random intercept (RILR), using dummy decrements from level 1. The study compared preference inversions, dimension ranking, percentage worse than dead, scale range, and an administration-mode dummy. Adjusted models included age, gender, race, ethnicity, region, TTO comprehension, general health, illness experience, education, and health literacy. Logistic regression estimated the odds of an inconsistency.

## Findings

Online means were higher than F2F for most severe states, although lower for the mildest states. Online respondents assigned 55555 the lowest value in only 58.7% of cases (41.3% failed), versus 87.8% F2F (12.2% failed; *p* < 0.001). Online had more short tasks: 15.8% completed all tasks within three trade-offs versus 3.7% F2F. Online mean trade-offs per task were 5.6 versus 6.6 F2F, and median time per task was 46.75 versus 49.75 seconds. Online had more better-than-dead-only traders (46.3% versus 31.0%) and non-traders (7.2% versus 5.7%) (Results, “Raw elicited TTO values and preference patterns”; Table 2).

Online data had over 60% of respondents with at least one inconsistency of any type and over 40% with a 55555 inconsistency; F2F Full rates were 16.0% for any inconsistency and 3.1% for a 55555 inconsistency, while F2F Valid rates were 31.8% and 12.2%, respectively (Results, Fig. 3). Online respondents had more than 2–5 times as many inconsistencies as F2F groups. The online RILR had eight insignificant parameters and inversions for UA4 and SC5; F2F models each had one inversion (UA5). Online scale range was 0.600 versus 1.307 F2F. Online 55555 was 0.400 (better than dead), whereas the F2F value was worse than dead. Administration-mode differences remained 0.27–0.31 utility units after adjustment; online inconsistency odds were 3.635 times F2F (95% CI 2.544–5.193) (Results, “Modeled value sets”).

## Interpretation and limitations

Online data showed poorer understanding, lower engagement, fewer worse-than-dead values, and compromised logical validity. The authors caution that increased online sample size would not solve central validity problems. Mode and respondent-source effects cannot be separated; samples differed in size and health characteristics; some variables were unmeasured; generalisability of this specific online implementation is unclear. Patients may be more motivated than general-population panel respondents. Possible future options include simpler ordinal tasks, personal utility-function checks, and strict but cautious data-quality rules.
