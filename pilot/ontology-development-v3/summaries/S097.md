# Exploring differences and similarities of EQ-5D-3L, EQ-5D-5L and WHOQOL-OLD in recipients of aged care services in Germany

## Aim and population

The paper compares EQ-5D-3L, EQ-5D-5L and the age-specific WHOQOL-OLD in older German adults receiving aged care. It assesses feasibility, test-retest reliability, association between instruments and sensitivity to known-group differences. The purpose is to identify whether EQ-5D measures capture outcomes relevant to older adults and care dependents, and what additional information WHOQOL-OLD provides. A two-stage convenience sample recruited from nursing homes, ambulatory care services and adult day-care centres in Bielefeld, Schwerin and surrounding areas between September 2019 and March 2020. Eligibility was age ≥65 and self-reported need for aged care; official care records were not reviewed. Ethics approval was Bielefeld University 2017–207 (Abstract; Methods—Sampling procedure).

Of 800 invited people, 334 returned questionnaires; five under age 65 were excluded, leaving 329. Retest data were returned by 266, and 168 respondents (64.9% of retest returns) were eligible because their general health category did not change. Sixty-one percent were home-based care recipients and 38.6% nursing-home recipients. Care levels 2 and 3 were most common (30.1% and 27.4%); 18.5% had level 0. The sample included a large proportion of people aged 80+ and people needing assistance (Results—Sample characteristics).

## Instruments and administration

EQ-5D-3L has five dimensions (mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain or discomfort, anxiety or depression), three severity levels and 243 health states. EQ-5D-5L has the same dimensions and five levels (no, slight, moderate, severe or extreme problems), yielding 3,125 states. Both include EQ VAS, a vertical 0 (“the worst health you can imagine”) to 100 (“the best health you can imagine”) scale. The German WHOQOL-OLD is a 24-item, six-facet measure for adults ≥60: sensory abilities; autonomy; past, present and future activities; social participation; death and dying; and intimacy. Facets and total score range 0–100.

Respondents completed one EQ-5D version with EQ VAS, then WHOQOL-OLD, then the other EQ-5D version; order of 3L and 5L was varied. EQ VAS was administered only once because it is identical in both EQ-5D versions. A retest questionnaire was completed after a planned 14 days. Instruments were self-complete, but assistance was allowed. The European VAS tariff was used for 3L index values and Ludwig et al.’s tariff for 5L; recommended procedures generated WHOQOL-OLD scores (Methods—Survey design and Data analysis).

## Analyses

Feasibility used missing values, completion rates, total completion time, and aid required. ICCs used two-way mixed-effects absolute agreement models for interval scores; weighted Cohen’s kappa assessed categorical dimensions/items. Retest eligibility required unchanged general-health response. Pearson or Spearman correlations assessed EQ-5D dimensions, indices and EQ VAS against WHOQOL-OLD total and facets. Known groups were gender, education, German statutory care level and general health status; t-tests/ANOVA, Cohen’s d and eta squared were used. Expected directions were lower scores for male gender, lower education, higher care level and poorer general health (Methods—Data analysis).

## Findings

Missing values for EQ-5D dimensions were below 4%; WHOQOL-OLD item missingness was mostly below 5%, with 5.8% missing for item 12 (“Satisfied with opportunities to continue archiving”). Complete summary scores were available for 94.2% (5L), 96.4% (3L) and 97.2% (WHOQOL-OLD). EQ VAS problems affected 31%: 25.5% did not place the required mark, 2.7% were missing, and about 3% had ranges or mismatching values. Mean total survey completion was 28.4 minutes (range 5–165). Help was required by 59%; 81% needed questions read and 79.4% had responses filled in, with 77.2% needing both. Translation help was reported by 8.8%, but its meaning was unclear (Results—Feasibility).

The retest interval averaged 17 days (median 14; range 7–75). 3L agreement was substantial for all dimensions except usual activities (moderate); 5L agreement was substantial for every dimension. Index ICC was 0.86 for 3L (good) and 0.91 for 5L (excellent); EQ VAS reliability was moderate. The authors report no clear overall superiority, but 5L was often better on reliability.

EQ-5D indices and EQ VAS differentiated care levels and general-health categories with medium to large effects: more extensive care needs and poorer general health produced lower scores. WHOQOL-OLD total and most facets showed similar discrimination; intimacy and death and dying were exceptions for some group contrasts. Gender and education differences were mostly non-significant or small. Correlations between EQ-5D and WHOQOL-OLD were poor to moderate. Health-related facets had expected associations in many cases, while death and dying and intimacy correlated poorly with most EQ-5D components, supporting partially overlapping but distinct constructs (Results—Relationship; Known-group differences; Discussion).

Care-setting differences were descriptive rather than causal. WHOQOL-OLD showed significant home-versus-institution differences for total score, sensory abilities, death and dying and intimacy; EQ-5D measures did not show significant care-setting differences. The authors conclude that WHOQOL-OLD alongside EQ-5D adds information on non-health and age-specific quality of life.

## Limitations and implications

The convenience and two-stage recruitment can cause positive health selection and prevent generalisation. People with dementia or cognitive impairment were not assessed, and these conditions may be underrepresented. Modes of assistance may affect responses. Direction of causality between health and care provision is unclear. The authors recommend further validation, especially evidence on EQ-5D-5L. Findings support using both EQ-5D and WHOQOL-OLD when aged-care outcomes may extend beyond health (Discussion; Strength and limitations; Conclusion).

The authors reported EuroQol Research Foundation grants and that OM and WG were members of the EuroQol Group; no other conflicts were reported (Funding; Competing interests).
