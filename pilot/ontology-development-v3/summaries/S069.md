# Psychometric evaluation of the EQ-5D-Y-3L in Ethiopian pediatric inpatients: comparing self and proxy reports

## Aim, setting, and sample

The study assessed agreement between child/adolescent self-reports and parent/caregiver proxy reports, sociodemographic variation, and responsiveness of the Amharic EQ-5D-Y-3L during hospitalisation. It took place at the University of Gondar Comprehensive Specialized Hospital, Ethiopia. Data were collected from June 1 to October 31, 2023, at admission and discharge. Eligible patients were 4–18 years old with prevalent acute illness. Children with low consciousness, disorientation, or visual impairment were excluded (Abstract; Methods > Study design and setting; Study population).

Of 985 dyads recruited by age-category quotas, 28 were excluded for incomplete descriptive-system or EQ VAS data. The analysis included 957 dyads, mean age 10.7 years (SD 4.3), 52.7% boys, and 81.3% rural residents. Insufficient household income was reported for 69.1%. Common diagnoses included respiratory infection or pneumonia/pharyngitis (27.3%), genitourinary conditions (13.8%), and malaria (12.2%). The study was in a referral hospital serving a large rural region (Results > Sample; Table 1).

## Instrument and administration

EQ-5D-Y-3L has mobility, looking after myself, doing usual activity, pain/discomfort, and worried, sad or unhappy. Each dimension has “no problems”, “some problems”, or “a lot of problems”. EQ VAS ranges from 0 (worst imaginable health) to 100 (best imaginable health). The modified Amharic version was used. Children aged 8–18 self-completed. Children aged 4–7 also self-completed, with trained data collectors reading instructions and questions aloud and giving simple clarification without changing meaning. Parents/caregivers completed the proxy version. Clinical diagnosis and length of stay came from medical records (Methods > Data collection).

Because no Ethiopian value set was available, the Zimbabwe value set was used for the EQ-5D-Y-3L index. The choice was based on geographic proximity, socioeconomic similarities, and shared regional health challenges. Physician-recorded clinical improvement was used as a responsiveness anchor (Methods > Analyses).

## Analysis

Level 1 was coded “no problems”; levels 2 and 3 were combined as “problems”. Chi-square compared problem proportions. Weighted Cohen’s kappa assessed dimension agreement. ICC assessed index and EQ VAS agreement. Spearman’s rank correlation assessed convergence between index and EQ VAS. Responsiveness used percentage reduction in problems, paired t-tests for index and EQ VAS, and Paretian Classification of Health Change (PCHC). In PCHC, a health state is better when it improves on at least one dimension and worsens on none. Data were analysed in STATA 17 (Methods > Analyses).

## Findings

- Dimension agreement was fair to moderate at admission, with weighted kappa 0.28–0.38. Agreement was lower at discharge for worried, sad or unhappy (κ=0.15). Agreement varied by age, gender, and residence. Agreement was highest around ages 7–15 and lower at ages 4–6 and 16–18. Older boys and urban residents showed reduced discharge agreement for some scores (Abstract; Results > Agreement).
- Index and EQ VAS agreement was acceptable overall. Total index ICC was 0.582 at admission and 0.498 at discharge. EQ VAS ICC was 0.671 at admission and 0.676 at discharge. Correlations between index and EQ VAS were moderate: child/adolescent reports 0.453 at admission and 0.514 at discharge; parent/caregiver reports 0.512 and 0.520 (Results > Agreement; Convergent validity).
- Both perspectives showed major improvement after treatment. Child/adolescent problem reporting fell by 75.2% for Mobility, 76.2% for Looking After Myself, and 75.4% for Worried, Sad or Unhappy. Parent/caregiver reports showed 82.0% reduction for Mobility, 83.5% for Looking After Myself, and 85.1% for Worried, Sad or Unhappy. Paired index and EQ VAS changes were significant (p<0.001). Most participants were improved under PCHC (Results > Convergent validity and responsiveness; Tables 5–6; Figs. 2–3).

## Interpretation and limitations

The results support responsiveness during inpatient treatment and moderate self–proxy agreement. The authors state that age-appropriate and context-sensitive assessment is important and that both perspectives can inform care and resource allocation in low-resource settings. Emotional domains had poorer agreement because they are less observable to proxies.

The hospital setting selected patients expected to improve and may overestimate responsiveness. Inter-data-collector reliability was not assessed. There were no cognitive interviews for younger children, and children with developmental disabilities were excluded. The Zimbabwe value set may not represent Ethiopian preferences. Selection bias, social desirability, and acute emotional states may affect reports. Findings may not generalise to outpatient or healthy populations (Discussion; Limitations; Conclusion).
