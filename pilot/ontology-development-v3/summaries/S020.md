## Aim and setting

The study compares four ways to obtain a 0 = dead anchor for latent-scale EQ-5D-Y values: visual analogue scale (VAS), lag-time TTO, DCE with duration (DCEd), and location-of-dead (LOD) (Abstract; Introduction). UK general-public adults valued EQ-5D-3L states from an adult perspective and EQ-5D-Y states from a 10-year-old child perspective. The main sample had 299 complete interviews conducted September–December 2017 in the Midlands and London/Southeast, recruited by door-knocking; one additional participant withdrew because the subject was distressing. Mean interview time was 40 minutes. A nine-person cognitive pilot preceded fieldwork, and five experienced interviewers used EQ-VT-based computer-assisted personal interviews.

## Instruments and methods

EQ-5D-3L described adult health; EQ-5D-Y described child health. Both use five dimensions and 3-level profiles, with 11111 as best and 33333 as worst. Respondents completed all methods under both perspectives, with perspective order randomized and a pop-up at the halfway point. A 50-person extended sample used EQ-5D-Y under both perspectives.

VAS rated 33333 and “Dead” on 0–100, assuming 11111 = 100; values were rescaled as `(Rating_h − Rating_dead)/(Rating_11111 − Rating_dead)`. Lag-time TTO compared 20 years full health followed by death with ten years in the state followed by ten years full health and death; `U = (t − 10)/10`. DCEd forced choices between two states lasting 1, 3, 6, or 10 years. LOD ranked level-1 dimension descriptors, then used up to five personalized choices between 10 years in a state and immediate death, with bisection to locate dead within 15–16 states. DCEd used six blocks of seven pairs selected through a six-step D-efficient design (Methods, “Study design”).

The DCEd analysis estimated conditional-logit models with constant proportionality and non-constant proportionality, including fixed 1/2 power and estimated power. LOD state ranges were converted to midpoint latent utilities using coefficients from a separate UK EQ-5D-Y DCE study, then rescaled between dead and 11111. TTO values at 0 and −1 were not censored (Methods, “Analysis”).

## Findings

Child-perspective anchors were generally higher than adult-perspective anchors. Anxiety/depression was most important for the child perspective; usual activities was most important for adults; mobility was least important under both (Results, “Ranking”). VAS values for 33333 were higher for children, and 70.2% valued 33333 better than dead for children versus 36.8% for adults. Mean TTO value for 33333 was about 0 (median exactly 0) for children and clearly negative for adults. DCEd values were positive for children and negative for adults, but child models were internally inconsistent because respondents appeared to prioritize duration over health problems.

In method comparison, 41.8% of child-perspective respondents versus 22.7% of adult-perspective respondents valued 33333 better than dead by TTO; VAS proportions were 70.2% versus 36.8%; LOD proportions were 32.8% versus 23.1%. Only 23.4% were internally consistent across all three methods. The LOD method could not handle respondents who considered 33333 better than dead, placing dead below the descriptive system (Results, Table 4). A majority (81.9%) found child-perspective questions more difficult; 54.5% found imagining a 10-year-old somewhat or very difficult, 81.6% expected answers to change for another child age, and 62.9% supported equal adult/child priority (Table 5).

## Interpretation and limitations

VAS, lag-time TTO, and DCEd were considered feasible anchors; LOD was least feasible because of out-of-range dead locations and its reliance on coefficients from another sample. Selection criteria include feasibility, acceptability to decision makers, online administration, coherence with the unanchored DCE, and consistency with adult HTA values. VAS is not choice-based; lag-time TTO is complex for online use; DCEd needs further design/model research; and using separate samples can violate preference comparability. Age specificity, adult self-perspective versus child other-perspective, limited tasks, inconsistent individual methods, and respondent distress are limitations. The paper stresses that commensurability depends on whether adult and child budgets are shared or ring-fenced.
