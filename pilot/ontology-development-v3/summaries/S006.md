# Exploring the importance of controlling heteroskedasticity and heterogeneity in health valuation: a case study on Dutch EQ-5D-5L

## Research aim and design

The study examined whether health valuation should control simultaneously for heteroskedasticity, taste heterogeneity and scale heterogeneity. It used a Dutch EQ-5D-5L dataset collected online in September 2016 from Survey Sampling International. The sample was stratified by known EQ-5D-5L impairment rather than designed as fully representative. Three hundred subjects were targeted; 385 completed and five click-through respondents were excluded, leaving 380. Fifty-two percent were men, respondents were divided across age groups, and 57.1% reported chronic illness (Overview; Demographics).

The EQ-5D-5L has mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort and anxiety/depression, each with five levels and 3125 profiles. The study used 24 paired-comparison (PC) tasks and 25 best–worst scaling case 2 (BWS) tasks after dropping the dominant PC task. In PC, respondents choose between two profiles. In case-2 BWS, one profile is shown and respondents select its best and worst attribute levels. The 25-profile BWS design was based on an orthogonal main effects plan (OMEP), with no all-level-equal states and one dominant PC comparison. Respondents completed identical task sequences and profiles to support heterogeneity analysis (EQ-5D-5L Profiles; Experimental Design).

## Analysis

The estimand is on a “pits scale”, with 55555=0 and 11111=1, because no duration or death attribute was included; it is not a QALY scale. Twenty incremental variables represented level changes. A hypothetical 00000 ideal and five ancillary variables were used for BWS identification; ancillary variables do not affect EQ-5D-5L values. Conditional logit, heteroskedastic conditional logit (HCL) and heteroskedastic scale-adjusted latent class (SALC) models were estimated by maximum likelihood in R 4.0.2.

Heteroskedasticity was modelled as task-level variance related to sequence and task type (PC, best, worst), separating it from latent scale heterogeneity. Taste classes represent differences in relative attribute effects; scale classes represent differences in absolute effect. SALC allowed two taste and two scale classes because the sample was small. Membership used demographics, education, gender, competency (passing the dominant task), and perceived task difficulty. BIC, AIC and CAIC supported class selection (Analysis; Heteroskedasticity and Differences by Task; Heterogeneity).

## Findings

Thirty-one percent found BWS difficult, 38% found PC difficult and 45.5% had no preference. Seventy-two respondents (19%) failed the PC dominance task. HCL fit better than conditional logit: BIC decreased by 1666.29 (CL 64,458.32; HCL 62,792.03). The sequence effect increased scale up to about 14 tasks and reduced it thereafter. PC had a significantly negative scale effect; best tasks had a positive effect. Heteroskedasticity changed standard errors modestly (Feasibility; Difference between Homoskedastic and Heteroskedastic Results).

After HCL adjustment, PC and BWS values were highly correlated (Pearson 0.9167, 95% CI 0.9109–0.9222) and had Lin concordance 0.7658 (0.7542–0.7769); median absolute difference was 0.0732 (IQR 0.0592–0.1565). The SALC model had the lowest BIC (56,698.35). Taste class 1 had logically consistent parameters and was treated as the Dutch EQ-5D-5L value set on the pits scale. Taste class 2 was a “garbage class” without relative attribute importance. Respondents failing dominance were more likely to have a lower scale class.

Compared with the original Dutch value set, SALC coefficients had Pearson correlation 0.7295 and Lin concordance 0.6904. Across all 3125 profiles, SALC values had Pearson 0.9293 and Lin concordance 0.8835. The paper interprets SALC as automatically dispensing with a garbage class and adjusting the scale for respondents who failed the dominant task (Differences between PC and BWS; Taste and Scale Heterogeneity; Differences between Original and New Values).

The study is exploratory and has limitations: pits rather than QALY scale, small sample, uncertain p-value interpretation, possible confounding of taste and scale, constant comparator, missing income and task-time variables, and dependence on a particular SALC parametrisation. It concludes that case-2 BWS can produce values similar to PC after controls, but recommends larger studies with richer behavioural information (Discussion; Conclusions).
