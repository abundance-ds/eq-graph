## Aim, design and participants

The online survey examined HTA practitioners’ practices, views and needs for measuring and valuing HRQoL to generate cost-effectiveness evidence. It ran April 2023–January 2024. Purposive recruitment through international professional networks targeted HTA agencies in 60 countries; 49 countries received invitations and 45 produced responses. Eligible participants were agency employees or contracted professionals who reviewed, generated or used QALY-based evidence, or other HTA personnel able to complete the English survey and open-ended questions. Ethics approval was NUS-IRB-2022-426. [Source: Abstract; Methods—Sampling and recruitment; Participants]

There were 238 respondents from 45 countries and 65 HTA agencies, with a median four respondents per country. Public-sector employees were 71.9%; 58.8% had at least four years of HTA experience; 81.1% reported QALY-estimation guidance; 89.5% had QALY responsibilities; 61.3% reviewed industry/contractor QALY evidence; and 91.2% worked at national level. Regional responses were Asia 95, Commonwealth 34, Western Europe 31, Central/Eastern Europe 25, Latin America 38 and Middle East/Africa 15. [Source: Results—Sample characteristics; Table 1]

## Survey and analysis

An anonymous Qualtrics survey was designed for completion within 20 minutes and pilot-tested with HTA personnel in Singapore, Indonesia, Canada, England, Norway, Australia, New Zealand, Colombia and Argentina. Six sections covered Utility Instruments, Elicitation Methods, Data Source, Data Quality and Appropriateness, and Research Topics of Importance. A four-point frequency scale was “never/not sure”, “occasionally”, “often” and “very often”. Closed questions used descriptive summaries by country and region; research priorities used country, regional and global importance scores. Open-ended answers were forward-back translated and analysed by structured content analysis. STATA v14 was used. [Source: Methods—Survey form; Statistical analysis]

## Instruments, methods and data sources

Among respondents involved in QALY work (n = 213), the most frequently used utility instruments were EQ-5D (“very often”), SF-6D (“occasionally”) and EQ-5D-Y (“occasionally”). EQ-5D-Y use was “never” in Western Europe and SF-6D “often” in Latin America. The most frequently used elicitation methods were TTO, VAS and SG, all “often”; TTO was “often” or “very often” across regions, while DCE was usually “occasionally” and BWS/PTO ranged from “never” to “occasionally”. Choice of instrument mattered to 87.7% and choice of elicitation method to 79.8%. TTO was cited as fit-for-purpose because of theoretical foundation, trade-offs, cardinal utilities, validation and ease of use, but high cognitive burden was a common limitation. Forty-seven respondents said method choice depends on disease area, context and evidence. [Source: Results—Use and importance of utility instruments; Use and importance of preference elicitation methods; Tables 2–3]

General-public preferences from another country were used “often”, while local public preferences were “occasionally”. Western Europe and Commonwealth respondents used their own-country public most often; other regions often used foreign public or patient values. Ninety-one percent said preference source matters. Of 144 qualitative responses, 81 favoured general-public values (guidance, consistency, taxpayers, and patient adaptation), 53 favoured patient values (patient voice and disease experience), and 10 supported both. Seventy-nine explicitly supported own-country data; none preferred foreign data over local data. [Source: Results—Use and importance of health preference data sources]

## Data problems and research priorities

“Often” encountered data-quality issues were poor sample representativeness, small utility-data samples, poor matching between available health states and CEA models, and use of multiple instruments or elicitation methods within one model. Outdated data were generally “occasionally” encountered. The top global priorities were recent tariffs/value sets (importance score 0.20), instruments for children/adolescents (0.19), and instruments capturing healthcare plus social-care impact (0.17). Social care ranked first in Asia (0.21) and Western Europe (0.33); recent tariffs ranked first in Middle East/Africa (0.33) and Central/Eastern Europe (0.31); carers ranked first in the Commonwealth (0.23); inequality in care ranked first in Latin America (0.22). Children were second in all regions except Western Europe and Latin America. [Source: Results—Use of data with quality concerns; Research priorities; Table 4]

## Interpretation and limitations

The authors see a gap between guidance and practice, with data scarcity and poor matching driving foreign-data use. They suggest user-oriented development with sustained HTA-agency engagement, public HSU repositories, systematic publication of HRQoL data, crosswalk tools and better guidance. Limitations include fewer than three responses in 12 countries, uncertainty about the most appropriate respondent, snowball recruitment through EuroQol networks, individual rather than agency-level views, no verification of eligibility and possible EuroQol familiarity bias. The survey did not support reliable subgroup analysis. [Source: Discussion; Conclusions]
