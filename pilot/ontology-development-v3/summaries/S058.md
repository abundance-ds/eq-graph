## Purpose and scope

The paper considers how to identify domains for a generic preference-based measure spanning health, social care, and informal-carer quality of life, with the goal of supporting QALYs and cross-sector resource allocation. It argues that EQ-5D, ASCOT, ASCOT-Carer, and CarerQol-type sector-specific measures cannot always combine outcomes without double counting or omission. The proposed measure is the EQ-HWB (EQ health and wellbeing instrument), developed within the “Extending the QALY” project (Abstract; Background).

The target concept is health, social-care, and carer-related quality of life, not all quality of life. It includes effects of physical and mental conditions, treatment, self-management, disability, social-care need/use, and informal caring. The paper compares three domain-identification approaches: existing measures, QoL theory, and views of relevant future subjects (Background; “Options for identifying domains”).

## Adopted requirements and theory

The project combined all three approaches. Requirement 1 was coverage of aspects identified by service users and informal carers as important. Requirement 2 was fitness for resource allocation, with a single cardinal figure anchored at zero equivalent to dead and one equivalent to full quality of life; interpersonal/inter-temporal comparability; inclusivity; low burden; content, face, and construct measurement properties; precision, responsiveness, reliability, avoidance of double counting, and translatability. Requirement 3 was alignment with health/social-care policymakers and practitioners (Methods, “Requirements for the new measure”).

The theoretical position was extra-welfarist, with a multidimensional objective-list account and social preferences. It also drew on the capabilities framework, but the paper states that actual functioning would be used unless capability/opportunity was raised by stakeholders and could be measured without undermining fitness for purpose. The conceptual model was adapted from Wilson and Cleary, expanded beyond biological function to caring and receipt of health/social care, and included functioning/activity, social connectedness, feelings/physical sensations, and identity. Expectations, adaptation, and response shift were recognised; a health-perception stage was omitted because value would be based on social judgement (Methods, “The theoretical approach adopted”).

## Domain selection method

Attributes were drawn directly from a large-scale literature review of qualitative studies involving patients, social-care users, and carers across conditions, ages, ethnicities, and Europe, USA, Canada, Australia, and New Zealand. The project used framework analysis: an a priori model was modified as themes emerged. Four criteria guided dropping or merging domains: reasonable consensus of importance; acceptability for self-report; avoidance of mostly instrumental attributes and double counting; and removal/merging of conceptually overlapping attributes. Examples dropped were dexterity (captured by activities, frustration, and confidence), knowledge/information (captured by feelings, activities, control, and coping), and “being a burden to others” (acceptability concerns). “Ability to keep things normal” merged with coping; disclosure/reaction of others merged with stigma (Methods, “Domain selection approach”).

Iterative decisions were checked against literature and service-user terminology and consulted through a PPIE group, NICE staff and Citizen’s Council, EuroQol Descriptive Systems Working Group, Steering Group (n=12), and online Advisory Group (n=124). Final selection used face-validity interviews, psychometric analysis, and further stakeholder consultation.

## Implications and limitations

Strengths were explicit theory, qualitative evidence, service-user voice, policy/public perspectives, psychometrics, transparency, and extensive consultation. Limits include the number of domains possible under valuation methods, self-completion design and uncertain proxy completion, restricted relevance outside health/social care (for example housing), possible omission of public views, and a potential deficit model. Validation against sector-specific and age-specific measures remains necessary (Discussion).
