## Aim and valuation setting

The study explored interviewer conversational patterns beyond quantitative quality-control (QC) indicators and examined how interviewer–respondent interaction may affect data quality. It was nested in the Italian EQ-5D-5L valuation study, in which 1183 EQ-VT interviews were collected October 2020–February 2021 by 11 interviewers through videoconferencing. The EQ-VT protocol uses composite time trade-off (cTTO) and discrete choice experiment (DCE) tasks: two wheelchair examples and three practice-state questions, 10 real cTTO questions, and seven DCE tasks. Interviews were collected in batches of 10 per interviewer with a QC call after each batch; a 10-interview practice round preceded official collection. Of 152 video recordings, 8 were pilot, 63 first batch, 57 second batch, and 24 later. [Abstract; Introduction; Methods—Data Collection]

## Qualitative methods and scoring

Two researchers transcribed verbatim, in Italian, 24 recordings from two interviewers (six first-batch and six second-batch recordings per interviewer), from the wheelchair example through the fifth non-practice cTTO task. Interviewers were selected because their initial QC data showed substantially different time stamps, number of moves, and data distributions. Conversational Analysis, supplemented by prior literature, identified patterns and themes. A scoring system used Likert 1–3 or 1–5 and yes/no items. Positive and negative items were standardized, averaged within scale, and combined with item-count weighting to yield separate 0–1 overall positive and negative scores. [Methods—Transcription and Coding; Development of a Scoring System]

For scoring, 42 other recordings were selected (three per interviewer in batch 1 and three in batch 2) from seven of the 11 interviewers; four interviewers had too few recordings. The respondents were aged 18–66, mean age 42; mean interview length was 49 minutes. Two authors independently scored recordings, with a third scorer to avoid self-scoring bias. QC results were shown to coders only after scoring. [Methods—Development of a Scoring System]

## Patterns and findings

Conversational analysis identified 20 positive and 14 negative patterns in two main themes—“task execution” and “communication skills”—and five sub-themes. Positive patterns included presenting alternatives neutrally (PC02), explaining there were no right or wrong answers (PC03), clearly illustrating study purposes and tasks (PC04), using previous examples (PC05), providing useful information (PC12), using respondent jargon without changing correctness (PC14), allowing reflection (PC15), encouraging low-reactive respondents (PC16), appending engagement questions (PC17), keeping rhythm (PC18), confirming understanding (PC19), and asking respondents to read health states aloud (PC20). Negative patterns included incorrect (NC01), incomplete (NC07), confusing (NC08), or redundant (NC09) explanations; accepting a zero without probing (NC02); forcing an answer (NC03); potentially influencing information (NC04); moving forward without checking understanding (NC05); skipping steps (NC06); interrupting at the wrong moment (NC10); losing interview lead (NC11); too-informal language (NC12); too-fast tone (NC13); and tense tone (NC14). [Results—Conversational Analysis; Table 1]

Examples show positive repetition of years to give up and inverse trade-off explanations to improve understanding and precision, and references to prior examples to prevent inconsistent values. Negative examples show respondents talking over interviewers, interviewers forcing a 4-year answer to obtain more diverse values, interrupting reasoning, and failing to slow shortcutting or probe a zero response. The authors describe this as possible “cheating” or an unintended “complying effect” arising from QC pressure. [Results—Table 2 and accompanying extracts]

Across seven interviewers, most showed increased positive patterns and/or reduced negative patterns from batch 1 to batch 2. The normalized mean score was 0.68 for positive checks and 0.49 for negative checks. Interviewer 3 and interviewer 7 showed both lower negative and higher positive scores. A sharp positive jump between the third and fourth scored interviews (last batch-1 and first batch-2 interviews) may reflect verbal QC feedback. Only interviewer 1 showed a double negative outcome. [Results—Scoring the Video Recordings; Table 3]

Qualitative and quantitative QC aligned preliminarily: interviewer 3, a qualitative “best performer”, had above-average WTD and BTD TTO duration and moves; interviewer 1, the “worst performer”, had below-average durations and fewer WTD moves. A full comparison was outside scope. The only sub-theme whose items all improved was clarity. [Results—Tables 4–5]

## Implications and limits

The patterns may complement quantitative QC and inform EQ-VT training worldwide, especially when distributions are suboptimal or inconsistencies are high. Suggested comparisons include cTTO time, clustering at 1, 0.5, 0, −0.5, and −1, inconsistent-response proportions, mean utility, and TTO moves. Limitations are exploratory scope, only 42 scored recordings, pattern development from two interviewers, incomplete batch coverage, videoconferencing rather than in-person administration, possible random sampling effects, and exclusion of non-verbal communication (facial expression, gestures, voice, posture). The pattern list may not be exhaustive. [Discussion; Conclusions]
