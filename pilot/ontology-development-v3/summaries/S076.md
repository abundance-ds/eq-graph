# Feasibility of the EQ-5D in the elderly population: a systematic review of the literature

## Aim and search

The review summarised feasibility of EQ-5D-3L and EQ-5D-5L in samples exclusively aged 65 years or older and examined interviewer support. Searches covered PubMed, PsycINFO (EBSCO), and the EuroQol Research Foundation website, in English or German, with manual reference-list searching. Inclusion required EQ-5D-3L or 5L use, a minimum sample age of 65, and feasibility information as a primary or secondary analysis. Feasibility outcomes were missing responses, completion rate, completion time, administration mode, and qualitative completion problems (Abstract; Methods—Literature search and data extraction).

The initial search to June 2019 yielded 2,063 records plus 12 manual references; 1,766 records were screened after deduplication and 153 full texts assessed. Fourteen articles qualified, and the November 2020 update added three, giving 17 studies. Sixteen were quantitative and one qualitative. Four studies used general populations and 13 patient populations; sample sizes ranged from 10 to 3,073. The abstract reports 15 studies using EQ-5D-3L and only two using EQ-5D-5L; the main text states that 14 studies used 3L and one additionally used a cognitive bolt-on. Most studies were European; others were from Australia, Canada, and South Africa (Results—Study selection and Study characteristics; Figure 1; Tables 1–2).

## Feasibility evidence

Eleven studies reported missing values, 13 completion rates, nine qualitative completion statements, and three completion time. Dimension-level missingness for 3L ranged from 0 to 10.7%, mostly below 7%. The only detailed 5L missingness report found 10.5% missing on usual activities. EQ VAS missingness was 2.3–25.3% and generally exceeded descriptive-system missingness. Completion of 3L was around or above 90% in most studies, with two studies just above 80%; the only 5L completion estimate was 89.5% (Results—Feasibility information).

Both versions could usually be completed in less than five minutes. Qualitative reports described the instruments as brief and easy to use. Comprehension issues concerned narrow or restrictive item wording, and EQ VAS understanding was problematic in some studies; one study reported 27% having difficulty with EQ VAS. The majority (13) used self-completion, with interviewer support available on request in nine. Two used interviewer-based administration. In one rehabilitation study, 50% required an interviewer; age-specific need was 11% at age 65, 37% at 75, and 73% at 85. Another study reported 55% requiring additional interviewer help (Results—Feasibility information).

## Interpretation

The review characterises elderly feasibility as roughly less than 10% missing descriptive responses and about 90% computable index values, with greater burden for EQ VAS. Feasibility was labelled inconsistently as “feasibility,” “completion,” “practicality,” or “acceptability”; the authors recommend using feasibility as the common term. The evidence suggests slightly worse outcomes than younger populations but still good applicability, especially for 3L. The review could not quantify the effect of interviewer support because studies did not compare assisted and non-assisted groups and were heterogeneous. Interviewer administration may prevent missing data but can add cost, interviewer effects, socially desirable responses, and measurement error (Discussion).

Only two studies assessed 5L, so evidence for 5L is weak. The authors expected comparable or better 5L feasibility because it retains brevity and may let respondents map health more precisely, but they also note possible cognitive burden. They call for studies that compare both versions in older adults, report missing values, completion time, EQ VAS, and interviewer support.

## Limits and implications

All included studies came from western and developed countries, limiting generalisation to Asia, Africa, and South America. Strict age eligibility excluded mixed-age studies. Samples varied from healthy independently living respondents to frail or cognitively impaired patients. Terminology variation may have caused missed studies, and 5L evidence was especially sparse. Feasibility findings should therefore be interpreted by population, cognitive status, disease, and administration mode rather than as a single elderly estimate (Discussion; Conclusion).
