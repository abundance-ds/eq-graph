# Examining the Association Between Equity-Related Factors and EQ-5D-3L Health Utilities of Patients with Cancer

## Aim, setting, and population

The study examined associations between equity-related patient characteristics and EQ-5D-3L health utilities in a real-world cancer sample, because clinical-trial utilities may over-estimate HRQoL in socioeconomically advantaged participants (Abstract; Introduction). This was a prospective convenience sample at Sunnybrook Odette Cancer Centre, Toronto, Ontario, Canada. Adults (18+) with any solid tumour or hematological malignancy, starting publicly reimbursed systemic therapy, completed the EQ-5D-3L, EQ-VAS, and demographic questionnaire during a chemotherapy appointment. Accrual ran May–November 2024. The full analytic sample was *n* = 170; the birth-sex model excluded sex-specific cancers (*n* = 111) (Methods, Sections 2.1–2.2).

## Measures and scoring

EQ-5D-3L has mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort, and anxiety/depression, each with no problems, some problems, or extreme problems/unable to perform activities. EQ-VAS records current health from 0 (worst imaginable) to 100 (best imaginable). EQ-5D-3L utilities were calculated with the Canadian TTO scoring model using the `eq5d` R package version 0.15.7. The Canadian value set ranged from 1.000 for 11,111 to predicted mean −0.340 for 33,333 (Methods, Sections 2.2–2.4). Covariates were age, sex, education, marital status, employment, family income, ethnicity, and primary cancer site.

## Analysis

Ordinary least squares (OLS) multivariable regression estimated associations with EQ-5D-3L utility. Main-effects models and birth-sex-by-age-category interactions (<50, 50–74, >75 years) were assessed with ANOVA; nested models used Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), with smaller BIC indicating the simpler model. Spearman’s rank correlations examined income categories against each EQ-5D-3L dimension because utilities were left-skewed and income was ordinal. Analyses used R 4.5.0 (Methods, Sections 2.3–2.4).

## Principal findings

The sample was mostly White (65.5%), college/university educated (75.6%), married/common-law (68.9%), and retired (50.3%); 46.5% did not report income. Highest mean utility by cancer site was colorectal 0.918 (SD 0.127), while lowest means were thoracic 0.712, genitourinary 0.717, and upper gastrointestinal 0.731 (Results, Sections 3.1–3.2; Table 2).

Lower income was associated with more problems in pain/discomfort (Spearman ρ = 0.291, *p* = 0.008) and anxiety/depression (ρ = 0.219, *p* = 0.046). The “prefer not to answer” and CAD 0–29,999 groups had more “some problems” and “extreme problems”, especially for usual activities, pain/discomfort, and anxiety/depression; the largest “extreme problems” proportion was 18% for pain/discomfort in CAD 0–29,999 (Results, Section 3.3, Table 3, Fig. 1).

In both multivariable models, CAD 0–29,000 income and undisclosed income were associated with significantly lower utility. In the birth-sex model, disutility estimates were −0.202 (95% CI −0.371 to −0.033) for income <CAD 0–29K and −0.123 (−0.235 to −0.012) for undisclosed income. In the all-cancer model without birth sex, estimates were −0.163 (−0.280 to −0.046) and −0.106 (−0.184 to −0.028). Colorectal cancer had higher utility than the reference head-and-neck group by 0.135 or 0.147, depending on model. Birth-sex-by-age interaction was not significant (*p* = 0.359) (Results, Section 3.4, Table 4).

## Interpretation, limitations, and use

The authors state this is the first Canadian real-world oncology estimate quantifying an income effect on EQ-5D-3L utility. They argue that trial-based utilities may over-estimate HRQoL and that equity-informed HTA should consider real-world health utilities, income, and other patient characteristics. The study is limited by cross-sectional single-site convenience sampling, small sample size, unmeasured confounding (for example comorbidity and cancer stage), possible ceiling effects of EQ-5D-3L, and wider uncertainty in the sex-specific model. Longitudinal collection and distributional cost-effectiveness analysis are proposed (Discussion; Conclusions).
