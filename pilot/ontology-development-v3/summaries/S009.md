## Objective and design

This German exploratory study tested EQ-VT version 1.1 and three modifications intended to improve EQ-5D-5L cTTO quality: a ranking task, a better-than-dead (BTD)/worse-than-dead (WTD) split, and a feedback module. It did not produce a national value set. The design had 200 German general-public respondents, randomly assigned to control (current EQ-VT 1.1; n=100) or test (modified EQ-VT 1.1; n=100). Five academic interviewers conducted computer-assisted personal interviews in Bielefeld and Hanover. Participants were recruited by personal contact, newspaper advertisement and university bulletins. Each interviewer completed training, pilot interviews and QC; interviewers needed at least 35 interviews in the final team (Study Design).

EQ-5D-5L has mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort and anxiety/depression, five levels and 3125 states. cTTO used 80 design states plus 55555 and one mild state per block, giving ten states per block over ten blocks. DCE used 196 pairs over 28 blocks, but DCE results were reported elsewhere. Interviews contained EQ-5D-5L self-report, background questions, wheelchair example, three practice states, ten cTTO tasks, debriefing, DCE, comments and further background questions (Methods of Eliciting Preferences).

## Modifications

In the test arm, respondents first ranked the ten cTTO states and death using physical cards. This ranking determined a top-to-bottom/intermediate presentation order. The BTD/WTD split first completed all conventional TTO BTD tasks; states judged WTD were parked and all WTD lead-time tasks were completed later. The control arm switched between conventional and lead-time TTO during each task. The feedback module showed the cTTO-implied ranking after ten tasks; respondents could flag but not revalue problematic states. The WTD range was −1 to 0; BTD was 0 to 1 (Modifications 1–3).

## Results

Both arms had higher quality than first-wave EQ-VT 1.0 studies. WTD values were 16.8% overall (18.2% control; 15.4% test), and more respondents supplied at least one WTD value in the test arm (71% versus 62%). Mean cTTO for severity 6 was 0.95 control and 0.97 test; severity 25 (55555) was −0.36 control and −0.44 test. The test arm had somewhat higher severe-state discrimination. There was little clustering at −1, −0.5, 0, 0.5 and 1. Feedback did not significantly change mean cTTO values.

Sixteen percent of control respondents and 13% of test respondents had at least one inconsistency. Feedback reduced this to 6% in the test arm (p≤0.001). Inconsistent responses fell from 1.4% to 0.7% after feedback (p=0.01). Forty test-arm respondents removed one or more values; 6.7% of responses were removed, most often 55555. The ranking task added about eight minutes in comparable studies and was reported as cognitively burdensome. Respondents and interviewers found the BTD/WTD split easier; feedback was viewed as useful (cTTO Data; Debriefing).

The ranking task was not recommended for the main German study because it increased burden and did not show a separable quantitative benefit. The authors decided to implement BTD/WTD separation and feedback in the main German valuation study. Limitations include simultaneous testing of three modifications, convenience/university recruitment, young and highly educated over-representation, and differences in arm demographics. The study did not address time discounting and notes that cTTO −1 censoring may conceal values below −1 (Discussion; Conclusions).
