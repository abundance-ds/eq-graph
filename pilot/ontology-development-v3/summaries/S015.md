## Aim and datasets

The study evaluates Kaizen tasks, in which a respondent sequentially improves one profile and reveals a preference path, against pick-one DCE tasks. It synthesizes the 2020 US COVID-19 vaccination (CVP), 2021 UK Children’s Surgery Outcome Reporting (CSOR), and 2023 US EQ-5D-Y-3L valuation (Y-3L) surveys (Abstract; Methods, “Secondary Datasets”). The analysis used marketing-panel and other online respondents but restricted the CVP sample to people with comparable improvement directions and excluded 230 CSOR respondents previously removed from the primary study. Final panel samples were CVP *N*=652 (3 Kaizen tasks), CSOR *N*=807 (3 tasks), and Y-3L *N*=631 (10 tasks); completion rates from assigned samples were 87%, 69%, and 87%, respectively (Table 1).

CVP used four two-level and one four-level attributes, random order, a full-factorial design, no hold-outs, and single-level improvements. CSOR used five four-level, one three-level, and one five-level attribute, fixed order, three hold-outs, and multi-level improvements. Y-3L used five three-level attributes, random order, one hold-out, and multi-level improvements. Kaizen paths represent sequential choices among improvements; for a 4-improvement task, the path implies 3 sets of choices and 14 probabilities, more evidence than a paired comparison.

## Response behavior and analysis

All surveys were online LimeSurvey DCEs with pick-one and Kaizen components, each beginning with a warm-up task and randomized task order. The authors compared page times using Mood’s median test, information/reset-button use with chi-square tests, and vertical response patterns to assess positional behavior. Attribute-order randomization was available in CVP and Y-3L but not CSOR (Methods, “Descriptive Analyses”).

Main effects were estimated using conditional logit and Zermelo–Bradley–Terry (ZBT) models. Conditional-logit effects are changes in log-odds; ZBT effects are non-negative percentage changes in odds and sum to 1. CVP lacked multi-level improvements, so its logit coefficients identify differences between effects rather than main effects. Standard errors and 95% CIs used respondent-clustered, block-stratified bootstrap with 1,000 iterations. Y-3L sample-size simulations used *n*=34–100 respondents and two Kaizen tasks per respondent. The authors also examined additivity (multi-level effect equal to the sum of single-level effects) and compared Kaizen predictions with paired-comparison probabilities.

## Findings

Kaizen tasks took modestly longer than pick-one tasks: median seconds (excluding warm-up) were 8.77 versus 8.74 (CVP), 18.82 versus 13.62 (CSOR), and 12.48 versus 10.79 (Y-3L). Warm-up tasks took longer and prompted more information-button and reset-button use than later tasks (all *p* < 0.001), supporting a warm-up task. Vertical response patterns exceeded random expectations in CVP and Y-3L. A single hold-out appeared to reduce the main pattern from 11.3% (CVP) to 6.8% (Y-3L), about half (Results, “Response Behaviors”).

All Y-3L logit and ZBT estimates were positive and significant; only 12 of 21 CSOR logit estimates were positive and significant. Logit/ZBT correlations were high: Pearson 0.959 (CVP), 0.832 (CSOR), 0.959 (Y-3L). CVP’s most important attribute was severe side-effect risk, while vaccination effectiveness had the largest effect. CSOR’s most important effect was emergency major operations (EMaj), with the largest effect for reducing six to two (EMaj3). Y-3L’s most important effect was pain or discomfort (PD), with the largest effect for reducing “a lot” to “some” (PD2) (Results, “Main Effects”).

Y-3L Kaizen predictions agreed strongly with paired comparisons: Pearson 0.802 (logit) and 0.882 (ZBT), Lin 0.744 and 0.852. Median Y-3L Kaizen SE was 0.052 for logit and 0.005 for ZBT, versus 0.057 and 0.009 for paired comparisons. Simulation suggested 60 respondents completing 20 Kaizen tasks might yield median SE 0.100 (logit) and 0.010 (ZBT), but this is a prediction requiring confirmation (Results, “Standard Errors”).

## Implications and limitations

The authors recommend warm-up tasks, hold-outs, multi-level improvements, respondent-level randomization of attribute order, and smaller samples than traditional pick-one DCEs. Kaizen may suit large descriptive systems or hard-to-recruit and heterogeneous populations. They do not recommend it yet for nominal attributes or interactions. Evidence is limited to three online surveys, CSOR had fixed order, CVP lacked multi-level improvements, and positional behavior and additivity remain design concerns. Funding supported the dissertation; no competing-interest statement was reported beyond acknowledgements.
