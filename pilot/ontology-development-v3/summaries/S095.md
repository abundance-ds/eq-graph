## Aim and data

The study tests whether EQ VAS scores show a socioeconomic gradient among people who report identical EQ-5D-5L profiles, and whether this suggests a “hidden” gradient not represented by the five descriptive dimensions. It replicates and extends earlier work by using EQ-DAPHNIE data from 32,327 adults aged 25–79 in eight high-income countries: Australia, Canada, France, Germany, the Netherlands, New Zealand, the UK and the US. Participants came from Dynata online panels; country targets were about 4,500, with quota sampling by age, sex, income, region and language (Abstract; Methods—Data and study design).

The analysis excluded respondents under 25 (to allow completion of university education), over 80, and those with BMI ≤15 kg/m². Data-quality checks included consent confirmation, duplicated/similar-question consistency checks and minimum completion times. The final sample was 32,327. Educational attainment was harmonised as Low (primary or secondary), Medium (post-secondary non-tertiary or equivalent) and High (tertiary, including bachelor’s degree or higher). Subjective income status asked how respondents felt about household income nowadays: Comfortable, Coping, Difficult or Very difficult; the last two were combined. The 10 selected EQ-5D-5L profiles were 11111, 11121, 11122, 21121, 11112, 11221, 11131, 11132, 21231 and 11123, covering 61% of respondents (Methods—Variables; Results).

## Measures and analysis

EQ-5D-5L dimensions were mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain or discomfort, and anxiety or depression. EQ VAS ranges from 0 (worst imaginable health) to 100 (best imaginable health). To remove internally inconsistent responses, EQ VAS below 50 was excluded for 11111 and below 30 for all other selected profiles; exclusions were 0.6–3.1% by profile. The authors state that these are logical consistency filters, not robustness choices.

Within each profile, the authors computed mean, standard deviation, median, quartiles and IQR and inspected violin/box plots. Separate linear regression models used EQ VAS as the dependent variable and educational attainment or subjective income status as the explanatory variable, controlling for age and country fixed effects. Survey weights accounted for the complex design. Full Information Maximum Likelihood was used for missing data under a missing-at-random assumption; maximum missingness was 5.8% for EQ VAS and 2.0% for education. Country-stratified models were restricted to profiles 11111 and 11121, with age and sex adjustment. Statistical thresholds were p<0.10, p<0.05 and p<0.01 (Methods—Analyses).

## Principal findings

The overall mean EQ VAS was 72.9 (SD 19.6). High education was reported by 44.0%; Coping income by 41.0%; Difficult by 30.0%; and Comfortable by 29.0%. Median EQ VAS declined across profiles as severity increased: 89 (IQR 80–93) for 11111, 82 for 11121, 79 for 11122, 80 for 21121, 80 for 11112, 79 for 11221, 75 for 11131, 70.5 for 11132, 69 for 21231 and 71 for 11123 (Table 2).

High education was associated with higher EQ VAS in all 10 profiles; seven associations were significant at p<0.10 and four at p<0.01. In profile 11121, Medium versus Low education was +0.863 points and High versus Low +2.135. Subjective income showed a stronger gradient: significant associations occurred in nine of 10 profiles. In profile 11121, Coping versus Difficult was +2.451 and Comfortable versus Difficult +4.969 points. Predictive margins showed gradients for almost all profiles; the only exception was a small, non-significant education decline in 11132 (Results—Regression results; Table 3; Figure 2).

Country-stratified analysis found a positive Comfortable-versus-Difficult coefficient for 11111 in all eight countries, significant at p<0.05 in each. For 11121 it was positive in all eight, significant at p<0.10 in seven and p<0.05 in six; the Netherlands result was non-significant and Canada was p<0.10 only. The income gradient was more stable than the education gradient (Results—Figure 3).

## Interpretation and implications

Two non-exclusive explanations are offered: the five EQ-5D-5L dimensions may omit domains such as vitality, sleep, social relationships and community connectedness; or people from different socioeconomic groups may use EQ VAS response scales differently (reporting heterogeneity). The data cannot distinguish these mechanisms. The authors state that EQ-5D-5L values may understate socioeconomic disparities in population monitoring and equity analysis, so EQ VAS and socioeconomic indicators may be useful alongside EQ-5D-5L values. The result does not invalidate EQ-5D-5L use; the descriptive system was not designed to measure socioeconomic circumstances (Discussion; Conclusion).

The cross-sectional design prevents causal inference. Online recruitment may cause selection bias. Subjective income may share psychological determinants with EQ VAS, such as optimism. EQ VAS may be sensitive to response-style differences. Eight high-income countries limit generalisability. Country-stratified estimates had limited precision, and the selected profiles excluded less prevalent states. The authors report no conflict of interest in the source (Strengths and limitations; Declarations).
