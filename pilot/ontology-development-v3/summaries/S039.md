## Aim, setting, and sample

The study explored Canadian stakeholder views on measuring and valuing child/adolescent health, focusing on EQ-5D-Y-3L versus EQ-5D-Y-5L, source of preferences, perspective, DCE versus TTO, and ethical issues when children complete valuation tasks (Abstract; Introduction). Fifteen one-hour Zoom interviews were conducted between May and September 2022. Participants came from HTA agencies, pediatric healthcare, academia, and pharmaceutical industry; 10/15 had economic-evaluation experience, 13/15 were parents, 11/15 were female, and 13/15 had health-utility familiarity. The sample was predetermined at 15 for information saturation and at least two participants per sector (Methods; Results, Tables 1–2).

## Instruments and elicitation issues

EQ-5D-Y is a child-friendly EQ-5D. The interview presented the 3-level and expanded 5-level versions and explained common child-valuation methods. DCE produces latent utility requiring anchoring; variants discussed were DCE with duration and DCE compared with immediate death. TTO was treated as a traditional adult method with possible cognitive burden (Introduction; Methods). Participants were asked about HTA guidelines, cognitive capability, and ethical concerns, including the “death” issue.

## Design and analysis

One interviewer used a pre-developed script covering demographics, Canadian HTA and preference-based instruments, instrument choice, preference source, task perspective, elicitation method, and (for DCE supporters) anchoring variant. Interviews were recorded and transcribed verbatim. Framework analysis followed six steps: familiarization, coding, developing the framework, indexing, charting in a participant-by-code Microsoft Excel matrix, and interpretation. Two researchers agreed the framework; stakeholder quotations were labelled A (academia), H (healthcare), G (government/HTA), or I (industry) (Methods, “Data analysis”).

## Results

- Eleven participants (73.3%) preferred EQ-5D-Y-5L, mainly because more response options may improve discrimination and change detection. Three preferred EQ-5D-Y-3L because five levels may be harder for children to distinguish; one had no strong preference (Results, “EQ-5D-Y-3L versus EQ-5D-Y-5L”, Table 2).
- Twelve (80.0%) supported children/adolescents as preference sources; two (13.3%) supported adults based on Canadian HTA guidance; one supported a mixture. Participants commonly recommended adolescents aged 12 or older as cognitively able to understand the tasks. The main rationale for direct inclusion was that children have perspectives adults may not represent (Results, “Source of health state preferences”).
- All 12 supporters of child sources preferred children/adolescents thinking about themselves. Other responses were own child’s friend/peer group, a general child without specification, or no strong preference (Results, “Perspective”).
- Eleven (73.3%) preferred DCE to TTO, describing DCE as straightforward, easier, and closer to reality. Among DCE supporters, six preferred DCE with duration and two DCE with death; three had no strong preference. Duration was viewed as more detailed, realistic, and potentially safer, while death was viewed by some as cognitively concrete and by others as romantic or triggering (Results, “Preference elicitation methods”).
- Ethical views were divided: eight reported no ethical concern and seven raised concern about discussing death with children, triggering fears, incomplete understanding, or need for parental/guardian permission (Table 2; Results).

## Interpretation and limitations

The authors conclude that most Canadian stakeholders supported direct adolescent preferences from the adolescent’s own perspective, with DCE considered more appropriate than TTO. Stakeholder engagement is presented as a way to address normative and local-context questions that an international protocol cannot resolve. The sample was highly educated health professionals/experts, not laypersons; two participants unfamiliar with health utilities found parts technical. Interviews focused on valuation methods, with limited time for policy implications. The paper recommends feasibility studies of direct adolescent EQ-5D-Y valuation in Canada (Discussion; Conclusions).
