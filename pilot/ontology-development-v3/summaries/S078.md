# Validity and responsiveness of the EQ-5D-5L, EQ-HWB and EQ-HWB-9 to measure health and wellbeing impact of heatwaves among older adults

## Aim, setting, and design

The study assessed whether subjective measures capture heatwave effects on health and wellbeing in older adults. Residents aged 60+ from two urban communities in Fuzhou, China, completed four face-to-face waves in 2023: pre-heatwave (10–25 May), heatwave 1 (26 June–7 July), heatwave 2 (3–22 August), and post-heatwave (15–28 October). China Meteorological Administration’s definition was used: daily maximum temperature ≥35°C for three consecutive days. Mean daily maximum temperatures were 31.70°C, 35.78°C, 36.61°C, and 28.93°C, respectively (Abstract; Methods—Study design and Data collection).

Recruitment required age ≥60, residence in the selected communities, Mandarin or Foochow communication, cognitive and physical ability to be interviewed, and consent. Analysis included 579, 510, 473, and 508 residents in the four waves. Most pre-heatwave respondents were 60–74 (67.5%), female (64.6%), married (80.5%), not employed (96.7%), and had secondary education or below (57.5%). Interviews were conducted in community recreation centres in the afternoon and responses entered on iPads. The study used simplified Chinese instruments and self-designed questions about heat effects, daily activities, sweating, adaptation, and feeling unwell (Methods—Participants and Data collection; Results—Table 1).

## Instruments and analysis

EQ-5D-5L includes mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort, and anxiety/depression, each with five levels, plus EQ VAS from 0 to 100; both refer to “today.” Index values used the Chinese value set, with UK crosswalk and England value sets in sensitivity analyses. Level sum scores (LSSs) were also calculated; higher EQ-5D-5L LSS indicates worse health. Experimental Simplified Chinese EQ-HWB v1.1 has 25 items across activity, autonomy, cognition, feelings and emotions, relationships, physical sensations, and self-identity, with a seven-day recall. EQ-HWB-9 was derived from nine EQ-HWB items and scored with the UK pilot value set. EQ-HWB and EQ-HWB-9 LSSs and subscales were also calculated (Methods—Measures).

The simplified Chinese Brief Inventory of Thriving (BIT) was also administered as a 10-item psychological wellbeing measure. Items used a 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree) scale; the BIT score was a level sum score, with higher scores indicating a stronger sense of psychological wellbeing, and no recall period was specified (Methods—Measures > BIT).

Ceiling/floor effects were flagged above 70% at item level or above 15% at instrument level. Known-groups validity compared excessive sweating and adaptation-to-weather groups with Cohen’s *d*. Responsiveness compared pre-heatwave with heatwave 1 and heatwave 2 with post-heatwave using standardized response means (SRM), with 1,000 bootstrap replications and 95% CIs. Chronic-condition subgroup analyses repeated the measures (Methods—Statistical analysis).

## Findings

At pre-heatwave, EQ-5D-5L item ceiling effects ranged from 58.2% for pain/discomfort to 94.3% for self-care. EQ-HWB item ceiling effects ranged from 32.3% for accepted to 94.0% for personal care. EQ-5D-5L had a 47.0% instrument-level ceiling and EQ-HWB-9 27.8%; no instrument or item floor effects occurred for EQ-5D-5L, EQ-HWB-9, or EQ-HWB (Results—Response distributions; Figure 1).

Mean EQ VAS, EQ-5D-5L index, and EQ-HWB-9 index were highest during heatwave 1: 80.05, 0.95, and 0.94, versus 77.17, 0.93, and 0.92 pre-heatwave; 78.84, 0.92, and 0.91 in heatwave 2; and 78.59, 0.93, and 0.92 post-heatwave. EQ-HWB LSS was lowest (better) during heatwave 1 at 34.07 versus 37.78 pre-heatwave, 37.53 heatwave 2, and 36.39 post-heatwave (Results—Table 2).

Excessive sweating was reported by 22.0% in heatwave 1 and 48.2% in heatwave 2; 64.3% felt adapted to weather in each heatwave. Those without excessive sweating or who felt adapted had better EQ VAS and EQ-5D-5L/EQ-HWB-9 index values and lower EQ-HWB LSS. Effect sizes were 0–0.52 for EQ VAS, 0.04–0.31 for EQ-5D-5L, 0.16–0.34 for EQ-HWB-9, and 0.28–0.45 for EQ-HWB. EQ-HWB pain/discomfort and psychosocial subscales showed small effects; activities and EQ-HWB-9 physical subscales were negligible (Results—Known-groups validity; Table 3).

Responsiveness was unexpected: residents whose self-perceived heat effect worsened during heatwave 1 reported better health and wellbeing. Responsiveness to improvement in self-perceived heat effects was negligible (SRM 0.01–0.19); overall SRM values ranged 0–0.44. Chinese, UK crosswalk, and England value sets gave consistent conclusions, as did chronic-condition subgroup analyses (Results—Responsiveness; Table 4).

## Interpretation and limits

All three measures had satisfactory known-groups validity but limited responsiveness. EQ-HWB, especially psychosocial and pain/discomfort subscales, was somewhat more responsive than five-item EQ-5D-5L. Possible explanations for improved scores during heatwave 1 include mild or short heatwaves, adaptation behaviours, post-lockdown COVID-19 effects, and timing of surveys. Centralised recruitment may exclude the most vulnerable, and attrition may be higher among unwell residents. The study used one city and two communities; EQ-HWB-9 was derived rather than independently administered. The authors suggest testing climate-related or psychosocial bolt-ons and other outcomes (Discussion; Conclusion).
