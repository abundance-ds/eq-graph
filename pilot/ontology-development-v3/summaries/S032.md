# How do Health State Values Differ When Respondents Consider Adults Versus Children Living in Those States? A Systematic Review

## Review aim and methods

This PRISMA systematic review examined how respondents, perspective and hypothetical age affect child HRQoL values. It asked whether willingness to trade length of life for quality differs, whether dimension importance differs, and which factors explain differences. Ovid MEDLINE, Ovid Embase and EconLit were searched from 1946 to 11 November 2024; the protocol was registered as PROSPERO CRD42023429906. Eligible English empirical quantitative or qualitative studies compared valuation from different respondent/perspective combinations. Included combinations used labels such as SA_A (adult self), OA_A (adult other adult), SC_A (adult imagining self as child), OC_A (adult proxy for child), SC_C (child self) and OC_C (child other child). [Source: Abstract; Methods—Search Sources and Search Terms; Eligibility Criteria; Table 2]

Studies were excluded when only one perspective was present, or when they were psychometric studies, single-instrument value-set studies without a perspective comparison, reviews, protocols, abstracts or papers without reported differences. One reviewer searched; 71% of titles/abstracts were independently screened by two reviewers and 91% of full texts were double screened. Three reviewers extracted study, sample, instrument, elicitation method, hypothetical age, statistical method, results and limitations. Quality used a checklist combining RETRIEVE, MMAT and author-developed items. [Source: Methods—Exclusion Criteria; Selection Process; Data Extraction; Quality Assessment]

## Evidence base and analysis

Twenty-four studies from 2004–2024 were included; 20 were predominantly quantitative, three qualitative and one mixed method. Seventeen studies (71%) used EQ-5D-Y-3L; other instruments included CHU9D, HUI2/3, PedsQL, EQ-5D-3L/5L, vignettes and other MAUIs. Nearly half (38%) used mixed valuation methods. Methods included TTO/cTTO, DCE, VAS, BWS, SG, self-report scoring and anchored approaches. Fifteen studies used age 10 as the hypothetical child. Thirteen studies compared adult self/other-adult with adult child/other-child perspectives; eight compared adults as child proxies with child respondents; two compared adult and child respondent groups directly; one combined further comparisons. [Source: Abstract; Results—Study Characteristics; Table 3]

A multi-level meta-regression analysed mean child-minus-adult differences. For OC_A–OA_A, TTO and level sum score (LSS) 6 were references. Predictors included perspective contrast, valuation method and health-state LSS. Quality reporting was high for 16–20 of 25 criteria in qualitative studies and 13–19 in quantitative studies; target sample-size justification was the main concern, and most qualitative studies did not report mode effects. [Source: Methods—Synthesis Method and Quality Assessment; Results—Reporting Quality; Table 4]

## Findings

Eight studies reported mean-value differences. TTO evidence was mixed for OC_A versus OA_A, but for most states OC_A values exceeded SA_A values: adults were less willing to trade life years for child HRQoL than for their own HRQoL. Differences and standard deviations were larger for severe states, including 33333, 33323 and 32223. VAS evidence was mixed: one study found higher child values, another small or absent differences, and another higher adult-self/other-adult values. DCE studies found higher child-perspective values for 33333 in two anchored comparisons; an unanchored/nonlinear DCE found more states worse than dead when adults valued for a 10-year-old. [Source: Results—Differences Reported; Figures 2–5]

Meta-regression found significantly larger child-minus-adult differences for LSS 8, 9, 12, 13, 14 and 15 than LSS 6; there was no clear monotonic pattern, but severe states generally produced larger differences. VAS showed a lower difference than TTO at p = 0.074; perspective contrast itself was not significant. The review reports that pain/discomfort was usually the most important dimension when adults valued states, while adolescent results were mixed: pain/discomfort or anxiety/depression could rank first. Self-care was usually least important; adolescents sometimes ranked mobility higher for themselves. [Source: Results—Overall Synthesis; Table 4; Table 5]

## Explanations, gaps and limitations

Possible explanations include reluctance to trade life years for children, emotional responses, attachment to an imagined child, difficulty imagining child ill health, heuristic or all-in-trading responses, parental status, respondent age/education, anchoring method, and the child’s hypothetical age. Parent versus non-parent effects were inconsistent. Qualitative studies described emotional difficulty and different beliefs about children’s coping or resilience. Quantitative studies rarely varied child age, so age effects remain uncertain; most used age 10. The review also cannot determine the optimal elicitation or anchoring approach, and generalisability beyond EQ-5D-Y-3L is limited. [Source: Results—Potential Reasons; Discussion; Conclusions]

The authors conclude that severe child health states can produce perspective-dependent values, with valuation method and severity influential. They call for better evidence on elicitation, anchoring, age-specific designs, parent/child involvement, and why parents and non-parents differ. The review reports EuroQol Research Foundation grant 348-PHD funding and author relationships with the EuroQol Group, including grants, travel reimbursement and board membership for some authors.
