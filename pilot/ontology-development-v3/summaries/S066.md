# Evaluating the content validity of the EQ-5D-Y for Chinese children and adolescents

## Aim, setting, and participants

The study assessed comprehensiveness, relevance, and comprehensibility of the simplified Chinese EQ-5D-Y-3L among healthy and diseased children and adolescents. It used the World Health Organization (WHO) physical, mental, and social health definition to guide open discussion. The study took place in Shanghai, China, and followed COREQ reporting. Ethical approval was IRB#2022-TYSQ-03–154 (Abstract; Methods).

Thirty participants aged 8–18 years took part: 15 healthy participants from local primary and secondary schools and 15 patients from Renji Hospital Children’s Medical Center. The disease group had three patients each with anemia, dermatopathy, asthma, congenital heart disease, and leukemia. Healthy participants were selected to match age and gender. Fourteen participants (46.7%) were female and 15 (50%) had rural Hukou; 16 participants were aged 11–15. There were no refusals or dropouts. Interviews were conducted face-to-face in Chinese by two trained interviewers, with guardians present for the disease group (Methods > Study design and participants; Results > Participants; Table 1).

## Instrument and procedures

Participants first answered open questions about the meaning of health, important health concerns, and potential health challenges. They then completed the simplified Chinese EQ-5D-Y-3L and discussed adequacy and possible improvements. The five dimensions were mobility, looking after myself, doing usual activities, having pain or discomfort, and feeling worried, sad, or unhappy. EQ VAS was also discussed. Interviews were audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim in simplified Chinese. No repeated interviews or transcript member checking took place, and data saturation was not pursued formally (Methods > Data collection procedures).

Two coders independently used framework analysis for health concepts. They applied the WHO themes Physical, Mental, and Social health. They removed concepts not describing current health status, including external events, financial effects, future health, organ or cellular function, and personality traits. A second adapted response-issue framework assessed understanding, interpretation, response-option selection, and relevance/comprehensiveness. Nvivo 14 supported data management (Methods > Data analysis).

## Findings on comprehensiveness

The team generated 126 codes, removed 39 non-health codes, retained 87 codes, and consolidated them into 50 concepts. The concepts were Physical health (*n*=16), Mental health (*n*=30), and Social health (*n*=4). All five EQ-5D-Y dimensions appeared in participant accounts, but Social health was frequently described and absent from the five dimensions (Results > Assessment of comprehensiveness; Fig. 1).

Physical health included pain and discomfort, mobility, self-care, and daily activity. Mental health included emotions, cognition, and autonomy, with worried, sad, and unhappy represented. Social health included good communication with others, social interaction, relationships with friends and classmates, and family relationships. Participants linked mobility to walking, self-care to daily living, and usual activities to school and recreation.

## Response issues and proposed changes

Participants generally understood the instrument. Four issues concerned unclear time frame or an unclear subject in question phrasing. Nine issues concerned dimension interpretation. Participants were unsure when a condition counted as a problem, whether “feeling worried, sad, or unhappy” required a specified reason, and how to answer when hospital routines prevented an activity. Examples in parentheses sometimes included activities that the participant could do and could not do, which caused uncertainty.

Suggestions included transportation under mobility; food preparation, housework, sport, social interaction, personal hygiene, homework, and self-discipline under looking after myself; socialising, sleeping, eating, exercise, hobbies, and recreation under usual activities; insomnia under pain or discomfort; and anxiety, low self-esteem, anger, fear, stress, despair, depression, and suicidal thoughts under feeling worried, sad, or unhappy. Two participants thought three response levels left a gap and suggested five levels. Suggested added dimensions included disease, social relationships, diet, sleep, and economic conditions. Three participants were unsure how to assign EQ VAS scores and found its many numbers confusing (Results > Assessment of relevance and comprehensibility).

## Interpretation and limitations

The authors conclude that EQ-5D-Y is relevant and generally comprehensible for Chinese children and adolescents but has limited coverage of Social health. They note overlap between looking after myself and doing usual activities and possible overlap between pain and mobility. The instrument is concise by design, so the study findings are evidence of content coverage rather than a recommendation to enlarge the instrument.

Potential leading bias came from using WHO themes as prompts. COVID-19 may have influenced health concepts. Formal saturation analysis was not done. The study focused on children with selected diseases and did not test psychometric properties or relative importance. The EQ VAS was not fully validated (Discussion; Conclusion).
