# H09 — Societal economic impact of vision impairment in Trinidad and Tobago

## 1. Identity and study type

- **Title:** *The societal economic impact of vision impairment in adults 40 years and above: findings from the National Eye Survey of Trinidad and Tobago* (article title, source title).
- **DOI:** `10.1038/s41433-023-02860-x`; source project folder `341-RA`.
- **Project-link status:** unverified candidates `341-RA` and `357-RA`. The article does not state that EuroQol supported this study.
- **Study type:** cost-of-illness study; population-based observational economic-burden study. The paper does not evaluate an intervention or apply an EQ utility instrument.
- **Source verification:** article file SHA-256 `6a27dd1b66faeb7e4095b044a2f9b8df67dc165bf02ff8811dbcc59b536643d6`; byte count `71413` (manifest and file match).

## 2. Population and samples

- **Target population:** adults aged 40 years and above in Trinidad and Tobago in 2014, for estimates of presenting vision impairment (VI) involving the better-seeing eye (Introduction; Methods).
- **Recruitment frame:** the National Eye Survey of Trinidad and Tobago (NESTT, 2014) sampled 9,913 eligible people aged ≥5 years in 3,556 households (95.9% coverage) within 120 clusters, using multi-stage random cluster sampling with probability-proportional-to-size methods. This study's analysis concerned adults aged ≥40 years; that sample contained 4,263 people (Methods, “Participants, recruitment and procedure”).
- **Measured and analytic samples:** presenting visual acuity was measured in 3,589 responders (84.2%) aged ≥40. Medical and ophthalmic utilisation data were available for 2,792/4,263 (65.5%); socioeconomic questionnaire data for 2,516/4,263 (59.0%). Direct unit costs came from a contemporaneous national eyecare system survey (Methods; Results).
- **Differential economic-impact sample:** 2,663 responders contributed to the analysis of employment, income, education, and related effects by vision level (Results, “Differential economic impacts by vision level”).
- **VI subgroups:** the cost analysis included 161/222 participants with mild VI, 158/215 with moderate or severe VI, and 13/31 who were blind in the better-seeing eye. The estimated 2014 adult population aged ≥40 was 541,894 (Results; Methods, “Cases of vision impairment”).
- **Demographic context:** NESTT age-≥40 participants had mean age 57.1 (SD 11.8) years and 54.3% female in the Methods description. The Abstract reports mean age 58.4 (SD 11.8, range 40–103) and 56.3% female for the cost-data participants. This is a source-level sample-description discrepancy.

## 3. Research concepts

- Societal economic impact and cost of vision impairment; direct medical and non-medical costs; indirect productivity loss; informal-care opportunity cost; loss of wellbeing; access to eyecare and low-vision support; and distribution of burden by vision level, employment, education, literacy, and income (Introduction; Methods; Results).
- The analysis uses a societal perspective. Cost bearers include affected people, family and friends providing informal care, Government, the healthcare system, and employers (Methods, “Classification of economic impact”).

## 4. Instruments and administration

- **Visual-acuity assessment:** uniocular presenting distance and binocular presenting near visual acuity were measured outside the household using a standardised measurement protocol (Methods, “Participants, recruitment and procedure”). The paper does not name an EQ-5D instrument.
- **Questionnaires:** socioeconomic questionnaire; NESTT medical and ophthalmic questionnaires; and a low-vision questionnaire for people with best-corrected visual acuity worse than 6/18 in the better-seeing eye. The study recorded eye-care use, medications, surgery/laser history, spectacles or contact lenses, low-vision service access, low-vision aids, employment, income category, and informal care (Methods, “Socioeconomic variables”; Table 1).
- **Administration:** adults received a comprehensive clinic-based assessment. Questionnaires were administered with Epi Info version 3.5.4. Respondents recalled most service use and costs over the past 12 months; topical ophthalmic medication use was recorded for the past 3 months. Interaction mode and instrument language were not reported (Methods).
- **External sources:** a contemporaneous national eyecare health-system survey supplied provider tariffs and unit costs; this was not a participant instrument (Methods; Results).

## 5. Methods, protocol, and task design

- **Research methods:** population-based national eye survey; bottom-up cost and utilisation estimation; prevalence-to-population extrapolation; contemporaneous eyecare-system survey; and deterministic sensitivity analysis.
- **Cost categories:** direct medical (services, surgery, medicines, laser, other treatment); direct non-medical (refractive correction, low-vision aids, transport); indirect (VI productivity loss, part-time work, sick leave, informal care); and intangible effects (DALYs/lost wellbeing). Transfer payments and dead-weight losses were considered but excluded from final estimates (Methods, Table 1).
- Cases in each vision category were estimated by applying adjusted prevalence to the 2014 mid-year population aged ≥40. YLD was calculated from prevalent cases and disability weights; YLL was set to zero because VI was assumed not to cause premature mortality (Methods, “Cases…”; “Definition and estimation of intangible effects”).
- The study used 2014 Trinidad & Tobago dollars and UK sterling equivalent. Unit costs were inflated to 2014 values and converted using the World Bank GDP deflator and official exchange rate (Methods, “Conversion of unit costs”).
- **Named protocol/guideline:** Cost of Vision Loss Consensus Guidelines (2010) informed cost categories; no named participant task protocol was reported.

## 6. Analysis and statistical models

- Statistical analysis used Stata Statistical Software Release 13.1. Crude means/proportions were adjusted with the `svy` command suite for island/cluster survey design, selection probability, variable cluster response rate, post-stratification to the 2011 Census, and finite-population corrections (Methods, “Statistical methods”).
- **Statistical model:** multi-level mixed-effects logistic regression (`melogit`), adjusted for age, sex, and study design, examined odds of presenting VI, use of eye-care services, health insurance, and exclusive public-sector eye-care use by vision category (Methods).
- **Other analyses:** cost aggregation from prevalence, utilisation, and unit costs; human-capital approach for productivity loss; opportunity-cost method for informal care; DALY calculation; and one-way deterministic sensitivity analysis. Parameters were varied within 95% confidence intervals or by ±50% when primary data were unavailable or subgroup-only (Methods, “Sensitivity analysis”).
- Sensitivity analysis varied disability weights and multiple cost parameters. The source gives alternative wellbeing estimates but does not define these as competing fitted statistical models (Results, “Sensitivity analyses”).

## 7. Products

- **Produced product:** a 2014 national estimate of the societal economic cost of vision impairment in adults ≥40 years, including and excluding lost wellbeing (Results, “Total economic cost…”).
- **Research outputs:** population estimates of distance and near VI, cost-category breakdowns, employment and access descriptions, and baseline information to support cost-effectiveness and policy planning (Results; Conclusion).
- The paper does not produce an EQ-5D value set or other instrument value set. It reports DALYs and discusses a previously reported QALY impact, but QALY is not the instrument used to generate the main cost estimate (Methods, Table 1; Limitations).

## 8. Outcomes or measurement properties

- Primary outcomes: total societal cost; direct medical, direct non-medical, indirect, and intangible cost components; per-capita cost; and estimated cases of distance and near VI (Abstract; Results; Table 2).
- Secondary outcomes: employment, household-income category, educational attainment, literacy, eyecare use, insurance, out-of-pocket expenditure, low-vision support, and informal-care needs by vision level (Results).
- DALY was the loss-of-wellbeing outcome. WHO disability weights used were 0.047 for near VI, 0.005 for mild distance VI, 0.089 for moderate distance VI, 0.314 for severe distance VI, and 0.338 for blindness (Methods, “Definition and estimation of intangible effects”).

## 9. Principal findings and interpretation

- Total 2014 societal cost of VI was TT$3,842,324,655 (UK£365,650,241); lost wellbeing was 73.3%. Excluding intangible effects, cost was TT$1,025,045,399, with indirect 70.5%, direct medical 17.9%, and direct non-medical 11.6% (Abstract; Results; Table 2).
- Estimated per-capita cost for the adult population was £674.76 including lost wellbeing and £180.01 excluding it. Affected people and families bore 97.6% of total cost (Results; Discussion).
- The study estimated 64,431 cases of distance VI (95% CI 54,623–74,077), of which 86.1% (55,481; 95% CI 53,401–57,221) were potentially avoidable. It also estimated 120,842 avoidable cases of near VI from uncorrected presbyopia (Results, “Estimation of cases…”).
- Greater VI was associated with lower employment and household income. Employment ranged from 73.2% for normal distance and near vision to 41.4% for moderate or severe VI, with no employment among five blind participants (Results, “Differential economic impacts…”).
- The authors interpret the findings as a baseline for cost-effectiveness and policy decisions. They identify possible investment in low-vision aids, workplace enablement, diabetic-retinopathy screening, spectacles, cataract surgery, and eye-care access (Discussion; Conclusion).

## 10. Limitations and source issues

- The study includes people aged ≥40 only, excludes the institutionalised population, and does not include long-term-care placement costs. Costs and utilisation may have non-response bias, especially for blind or housebound people (Discussion).
- Recall bias may affect reported 12-month service use and costs. The analysis excludes transfer payments, dead-weight losses, and opportunity cost of being a carer; several downstream health costs were not measured (Discussion).
- Individual income was not collected, so productivity loss used a human-capital assumption and median income. Low-vision-aid prices were not determined in Trinidad and Tobago and were taken from external sources adjusted to 2014 values (Methods).
- The source reports a discrepancy between the Abstract’s cost-data demographics (mean age 58.4; 56.3% female) and the Methods’ NESTT age-≥40 description (mean age 57.1; 54.3% female).
- The holdout selection note was corrected during validation because this article does not report EQ-5D-5L administration, scoring, or results. It reports visual-acuity measures, questionnaires, DALYs, and a discussion of QALYs. No EQ-5D-5L fact was added.
- The Funding section names the Trinidad and Tobago Ministry of Health and Fight for Sight. EuroQol grants `341-RA` and `357-RA` occur only in one author's competing-interest statement. This does not verify that EuroQol supported the paper. An authoritative project-output record is required before portfolio inclusion (Funding; Competing interests).
