# H11 — Cultural values and self-reported health

## 1. Identity and study type

- **Title:** *Cultural values: can they explain self-reported health?*
- **DOI:** `10.1007/s11136-017-1512-x`; EuroQol project `2015150`.
- **Research purpose:** study cultural influences on self-reported health within and between countries.
- **Study design:** secondary cross-national observational analysis of repeated World Values Survey and European Values Survey data, with individual- and country-level regression analyses.
- **EQ instrument use:** none reported.
- **Funding link:** accepted. The Acknowledgements state that EuroQol funded the study under project `2015150`.
- **Source verification:** article SHA-256 `b20c61de79db4740a9852bd43f96acff500f7f5a383929ef915346ef7ce4e77f`; 44,907 bytes.

## 2. Population and samples

- **Target population:** respondents represented in the World Values Survey and European Values Survey across 51 countries; the paper studies individual and country differences rather than one clinical population (Methods—Measures; Results).
- **Source dataset:** the Integrated Values Survey combined longitudinal WVS and EVS data and contained 506,268 unique respondents from almost 100 countries before study exclusions (Methods—Measures; Results).
- **Within-country analytic sample:** 157,583 respondents from 51 countries after removal of waves with missing macroeconomic data, unasked questions, and records with missing study variables (Results).
- **Mixed-model analytic sample:** at least 45 countries and 100,590 respondents because additional macroeconomic data were missing (Results; Table 1).
- **Between-country analysis:** 51 country-level observations (Results; Table 2).
- Most country-wave samples contained more than 1,000 respondents and used representative survey sampling. The article does not give one age range for the pooled sample (Methods—Measures; Discussion—Limitations and strengths).

## 4. Instruments and administration

- **Health measure:** one World Values Survey self-reported-health item with categories from `Very good` to `Very poor`; the analysis reversed it to a score from 5 for very good to 1 for very poor (Methods—Measures).
- **Cultural measures:** two continuous WVS dimensions derived from ten items: `traditional versus rational/secular` and `survival versus self-expression` (Methods—Measures; Figure 1).
- **Other measures:** age, gender, education, income, subjective social class, and World Bank macroeconomic variables for wealth, health expenditure, life expectancy, and out-of-pocket expenditure (Methods—Measures).
- **Administration:** secondary analysis of WVS and EVS survey data. The article does not report one common interaction mode, channel, or language across countries.
- **EQ instrument:** none administered, scored, or analyzed.

## 5. Methods, protocol, and task design

- The authors merged WVS and EVS longitudinal data under the WVS integration protocol and linked country-level World Bank data (Methods—Measures).
- The study tested the association between cultural values and self-reported health separately within countries and between countries to avoid an ecological inference based only on country means (Methods—Rationale).
- Socio-demographic and macroeconomic variables were added as control variables. Continuous control variables were centered (Methods—Analyses).
- The analysis collapsed survey waves because the research question concerned country differences rather than change over time (Discussion—Limitations and strengths).

## 6. Analysis and statistical models

- **Models 1 and 2:** separate linear regressions for each country. Self-reported health was the dependent variable; the two cultural dimensions were predictors. Model 2 added socio-demographic controls (Methods—Analyses).
- **Models 3 to 5:** multilevel mixed-effects models with random country intercepts and random slopes for both cultural dimensions. Model 4 added socio-demographic controls and model 5 added macroeconomic controls (Methods—Analyses; Table 1).
- **Model 6:** country-level linear regression of mean self-reported health on country cultural values and controls (Methods—Analyses; Table 2).
- The source notes that a conditional probit model would have been more appropriate for the categorical outcome, but it used linear regression to simplify comparison and interpretation (Discussion—Limitations and strengths).

## 7. Products

- The study produces cross-national evidence and interpretation guidance for self-reported health. It does not produce an EQ instrument, value set, mapping function, or scoring product (Discussion; Conclusion).
- The merged analytic dataset is not presented as a new reusable public research product.

## 8. Outcomes or measurement properties

- **Primary outcome:** self-reported health on the reversed five-category WVS scale.
- **Main relationships:** association of self-reported health with the two cultural-value dimensions within individuals and between countries.
- **Model outcomes:** explained variance, fixed effects, random slope and intercept variation, and country-level regression effects.

## 9. Principal findings and interpretation

- Survival/self-expression values were positively associated with self-reported health. Traditional/rational-secular values were negatively associated with self-reported health, contrary to the authors' directional hypothesis (Abstract—Results and Discussion; Results; Discussion).
- The direction of the associations was similar within countries and between countries. This supports cross-country interpretation without assuming an association that exists only at the ecological level (Discussion—Ecological fallacy).
- Cultural values explained between 4% and 17% of within-country variation. Adding socio-demographic and macroeconomic controls did not materially change the cultural-value associations (Abstract—Results; Discussion).
- At the country level, the cultural variables could correspond to a change of about 0.75 on the five-point self-reported-health scale across their observed range. Model 6 reported an R-squared of 0.3352 (Results; Table 2).
- Random slopes and intercepts differed across countries. The authors conclude that cultural values should be considered when interpreting self-reported health between countries, while other unmeasured country differences remain (Results; Discussion; Conclusion).

## 10. Limitations and source issues

- Missing macroeconomic data, unasked survey questions, and missing individual values reduced the source data from about 506,000 to 157,583 respondents for within-country analyses and 100,590 for mixed models (Discussion—Limitations and strengths).
- Survey waves were collapsed, so the study assumes that the cultural-value associations did not change over time (Discussion—Limitations and strengths).
- Cultural subgroups within countries can differ from country aggregates, but the analysis could not represent those subgroup relationships (Discussion—Limitations and strengths).
- The source data did not contain information on response styles, although response styles can affect international self-reported-health comparisons (Discussion—Limitations and strengths).
- Linear regression treated the categorical health outcome as continuous. The authors state that conditional probit would have been more appropriate but less comparable and interpretable (Discussion—Limitations and strengths).
- The explanations proposed for the unexpected negative rational/secular association are post hoc. The authors call for further research and do not claim a firm mechanism (Discussion—Associations between cultural values and SRH).
