Background The 9-item EQ Health and Wellbeing (EQ-HWB-9) is a generic preference-based measure designed for assessing health and wellbeing in patients, social care users and carers. Cancer patients and their carers often experience health and socioeconomic challenges (e.g., financial burden, fears of death), making the EQ-HWB-9 particularly useful for this population. Few studies have examined its psychometric performance in cancer populations. This study, therefore, aimed to evaluate the psychometric properties of EQ-HWB-9 in gastric, pancreatic and colorectal cancer patients, separately. Methods Patients were recruited from a tertiary hospital in Shanghai, China, from Jan to Dec 2025. Consenting patients assessed their own health using experimental EQ-HWB-9 (2022) version, EQ-5D-5L, SF-6Dv2, PROMIS-10, FACT-G (from which the FACT-8D was extracted), and EORTC QLQ-C30 (from which the QLU-C10D was extracted) at two subsequent visits. Clinical data was extracted from medical records. Convergent validity, known-group validity, and responsiveness were assessed for item, index score and level sum scores (LSS). EQ-HWB-9 index scores were computed using an experimental UK value set. Results Analysis included 164 gastric, 135 pancreatic, and 203 colorectal cancer patients. ECOG=1 was the most common performance status (82-84%), and Stage-IV cancer predominated (59-64%) across three cancers. The EQ-HWB-9 demonstrated sufficient construct validity, fulfilling 159 out of 180 hypotheses (88%). The index and LSS of EQ-HWB-9 showed strong correlations with those of EQ-5D-5L, SF-6Dv2, FACT-8D and QLU-C10D across three cancer types (rs range: 0.60–0.80), except for moderate correlations with those of FACT-8D in colorectal cancer (rs: 0.46 and 0.58 for index and LSS, respectively). At the item level, moderate to strong correlations were observed for 52 out of 60 hypothesized item pairs (rs: 0.31-0.91). The EQ-HWB-9 Index and LSS demonstrated strong known-group validity by ECOG status (0–1 vs 2–5) across three cancer types (Cohen’s d: 0.74–1.49). Known-group validity by cancer stage (0–III vs IV) was limited, with effect size small to moderate in gastric cancer (d: 0.49, 0.53 for index, LSS), small in pancreatic cancer (d:  0.29, 0.26), negligible in colorectal cancer (d: -0.09, 0.10). Both scores differentiated patients with and without financial burden, showing small to moderate effect size (d: 0.38–0.55).  Responsiveness assessment included 91 gastric, 82 pancreatic, and 115 colorectal cancer patients. Responsiveness varied by cancer type. Among anchor-defined improved patients, SRM values were larger for pancreatic cancer (range: 0.24-0.59) than for colorectal cancer (0.12-0.15) and gastric cancer (0.07-0.28). Among anchor-defined deteriorated patients, SRM values indicated small to moderate responsiveness, which were slightly larger in gastric cancer (0.30-0.71) compared with pancreatic (0.21-0.70) and colorectal cancer (0.30-0.64). Responsiveness patterns of items across cancer types align with those observed for index and LSS. “Daily activities” demonstrated the largest responsiveness, while ‘exhaustion’, ‘mood’, ‘anxiety’, and ‘control’ showed small-to-moderate effects; ‘cognition’ and ‘pain’ were least responsive. Conclusions The EQ-HWB-9 demonstrated sufficient construct validity and responsiveness, with performance varying across gastric, pancreatic, and colorectal cancers. This is the first study to evaluate the psychometric properties of EQ-HWB-9 by individual cancer type. Findings highlight that treating cancer types as a single, homogeneous category in validation studies may obscure clinically relevant heterogeneity and underscore the need for cancer-type-specific psychometric evaluation.
