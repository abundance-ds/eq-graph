Background: While Asian HTA agencies accept or recommend EQ-5D instruments, they are continuously exploring better ways of measuring health-related quality of life. This study addresses a critical knowledge gap by engaging directly with Asian HTA practitioners and researchers to understand their views on EuroQol instruments.Methods: We conducted in-depth qualitative interviews with 22 HTA practitioners and researchers across 10 Asian countries (China n=4, India n=1, Indonesia n=2, Japan n=2, Malaysia n=3, South Korea n=2, Thailand n=2, Taiwan n=2, Singapore n=2, Philippines n=2). Interview transcripts were systematically coded using a comprehensive 18-category framework covering current use, strengths/weaknesses, implementation challenges, cultural considerations, future recommendations, evidence gaps, and collaboration expectations.Findings: EQ-5D-5L has achieved strong institutional adoption (mandated or de facto standard in 90% of agencies), but significant implementation gaps prevent optimal utilisation. The "Philippines paradox" exemplifies this: a locally-developed value set exists but remains underused due to capacity constraints. Three critical barriers emerged: (1) fundamental capacity gaps in basic methodologies blocking QoL expertise development, (2) awareness deficits regarding value sets, bolt-ons, and registration processes, and (3) absence of utility infrastructure (databases, guidelines, quality assurance protocols). Cultural factors may significantly impact measurement validity—mental health stigma limits EQ-HWB adoption, toxic positivity/religious coping inflates wellbeing scores, and complex family structures complicate pediatric proxy validity. Priority needs cluster around: ecosystem-wide training (HTA staff + academics + external researchers), regional LMIC utility databases, EQ-5D-Y value sets (highest priority across all countries), and methodological guidance on transferability/proxy use for adaptive HTA contexts.Implications: EQ-5D is institutionally entrenched but operationally underutilised. The highest ROI interventions are: (1) comprehensive training programmes targeting entire research ecosystems, not just HTA agencies, (2) development of maintained utility databases/registries aligned with national HTA priorities, (3) prioritisation of EQ-5D-Y value sets given universal paediatric gaps, and (4) creation of methodological guidance for adaptive HTA contexts where primary data collection is infeasible. Regional collaboration through HTAsiaLink offers scalability, and individual country partnerships (particularly collaboration with universities) could be more effective due to legal/institutional constraints.
