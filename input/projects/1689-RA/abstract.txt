Eliciting health state utilities with the time trade-off (TTO) method relies on assessing the relative importance of health-related quality of life (HRQoL) versus longevity. Adults typically assign higher utilities when considering a hypothetical child than when considering their own HRQoL. We aimed to decompose this effect by comparing the importance attached to survival and to HRQoL in adolescence versus adulthood.We developed and used a method that employs intrapersonal choices between lotteries involving health problems that differ in age of onset or probability of occurrence, thereby avoiding ethical concerns about interpersonal comparisons. Respondents chose from the perspective of a hypothetical 10-year-old and 20-year-old facing HRQoL worsening or death. They also completed TTO tasks from both perspectives, as well as tasks intended to measure probability weighting.We piloted the method in a convenience sample of 100 respondents. Our results suggest that the respondents value the nearest 10 years more than subsequent years; the implied annual discount rates range from 0.26% to 9.49%, depending on the health problem. Crucially, this increase of importance does not vary by perspective. Thus, we find no evidence that hypothetical adolescents receive greater weight on either HRQoL or survival than hypothetical adults. TTO results are consistent with these findings. Probability-weighting tasks yielded ambiguous patterns and were excluded from the main analysis.We conclude that higher TTO utilities when adults value a hypothetical child’s health versus their own are unlikely to be driven by adolescent years receiving special value. Instead this effect may be driven by the change from self- to other-perspective.
