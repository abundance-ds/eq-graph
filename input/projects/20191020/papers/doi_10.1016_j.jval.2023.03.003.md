# Using Time Trade-Off Values to Estimate EQ-5D-Y Value Sets: An Exploratory Study

Contents lists available at sciencedirect.com Journal homepage: www.elsevier.com/locate/jval

#### Brief Report

Zhihao Yang, PhD, Jie Jiang, PhD, Pei Wang, PhD, Jing Wu, PhD, Yu Fang, PhD, Da Feng, PhD, Xiaoyu Xi, PhD, Nan Luo, PhD

#### A B S T R A C T

Objectives: The published international EQ-5D-Y-3L valuation protocol does not recommend the composite time trade-off (cTTO) method as the primary valuation method because of 2 major concerns. First, the cTTO method was shown to generate high values. Second, the cTTO method is not as feasible for valuing children’s health as other established methods. This study aimed to explore the feasibility of using cTTO values alone to estimate EQ-5D-Y-3L value sets.

Methods: We analyzed the cTTO data derived from the recently completed Chinese EQ-5D-Y-3L valuation study in which a total of 28 health states were valued. We assessed the feasibility of the cTTO tasks in terms of survey completion time and participant-reported difficulty of understanding the task, differentiating the health states, and deciding the answer. We also examined the data distribution characteristics and modeled the data using different models.

Results: In total, 418 participants completed the cTTO interview. On average, participants took approximately 35.70 minutes (SD 12.42) to complete the interview and made 13.21 moves (SD 9.00) in the cTTO tasks. There were 74.16%, 59.33%, and 11.48% of participants indicated that it was easy to understand the cTTO tasks, easy to differentiate between the health states, and difficult to decide on an indifference point, respectively. The data distribution was smooth, and a random-effects model performed the best in terms of coefficient significance, monotonicity, and predictive accuracy.

Conclusion: Our finding suggests that estimating EQ-5D-Y-3L value sets using cTTO data alone is feasible and therefore could be considered as an option in future valuation studies for EQ-5D-Y-3L.

Keywords: composite time trade-off, EQ-5D-Y-3L, feasibility, value set.

VALUE HEALTH. 2023; 26(7):968–973

### Introduction

Although remarkable progress in health state valuation has been made over the past decade,1,2 there is still further work to be done on the valuation of child health states and subsequent value set development. To address various methodological questions for valuing children’s health, the EuroQol Group conducted a program of methodological research3-5 and developed the first international EQ-5D-Y-3L valuation protocol.6 This protocol aims to guide the development of value sets for child health states defined by EQ-5D-Y-3L.3 Several countries have since established their national value sets following this protocol.7-9

The international EQ-5D-Y-3L valuation protocol recommends using discrete choice experiment (DCE) data as the primary preference data to estimate the relative importance of the 5 health dimensions and composite time trade-off (cTTO) data to anchor the estimated DCE values onto the quality-adjusted lifeyear (QALY) scale.6 This is in contrast to the protocol for valuing adult 5-level version of EQ-5D (EQ-5D-5L) health states, where cTTO is the recommended valuation method.1,10 The reason for downgrading the role of the cTTO method in the EQ-5D-Y-3L

valuation protocol was twofold. First, it is more feasible and cost-efficient to collect DCE data because it can be conducted online without assistance from trained interviewers. More importantly, a multicountry study found that adult respondents tended to avoid trading children’s life for better health,3 indicating that cTTO values might not be able to identify the relative importance of the EQ-5D-Y-3L health dimensions.6 The authors of that study concluded that further investigation is needed to determine the suitability of using cTTO to value child health states.

Although high cTTO values were observed in several EQ-5D-Y- 3L valuation studies,7,9,11 a Slovenian study8 observed negative mean cTTO values for 4 of 10 EQ-5D-Y-3L states, with the value for state “33333” (−0.691) being lower than all the EQ-5D-3L values for the country.12 This finding challenges the assumption that the cTTO method might fail to identify the relative importance of the different health dimensions or levels of child health states. Nevertheless, most published EQ-5D-Y-3L studies, including the Slovenian study, valued only 10 states using the cTTO method, which does not allow for the estimation of a cTTO-only value set to assess this assumption.13

This study aimed to further explore the cTTO valuation of EQ- 5D-Y-3L health states by using a cTTO design larger than the one used in published studies. We hypothesized that the cTTO method is feasible for valuing EQ-5D-Y-3L health states and that EQ-5D-Y- 3L value sets can be estimated using cTTO values alone.

### Methods

Our study used the cTTO data collected from the EQ-5D-Y-3L valuation study conducted in China,14 which followed a modified international EQ-5D-Y-3L valuation protocol.6 In that study, a total of 28 health states were valued using the cTTO method, which allows us to explore different ways of modeling the cTTO data. The study was approved by the Jinan University research ethics committee (approval number JNUKY-2021-002) and data collection was conducted between November 2019 and June 2020. The study design for the cTTO component is described below.

#### Health State Selection

The 28 EQ-5D-Y-3L health states selected for valuation included the 10 health states from the international EQ-5D-Y-3L valuation protocol. The remaining 18 health states were determined using an orthogonal design.13 The health states were divided into 3 blocks of 10 states, with state “33333” being included in every block. A sample of 400 respondents each valued one block of health states, which allows the estimation of a cTTO value set.13,15

#### Sampling and Recruitment Strategy

A representative general Chinese population sample was recruited using a quota sampling method. Quotas for sex, age, education attainment, and hukou (rural or urban, a surrogate of socioeconomic status) were set based on the sixth National Population Census of China16 and applied to each of the 8 provinces or cities selected for participant recruitment, including Tianjin (North China), Shanghai, Jiangsu (East China), Guangdong (South China), Hubei (Central China), Guizhou, Chongqing, and Shanxi (West China). A quota of 50 participants was allocated to each recruitment city or province. A local team was assembled and tasked to be responsible for both participant recruitment and data collection. Participants were contacted and recruited from selected public locations or from the interviewers’ social networks. Participants must (1) meet predetermined quotas, (2) be Chinese and have lived in China for at least 5 years before the interview, and (3) be well enough to provide an informed consent.

#### Data Collection Procedures

All participants were invited to a face-to-face, one-on-one computer-assisted personal interview. All interviews adhered to the EuroQol-Valuation Technology protocol.1 After providing a brief introduction to the study, trained interviewers went through some warm-up questions, demonstration, and some practice of cTTO questions and then guided the participants to complete the valuation of 10 EQ-5D-Y-3L health states. In each cTTO task, participants were asked to imagine that an EQ-5D-Y-3L health state was experienced by a 10-year-old child and to state their preferences between 2 hypothetical lives (ie, life A and life B). Detailed descriptions of the cTTO elicitation procedures can be found in the EuroQol protocol for time trade-off valuation of health outcomes.10 Briefly, for the health states that a participant considered to be “better-than-dead,” the indifference point between life A (x years in full health followed by death) and life B (10 years in the health state being valued followed by death) was identified; for

“worse-than-dead” states, the indifference point between life A (x years in full health followed by death) and life B (10 years in full health followed by 10 years in the health state being valued and then death) was identified. The cTTO value was x/10 and (x − 10)/ 10 for the “better-than-dead” and “worse-than-dead” health states, respectively. Upon completion of the valuation tasks, participants were asked to report their experience of the valuation tasks by indicating their levels of agreement or disagreement for the following statements: (1) “I found it easy to understand the question,” (2) “I found it easy to tell the difference between the 2 health states,” and (3) “I found it difficult to make my decision.” Response options ranged from “agree” to “disagree.” Characteristics of the “10-year-old child” participants had in mind when completing the cTTO tasks were elicited. Demographics and health characteristics of the participants were also recorded.

In each study city or province, a graduate student from a local university was recruited and trained to be the interviewer. Following the EuroQol-Valuation Technology protocol, all 8 interviewers were trained in a 2-day workshop and their compliance to the interview protocol was monitored throughout the entire data collection period using a quality control (QC) procedure.17

#### Data Analysis and Model Evaluation

We assessed the feasibility of the cTTO tasks in terms of survey completion time and participant-reported difficulty in terms of (1) self-reported understanding of the task, (2) self-reported ease of differentiating the health states, and (3) self-reported difficulty in deciding on an indifference point when completing the cTTO task. We compared the results with those from the Chinese EQ-5D-5L study18 where the same cTTO tasks were conducted in a general population sample for similar health states, except that participants were told to imagine themselves, rather than a 10-year-old experiencing the hypothetical EQ-5D health states.

We examined the distribution of the cTTO data using a histogram and reported the observed health state values. Next, we modeled the effects of health state characteristics on the disutility (ie, 1 − cTTO value) using 2 model specifications (ie, main effects only and main effects plus a “All 3” term [A3]). A3 takes the value of 1 for state “33333” and 0 for all other states. It was used to account for the extra utility decrement of state “33333.” For each model specification, we estimated 3 different models: (1) ordinary least square (OLS) as the base model, (2) a heteroscedastic model, and (3) a random-effects intercept model. We evaluated the models using 3 criteria: (1) monotonicity and logical consistency of the predictions, (2) significance and uncertainty of the estimated coefficients, and (3) prediction accuracy, measured using the mean absolute errors derived from the leave-one-state-out cross-validation analysis.19

### Results

The demographic characteristics of the participants are presented in Table 1. In total, 418 participants completed the cTTO interviews. The mean age of the participants was 42.40 years (SD 14.62), with the majority being female (51.67%) and urban residents (64.35%) and completed either high school or higher education (66.91%). Our sample was slightly over-represented by females and those who completed higher education compared with the general population of China.16

### Discussion

Our study demonstrated that it is feasible for members of the general public to value EQ-5D-Y-3L health states using the cTTO method. The data distribution was largely smooth without strong signs of data clustering or gaps. Most importantly, using the Chinese EQ-5D-5L valuation study18 as a reference, the EQ-5D-Y-3L cTTO tasks showed higher feasibility in terms of self-reported difficulties in task completion by participants. The interview time was slightly longer for the EQ-5D-Y-3L valuation tasks, which could be attributed to the higher QC standards implemented in the study.1,17 One QC indicator was whether the time for completing a cTTO task passed a minimum threshold, which the interviewers were aware of, and therefore possibly deliberately slowed down the interview pace by giving respondents more time to think or prompted them more carefully. In comparison, no such QC process was implemented in the adult EQ-5D-5L valuation study. Another benefit of implementing a QC process is that the interviewer effect was minimized in this study, given that all interviewers had similar data distributions (see Appendix 4 found at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jval.2023.03.003). Furthermore, we found only one nontrader among our participants, further suggesting the suitability of using the cTTO method to value EQ-5D-Y-3L health states.

It should be noted that jointly using DCE data might not increase the utility decrements given that the scale range is mainly determined by the cTTO data.6 A possible explanation of the higher EQ-5D-Y-3L values observed is that the general public values children’s life more than adults’ life. The implication of this is that lifesaving interventions for children are more likely to be deemed cost-effective when EQ-5D-Y-3L values are used to estimate QALYs in economic evaluations.

Both TTO and DCE methods require respondents to make trade-offs and choices between alternatives, which mimics decision making in reality. DCE tasks ask respondents to trade between different health dimensions. Comparatively, the TTO method has the advantage of reflecting the QALY concept, given that it forces trade-off between quantity and quality of life. Moreover, the TTO method is more aligned with real-world medical decision making where survival and quality of life are the 2 primary outcomes.21 Thus far, all the published EQ-5D-Y-3L valuation studies7-9,22 have exhibited logically consistent and reasonable cTTO distributions, suggesting that cTTO-only value sets are possible if sufficient health states had been valued in those studies. For example, the Japanese study valued 26 states (18 from an orthogonal array 1 8 handpicked states) using the cTTO method and estimated 3 cTTO models with consistent and significant coefficients.7 Kreimeier et al23,24 valued 17 states used in previous EQ-5D-3L valuation studies. Those data should also be sufficient for estimating a value set.3 More importantly, estimating a cTTO-only value set avoids the complex modeling analysis that is needed when both cTTO and DCE data are used. Therefore, cTTO-only value sets could be considered as an option in future EQ-5D-Y-3L valuation studies when the cTTO data are sufficient.

### Conclusion

It is feasible to establish an EQ-5D-Y-3L value set using cTTO data alone. Given that the best strategy for combining the cTTO and DCE data to determine health state utilities is still unknown, using only cTTO data to establish EQ-5D-Y-3L value sets may be an option for future valuation studies.
