BackgroundThe EQ Health and Wellbeing (EQ-HWB) is a new generic questionnaire designed to measure quality of life across healthcare, public health, and social care. A shorter version with nine questions (EQ-HWB-9) has been developed, but it needs to be tested in different populations to ensure it works as intended.In this study, we aimed to test the EQ-HWB-9 among adults who had downloaded a meditation app and were likely experiencing poorer mental health. We also examined whether proposed modifications to the questionnaire affected how well it performed. These changes included switching the order of the first two items and making three minor adjustments to the response options.MethodsThis study was conducted as part of a larger project investigating engagement with a free meditation app. Participants were randomly assigned to complete either the original version of the EQ-HWB-9 (from 2022) or a slightly modified version (from 2024). We assessed how well the questionnaire performed using standard psychometric tests, including how responses were distributed, whether the measure could distinguish between known groups, how closely it aligned with related measures, and how sensitive it was to change over time.ResultsParticipants completing the original and modified versions of the EQ-HWB-9 were similar in terms of their demographic characteristics at both baseline (n = 865) and follow-up (n = 130). Overall, the results showed that the EQ-HWB-9 performed well in this population. Changing the order of the first two questions appeared to be beneficial: when the activities question was presented first, it produced higher average scores, greater variation in responses, and stronger relationships with psychosocial items. No meaningful differences were found for the other minor changes made to the questionnaire.ConclusionsThese findings add to the growing evidence that the EQ-HWB-9 is a suitable tool for measuring quality of life across a wide range of settings. They also support a hypothesis from earlier qualitative work suggesting that when the activities question followed the mobility question (original version), respondents tended to think of activities in more physical terms. Placing the activities question first (modified version) seemed to encourage a broader interpretation, consistent with the intentions of the measure’s developers. On this basis, changing the order of the items appears justified. The other modifications had little impact, indicating that further qualitative research may be needed to guide such decisions. Overall, the results suggest that the instrument is ready for wider use, and that developing country-specific value sets will be essential to support its application in economic evaluations.
