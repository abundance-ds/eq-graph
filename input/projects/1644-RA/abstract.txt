Objectives The EQ Health and Well Being is a new generic measure of health and wellbeing, with two versions: a 25-item long form (EQ-HWB) and a shorter 9-item version representing a subset of items (EQ-HWB-S). This study aims to test the psychometric performance of the EQ-HWB and EQ-HWB-S in breast cancer. Methods A single-center longitudinal survey was conducted in Bandung, Indonesia in 2023-24. Soft quotas were used to ensure variability in cancer stage and treatment cycles. Patients undergoing active treatment at baseline were invited for the 2-to-3-week follow-up (test-retest subsample), while those in their final treatment cycle were invited for the 2-to-3-month follow-up (responsiveness subsample). The interviewer-administered paper-and-pencil questionnaires included the following outcome measures: EQ-HWB, EQ-5D-5L, Functional Assessment of Cancer Therapy-General (FACT-G), Warwick-Edinburgh Mental Wellbeing Scale (WEMWBS), and questions on socio-demographic and clinical characteristics. The 25-item EQ-HWB was administered, from which the EQ-HWB-S responses were derived. Index values were computed using the pilot UK (EQ-HWB-S), Indonesian (EQ-5D-5L), and Australian (FACT-8D) value sets. The following psychometric properties were tested: ceiling and floor, convergent validity with corresponding items and scales of other outcome measures, known-group validity (Cohen’s d effect sizes), test-retest reliability (Gwet’s AC2 coefficients and intraclass correlations [ICC]) and responsiveness (standardized response means [SRM] and effect sizes [SES]). Known-groups were defined as follows: patients with EQ VAS scores ≥80, 5-point self-perceived health scale, number of symptoms, and metastasis status. Test-retest analysis was conducted on patients with unchanged health status, while responsiveness analysis was performed on those with improved health, as evaluated by the Global Rating of Change scale. Results Overall, 300 female patients (aged 51.3±10.3) completed the baseline questionnaire. Across the 25 items of the EQ-HWB, the ceiling ranged from 29.0% (‘Exhausted’) to 88.3% (‘Unsupported’). For overall scores, the EQ-HWB-S exhibited a significantly lower ceiling (10.7%) compared to EQ-5D-5L (35.0%) albeit higher than FACT-8D (7.67%), while EQ-HWB and FACT-G yielded 2.3% and 0.7%, respectively. Convergent validity was established between conceptually-overlapping items, e.g., EQ-HWB Getting around inside and outside and EQ-5D-5L Mobility (r=0.60) and EQ-HWB-S Pain and FACT-8D Pain (r=-0.56). EQ-HWB level summary scores correlated at least moderately with FACT-G (r=0.69) and WEMWBS (r=-0.49). Strong correlations were found between the index values of EQ-HWB-S and EQ-5D-5L (r=0.73) and FACT-8D (r=0.70). The EQ-HWB and EQ-HWB-S effectively discriminated across known-groups with large effect sizes, ranging from -0.72 to -1.11 for EQ-HWB and -0.83 to -1.18 for EQ-HWB-S. In test-retest reliability (n=32), 17 EQ-HWB items demonstrated strong to almost-perfect agreement (Gwet’s AC2=0.60-0.97), with Sleep, Exhausted, and Anxious as the lowest performing items (Gwet’s AC2=0.32-0.35). Excellent test-retest reliability was observed at instrument level for EQ-HWB (ICC=0.83, 95%CI 0.68-0.91) and EQ-HWB-S (ICC=0.79, 95%CI 0.61-0.89]. Further, EQ-HWB and EQ-HWB-S showed small but significant responsiveness to change in health (n=75, SRM=-0.29, 0.24; SES=-0.19, 0.14, respectively). Conclusions This is the first study to validate the EQ-HWB and EQ-HWB-S in breast cancer, and also the first to provide test-retest reliability and responsiveness evidence about the two instruments. Our findings add to the growing body of positive psychometric evidence supporting the EQ-HWB and EQ-HWB-S.
