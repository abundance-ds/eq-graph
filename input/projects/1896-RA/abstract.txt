Background: Global migration has led to a growing number of migrants residing in host countries, and it is widely recognised that the migration experience significantly influences health and wellbeing. These population groups are often described as Culturally and Linguistically Diverse (CALD), and referred to internationally as ethnic minorities, racial groups, or by other related terms. The EQ Health and Wellbeing Short version (EQ-HWB-S) is a recently developed instrument by the EuroQol Group, designed to evaluate outcomes across health, public health, and social care settings—covering patients, carers, and social care users. However, further evidence is needed regarding its psychometric performance across diverse populations. This study aimed to assess the psychometric properties of the EQ-HWB-S among CALD individuals in Australia, and compare these with non-CALD Australians. Methods: Data were drawn from an online survey administered to 877 Australians from CALD backgrounds and 2,175 Australians without CALD backgrounds. Respondents completed the EQ-HWB-S, the Recovering Quality of Life Utility Index (ReQoL-UI), the EQ-5D-5L, and the Kessler Psychological Distress Scale (K6) in random order. Psychometric evaluation of the EQ-HWB-S included analysis of response distribution (floor and ceiling effects), convergent validity, and known-group validity. Convergent validity was assessed using Spearman’s rank correlations (interpreted as weak [≤0.4], moderate [0.4–0.7], or strong [>0.7]). Known-group validity was examined across 18 subgroups with and without physical or mental health conditions. Results: The EQ-HWB-S demonstrated similar response patterns among CALD and non-CALD Australians. Strong convergent validity was observed for the EQ-HWB-S when compared with the EQ-5D-5L, ReQoL-UI, and K6, using both sum and index scores. At the item level, the EQ-HWB-S items were moderately to strongly correlated with items of similar constructs from EQ-5D-5L, ReQoL-UI and K6. Known-group validity was generally supported, with the EQHWB-S effectively distinguishing between groups with and without health conditions, except in the cases of hypertension and osteoporosis. Conclusion: This study provides preliminary evidence supporting the psychometric performance of the EQHWB-S in a large sample of Australian adults from CALD backgrounds. The findings suggest that the EQ-HWB-S is a promising outcome measure for use in diverse population groups.
