# **Developing the EuroQol toddler and infant populations (EQ-TIPS) instrument: qualitative analysis of expert views on content validity and conceptual challenges**

## Abstract

### Purpose

The EuroQol Toddler and Infant Populations (EQ-TIPS) is an experimental generic measure of Health-Related Quality of Life (HRQoL), for which preference weights are planned. Expert consultation aimed to (a) review EQ-TIPS V2.0 wording and content, (b) discuss possible uses for the new measure and challenges with instrument development.

### Methods

Semi-structured online focus group consultations were conducted with three expert groups: (1) EuroQol member experts in preference-weighted questionnaire development and use, (2) paediatric health and development practitioners, and (3) paediatric HRQoL instrument developers. Discussions were audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim and thematically analysed.

### Results

Experts thought EQ-TIPS would be useful in informing health research and clinical/economic decision-making, due to limited availability of preference-weighted measures for this age group. EQ-TIPS wording was generally considered appropriate, easy to follow and understand. Overall, the six EQ-TIPS dimensions included in version 2.0 were considered to provide an adequate representation of HRQoL in this age group. However, there was substantial agreement that sleep and emotions, not included in the version discussed, could be relevant. All groups further suggested that including dimension examples, rather than referring to ‘age-appropriate behaviour’, could enhance proxy comprehension. Experts emphasised that future iterations of the descriptive system should ensure dimensions remain relevant to children’s HRQoL, rather than measuring the impact on the proxy respondent.

### Conclusion

Overall, content in EQ-TIPS version 2.0 was considered appropriate for the target age group, but some rewording was suggested, as well as testing of additional content to ensure adequate coverage of the construct measured. Future work should include caregivers from diverse geographical, socio-economic and cultural settings.

## Introduction

The EuroQol Toddler and Infant Populations (EQ-TIPS) (formerly TANDI) is an experimental Health-Related Quality of Life (HRQoL) instrument for very young children. EQ-TIPS was initially developed in South Africa, with extensive work to ensure that items were relevant for infants and toddlers \[1, 2\]. Ongoing multi-national work undertaken within the auspices of the EuroQol Group follows the same philosophy as other EuroQol instruments, e.g. to develop a simple, concise, generic preference-weighted measure \[3\]. EQ-TIPS will, in due course, be accompanied by value sets to facilitate its use in generating utilities for economic evaluation. Considering the large number of cost-utility analyses in very young children \[4\] and the need to collect data from age-appropriate measures \[5\] there is an increasing number of preference-weighted measures being developed or adapted for infants, toddlers and preschoolers (younger than 5 years) \[6\].

EQ-TIPS V2.0 (Fig. <a href="#Fig1" data-ref-type="fig">1</a>), available for testing in 2023, includes six dimensions (*Movement*, *Play*, *Social Interaction*, *Communication*, *Eating*, *Pain*). Each of the dimensions, except pain, references ‘age-appropriate behaviour’ in the survey wording, to ensure applicability for infants and toddlers. The dimension of pain includes examples of observable behaviours associated with pain in non-verbal children (*Painful behaviour includes inconsolable crying*, *restless movement*, *grimacing)* \[1, 2\]. Version 2.0 of the instrument includes three levels of response in each dimension using the severity qualifiers no, some and a lot of problems. Although there is now a five-level version of the EQ-TIPS, it was not available for testing in this study. The final part of EQ-TIPS, the Visual Analogue Scale, records the proxy’s rating of the child’s overall health between 0 and 100 and is adopted from the EQ-5D and EQ-5D-Y instruments \[7\]. EQ-TIPS is considered an experimental version by the EuroQol Research Foundation, indicating it is still under development \[8\].

The ongoing development of EQ-TIPS aims to ensure that it is acceptable and appropriate across diverse geographical locations for toddlers and infants with a wide range of health conditions. Although the initial work in South Africa was robust and included a range of international experts in the Delphi study and choice of item wording, additional consultation to ensure international content validity was considered necessary \[2\]. Expert and patient consultation is an important step in instrument development \[9–12\]. ISPOR (The Professional Society for Health Economics and Outcomes Research) and the Food and Drug administration guidelines \[13, 14\] emphasise expert involvement in instrument development to define key domains, framework conceptualisation, and ensure content validity. Similarly, COSMIN guidelines \[15\] stress the role of experts in evaluating the relevance and applicability of dimensions/items for the target population.

A previously conducted online survey with experts and caregivers provided valuable feedback on EQ-TIPS, and suggestions were made for modifications to the wording and additional items to be tested \[16\]. Noting the limitations of survey responses, it was considered that further consultation with experts was necessary before modifications were made or further tested with caregivers of young children. In the context of EQ-TIPS, parents will represent the ‘patient perspective’ and were included in the previous surveys and will be further consulted in separate future research. Three online expert engagement sessions were thus held, which aimed to (a) review EQ-TIPS V2.0 wording and content, including discussing whether content was sufficiently comprehensive; and (b) discuss the possible uses for the new measure and highlight any challenges in developing HRQoL measures in infants and toddlers.

## Methods

### Experts

A qualitative semi-structured virtual focus group consultation was conducted with each of the three expert groups: (1) EuroQol members with an interest in measurement and valuation of health in Younger Populations, the development of the descriptive (classification) systems for preference-weighted measures, and translation and cultural adaptation of measures (Online Consultation Group 1), Paediatric Health and Development Experts (Online Consultation Group 2), and Paediatric HRQoL Instrument Developers (Online Consultation Group 3). Experts were purposively selected to participate in the study based on their expertise, experience, and to ensure representation of a spread of geographical locations. Experts were identified from the authors’ contacts, through colleagues and/or the relevant published literature.

### Procedure

Online focus group consultations were conducted on the Zoom™ video conferencing platform \[15\] between December 2022 and February 2023. Before each consultation, experts were provided with: a copy of EQ-TIPS instrument; a *Vimeo* link (<https://protect-za.mimecast.com/s/-KL0CMjKp1SqqnngnUWqCaG>*)* to a short introductory video (definition of HRQoL, EQ-TIPS development and descriptive system) and preparatory reading material (publications on the initial EQ-TIPS development \[1, 2\]. All experts provided permission, on the Zoom™ platform, for recording and reporting the meeting. The consultations were iterative, ordered by group number, allowing adjustments and improvements to be made to subsequent discussion guides.

Ethical approval for the study protocol was granted by the Human Research Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Health Sciences at the University of Cape Town (HREC: 307_2022).

#### Online consultation group 1: EuroQol members with expertise in younger populations, descriptive systems, and version management (EQ expert members)

An overview of the development of EQ-TIPS and a summary of the results from the previously conducted online surveys \[16\] were presented as a departure point for discussion. Experts were purposively allocated to one of three breakout rooms to ensure a balance of expertise. Breakout rooms discussions followed a predetermined topic guide and were facilitated by ES, MH and ND. These topics included: overall impression, understanding and relevance of current dimensions, comprehensiveness of EQ-TIPS (missing dimensions or overlap), inclusion of the reference to ‘age-appropriate’ behaviour in the dimension headings, response levels, recall period, considerations for transitioning between EuroQol instruments, and future research priorities. Facilitators presented a summary of each breakout room discussion to the larger group for any further feedback.

The members of the research team involved in facilitating the docus group discussions had diverse professional backgrounds, experiences and prior assumptions. Consideration was given to the most appropriate facilitators for each of the three focus group discussions to ensure participants’ willingness to engage openly about their experience and knowledge.

#### Online consultation group 2: paediatric health and development experts (paediatric experts)

Experts were presented with an overview of (1) the existing EuroQol adult (EQ-5D) and youth (EQ-5D-Y) instruments to measure and value health and (2) the development of EQ-TIPS. Experts were purposively assigned to one of three breakout rooms to ensure a spread in expertise and geography. All breakout rooms followed the same discussion guide and were facilitated by MH, KD and JJ with notetaking and support from EB, JV and BM. Predetermined topics covered included general impression of EQ-TIPS, relevance across the age range of 0–3 years, and the need to refine, add or remove dimensions. A summary of all three breakout rooms was developed by the facilitators and forwarded to experts by e-mail for any further comments and to check for errors or omissions.

#### Online consultation group 3: experts in the development of paediatric outcome measures (instrument developers)

This group began with a general discussion on the feasibility of measuring HRQoL in toddlers and infants and the challenges involved and was facilitated by ES. Experts were probed on whether and how constructs of health that are influenced by rapid development during this young age range can be measured accurately. Experts were further asked to discuss the challenges associated with the use of proxy reporting for toddler and infant outcomes, and whether any special considerations need to be given to the recall period and response options for this age group. This discussion was followed by a brief introduction to EQ-TIPS descriptive system and a follow-up discussion regarding the content validity (relevance, comprehensibility and comprehensiveness) of EQ-TIPS, facilitated by MH.

### Data management and analysis

The recommendations outlined in the Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research (SRQR) were followed in this study \[16\]. Each group discussion and the breakout rooms were recorded (with the experts’ permission) using Zoom (version 5.0.2). The audio recordings were transcribed verbatim and de-identified, with each expert allocated a sequential number, and country-specific information was replaced with “country X”. However, the transcripts could not be completely anonymised, as references to expertise or experience may make them identifiable to a familiar audience. All information relating to the individual recruitment was kept separate from the analysed data. Recordings and transcripts were stored on a secure server accessible to the research team only. The data will be retained for 10 years after publication.

Thematic analysis of the qualitative data followed the six-phase process outlined by Braun and Clark \[17\]. Data analysis was carried out by two members of the research team (JV, EB) using the qualitative analysis software NVivo (Version 14). The themes were deductively coded according to the semi-structured questioning, with allowance of inductive themes and sub-themes to develop or be renamed during analysis. Researchers met to discuss findings after each phase to ensure consistency and agreement regarding analytical interpretations. The researchers analysing the data had unique clinical and public health/economic backgrounds. A data extraction table was generated to report codes, sub-nodes and related quotes (Table <a href="#Tab1" data-ref-type="table">1</a>). The data extraction table was further checked by all the researchers, facilitating the focus group discussions to ensure that the individual biases of those analysing the data were minimised.

## Results

### Sample characteristics

A total of 44 experts (Group 1, *n* = 21; Group 2, *n* = 13; Group 3, *n* = 9) were invited to attend one of three online consultations. Eleven did not attend due to work commitments, resulting in a final sample size of 33 (Group 1, *n* = 17; Group 2, *n* = 11; Group 3, *n* = 5).

Fifteen countries were represented across the three consultations (Fig. <a href="#Fig2" data-ref-type="fig">2</a>). Within the paediatric health and development expert group (Group 2), there was representation from generalised paediatrics and sub specialities (e.g. sleep, neurodevelopment, and infant mental health), in addition to experts in paediatric nursing and education. In comparison to the other two groups, the number of experts with experience in paediatric instrument development was small and drawn from in and around Europe and Canada.

### Theme: overall impressions

Most experts found EQ-TIPS easy to complete and valued its short length for feasibility in clinical trials and research. They endorsed the instrument layout, response options and EQ VAS, but noted its performance across diverse health conditions required further evidence. There was some discussion of the measure’s ableist focus on problems rather than abilities or strengths (Table <a href="#Tab1" data-ref-type="table">1</a>, Quote 4), but acknowledged this as a common limitation across most HRQoL instruments and not specific to EQ-TIPS.

### Theme: conceptual framework

#### Sub-theme: construct of measurement

Some experts remarked that it was not entirely clear to them what EQ-TIPS was intended to measure, i.e., whether health, health status, HRQoL or development (Table <a href="#Tab1" data-ref-type="table">1</a>, Quote 7) and suggested that a clear definition should be provided to guide future users. Experts emphasised that dimension wording and inclusion should align with this construct whilst prioritising dimensions that focus on the child’s health and avoid assessment of the impact of the child’s health on the family or caregiver (Table <a href="#Tab1" data-ref-type="table">1</a>, Quote 6; Quote 76). This could reduce the risk that the instrument would incidentally capture the spillover effect of the child’s health problems on the caregiver/family HRQoL when completing EQ-TIPS. There was discussion around the need for EQ-TIPS to be accompanied by preference-weighted scores and a scoring system with broader applications beyond health economic evaluation.

There was debate on whether EQ-TIPS should be consistent, in content, with other HRQoL measures developed by EuroQol for children (e.g. EQ-5D-Y-5 L) and adults (e.g. EQ-5D-5 L). Measurement consistency for the underlying construct, i.e., HRQoL, was recommended if the goal is for seamless transition across EuroQol instruments (Table <a href="#Tab1" data-ref-type="table">1</a>, Quote 8). While some experts suggested that consideration should be given to how EQ-TIPS dimensions will map to EQ-5D-Y dimensions, most thought that including dimensions relevant to the age group was more important (Table <a href="#Tab1" data-ref-type="table">1</a>, Quote 11; Quote 12). Discussions from all three groups highlighted potential applications, with clinical experts suggesting EQ-TIPS as a complement to clinical patient assessment (Table <a href="#Tab1" data-ref-type="table">1</a>, Quote 15), routine outcome measurement and clinical trials (Table <a href="#Tab1" data-ref-type="table">1</a>, Quote 13).

#### Sub-theme: child health circumstances

Important challenges highlighted by experts when measuring health in this age group (in comparison to other age groups) included: the need to consider each child’s own unique health status and developmental trajectory, which could be independent of age; how premature infants would be assessed, as their developmental trajectory during this period differs from term infants (Table <a href="#Tab1" data-ref-type="table">1</a>, Quote 16). It was also noted that the health of a child with an acute health condition may be compared to others; this comparison may differ for those with chronic conditions (Table <a href="#Tab1" data-ref-type="table">1</a>, Quote 17).

#### Sub-theme: cultural and translation considerations

Across all three groups, a recurring theme was the need to assess the ease of translation and the relevance of EQ-TIPS descriptive system across diverse cultural and socio-economic contexts. It was noted that health and development, and age-appropriate norms, might vary across cultural settings (Table <a href="#Tab1" data-ref-type="table">1</a>, Quotes 21–24). Experts suggested that including examples in the dimension headings would aid standardisation of the interpretation of all dimensions across diverse cultural and socio-economic settings (Table <a href="#Tab1" data-ref-type="table">1</a> Quote 53; Quote 64).

### Theme: age range

The discussion on the age range for which EQ-TIPS could be used provided valuable insights from all three groups. Support for the current age range, 0–3 years, highlighted its relevance, particularly in capturing infants’ and toddlers’ expressions through non-verbal cues and actions. Although it was noted that all children, regardless of age, experience the dimensions included, it was debated whether they were equally relevant across the age range. There was little consensus on whether multiple instruments were needed for this age range, and if required, what the age ranges for multiple instruments should be. The addition of appropriate examples across the age range, for each dimension, was considered by most to be a good solution to ensure relevance for all intended ages.

### Theme: proxy completion

Discussion across groups highlighted several concerns relating to proxy reporting and potential subjectivity introduced from a perspective external to the child. This subjectivity is linked to intrinsic (age, personal experience, emotional development, number of children) and extrinsic (social support, resource access and availability) factors. While these influences are unavoidable in proxy completion, it was suggested that additional questions capturing proxy characteristics and HRQoL, to measure spillover, accompany EQ-TIPS or be included within individual study designs (Table <a href="#Tab1" data-ref-type="table">1</a>, Quote 32). Additionally, comparing responses to age-appropriate behaviour may further emphasise such factors and introduce unnecessary subjectivity. This further supported the exclusion of the reference to ‘age-appropriate behaviour’ in the dimension headings.

The importance of determining the ideal proxy respondent who has familiarity with the child was highlighted. This was especially relevant for those in childcare settings or during hospitalisation (Table <a href="#Tab1" data-ref-type="table">1</a>, Quote 35). It was generally concluded that the best proxy may depend on the context and study design: for example, for hospitalised children, clinicians were suggested as alternative proxy respondents.

### Theme: content validity—relevance, comprehensibility, comprehensiveness

#### Sub-theme: recall period

Experts across groups generally agreed that ‘Today’ was the most suitable recall period for this age group and context. However, some experts proposed extending the recall period beyond ‘Today’, to better capture the HRQoL of children living with chronic or fluctuating conditions. The recall period of today was considered optimal for acute health conditions or for repeated measures to test responsiveness to interventions.

#### Sub-theme: response levels

Discussion on the response levels was limited, with few concerns raised about the three levels of severity used in EQ-TIPS v2.0. Some experts highlighted the need to test extended response levels to possibly improve measurement sensitivity. As the respondents will be adults, completing more levels was considered unlikely to be challenging.

#### Sub-theme: dimensions

All dimensions, except for pain, refer to ‘age-appropriate’ behaviour (Fig. <a href="#Fig1" data-ref-type="fig">1</a>). Many experts noted that while the term ‘age-appropriate’ enhances EQ-TIPS’ applicability across the entire age range, its meaning could be better clarified with age-specific examples. Another challenge in understanding ‘age-appropriate’ was the potential for low health literacy among some respondents (Table <a href="#Tab1" data-ref-type="table">1</a> Quote 49; Quote 53) and differences across countries in the understanding of developmental norms. Some experts did question whether ‘developmentally appropriate’ has a more standardised meaning than ‘age-appropriate’ across different populations.

##### Movement (moves about at an age-appropriate level)

Although discussion on movement was limited among experts, it appeared to be relatively well accepted. It was noted that other language groups often have fewer descriptive words when compared to English, potentially limiting the meaning (Table <a href="#Tab1" data-ref-type="table">1</a>, Quote 60). This was noted for movement, where the translation into Chinese would require care as the most frequently used term would reflect walking, whereas movement in this case is intended to relate to a range of developmental tasks, including rolling and crawling.

##### Play (enjoys playing with objects or toys at an age-appropriate level)

Play was recognised as important for all children. However, concerns were raised about whether the reference to toys was relevant across diverse cultural and socio-economic contexts (Table <a href="#Tab1" data-ref-type="table">1</a>, Quote 64). There was acknowledgement that the inclusion of objects broadens the description of play, but it was noted that further consideration should be given to a variety of play, including interactions with others. A few experts further suggested that the relevance of play may vary across the age range and warrants further research.

##### Social interaction (engages with others in an age-appropriate manner)

Experts emphasised the importance of refining the descriptor and providing examples of social interaction to prevent misinterpretation (Table <a href="#Tab1" data-ref-type="table">1</a>, Quote 66). It was further suggested that social interaction may be difficult to interpret in very young children (younger than 3 months), and explicit examples may assist understanding. An expert in child health and development noted that inconsistency may occur if the same attribute is applied to a range of ages, as social interaction could be interpreted differently according to the age or stage of verbal communication in a child.

##### Communication (communicates at an age-appropriate level)

Although some experts acknowledged the importance of communication in this age group, others questioned whether this dimension would discriminate between groups and/or whether it would be confounded by age-related factors, such as self-limiting communication in new environments. Experts also noted some ambiguity in the wording of the descriptor and the fact that no distinction was made between non-verbal and verbal communication (Table <a href="#Tab1" data-ref-type="table">1</a>, Quote 73).

##### Eating (eats adequate available food in an age-appropriate manner)

The inclusion of eating was endorsed, with discussions acknowledging its crucial role in the overall health and development of children in this age range. Discussions across expert groups emphasised that normal variations in toddler eating behaviour, such as ‘picky eating’, should not be captured as a ‘problem’ in this dimension (Table <a href="#Tab1" data-ref-type="table">1</a>, Quote 76). This called for categorisation of the item as a health problem, within the HRQoL framework, and not capturing parental concern, e.g. “*He just won’t eat broccoli*”. Experts further noted that wording for eating may not be inclusive of breast or bottle-feeding, characteristic of infancy.

##### Pain (painful behaviour includes inconsolable crying, restless movement, grimacing)

Inclusion of a dimension assessing the presence and severity of pain was considered important in this age group. Experts in group 3 highlighted concern around caregivers’ ability to accurately assess the severity of the child’s pain. It was suggested that caregivers would rate the child’s expression of distress rather than pain. Whether distress was a better description of what should be measured was heavily debated, but some would classify this as a broader construct than pain and more difficult to translate (Table <a href="#Tab1" data-ref-type="table">1</a>, Quote 81).

#### Sub-theme: dimension overlap and missing dimensions

##### Dimension overlap

Experts from all three groups raised concerns about the overlap between communication and social interaction and discussed whether these two concepts could be collapsed into one dimension.

##### Missing dimensions

Experts proposed and discussed several additional dimensions for EQ-TIPS to enhance its ability to provide a more comprehensive HRQoL assessment. The inclusion of sleep as a dimension was widely discussed (Table <a href="#Tab1" data-ref-type="table">1</a>, Quote 87). However, experts questioned whether sleep is inherently a health concern or if it is problematic only when it impacts the child’s functioning. Questions were also raised about whether a dimension of emotions should be included or whether this was adequately covered by social interaction (Table <a href="#Tab1" data-ref-type="table">1</a>, Quote 94). As infants and toddlers are influenced by their environment, this was raised as a potential future consideration. Bowel habits were raised by a few experts (Table <a href="#Tab1" data-ref-type="table">1</a>, Quote 98), which may be important for a young child’s health; however, overall, there was relatively little support for its inclusion in a measure of HRQoL.

## Discussion

Stakeholder engagement is a key consideration in instrument development and can include several role players, but most often includes experts \[9, 17–21\]. The consultation with diverse groups of experts during the early stages of multinational refinement of EQ-TIPS ensures global expert content validity and enhanced applicability to clinical and Health Technology Assessment applications. Results from this expert consultation affirm those from the previously conducted multinational stakeholder engagement survey \[16\]. Considering the limited number of preference-weighted measures in this age group, experts acknowledged the need for EQ-TIPS to inform health research and clinical/economic decision-making. EQ-TIPS wording was generally considered appropriate, easy to follow and understand. Overall, the six EQ-TIPS dimensions included in version 2.0 were considered to provide an adequate representation of HRQoL in this age group. However, there was substantial agreement that sleep and emotions, not included in the version discussed, could be relevant and warranted further testing. There was no consensus in any of the three groups regarding the overlap between dimensions, particularly communication and social interaction, highlighted in the previous survey engagement \[16\]. All groups further suggested that including examples, rather than reference to ‘age-appropriate behaviour’, could enhance proxy comprehension. Issues with instrument development and proxy completion were more heavily debated, particularly in groups 1 and 3.

The general debate on what patient/observer-reported outcome instruments measure, whether health, health status, Quality of Life or HRQoL, is not unique to EQ-TIPS and has been the subject of many publications \[22–25\]. Many definitions exist for these terms, with little consensus in the literature \[23\]. In the case of EQ-TIPS, Verstraete et al. state that it was developed as a generic measure of HRQoL amenable to generating preference-weighted scores \[2\]. Instrument development and item selection followed the framework described, including: (1) HRQoL defined by three broad domains, including physical, emotional and social functioning, (2) the measure needed to be sensitive to aspects of development across the age group and (3) to allow for proxy completion, items should be easily observable or described in terms of observable behaviour \[33\]. The experts agreed that EQ-TIPS reflects the three broad dimensions, and the aspects are sensitive to the age groups. Further, they agreed that those aspects are observable.

As EQ-TIPS is part of the EuroQol family of instruments, which aims to allow measurement and valuation of HRQoL across the lifespan, the transition between EQ-TIPS and EQ-5D-Y and consistency in measurement concept were considered important. The difference in dimensions between the EQ-5D-Y and EQ-TIPS was noted across expert discussions. A few experts highlighted these differences as problematic when considering transition, while most recognised it as an important departure to accurately measure HRQoL in the target age groups. Although mapping of constructs between measures is advantageous, any change in wording, even within the same construct or dimension, has the potential to make comparison challenging, but not impossible \[26, 27\].

Retention of the EQ-5D-Y-3L recall period and severity levels was considered to be advantageous in the initial development effort \[2\]. Discussion of the recall period was similar to that from the online survey, where ‘Today’ was generally considered favourable, but a longer recall period may have advantages for fluctuating health conditions \[16\]. Like most generic paediatric HRQoL measures, EQ-TIPS was developed with a deficit focus, where the descriptive system measures the frequency or severity of experiencing problems \[28\]. Some experts, particularly in group 2, considered this to be ‘ableist’ and referred to the recent and growing interest in developing measures of well-being with a focus on capability rather than disability \[29\]. Strengths-based language may enhance inclusion and survey completion for children with severe conditions or complex disabilities that are not expected to cure or improve. However, medical technology is advancing rapidly, and the advent of gene-modulating therapy has seen children with severe or complex disabilities such as Duchenne’s Muscular Dystrophy \[30\], Spinal Muscular Atrophy \[31\] and Cystic Fibrosis \[32\] experiencing a significant improvement in their previously incurable symptoms. Evidence based on this deficit-focused model may still be needed to measure responsiveness in clinical trials. Strengths-based measures have some advantages \[33, 34\], but further evidence may be needed to inform their usefulness for economic evaluations, where the focus is on assessing incremental change in HRQoL as a result of healthcare (or other) interventions. The consistency of language and construct of measure between EQ-TIPS, EQ-5D-Y and EQ-5D is also considered important to allow measurement of HRQoL from the ‘cradle to the grave.’

Proxy completion of EQ-TIPS and the limitations of proxy responses were discussed with the experts. It was agreed that proxies will typically be a parent. Parents may, however, not be the best option in all instances, and the choice of proxy should depend on the objectives of any study in which EQ-TIPS is used. Parents who had limited interaction with their child due to the child’s hospitalisation, periods of parental absence etc. were considered, by some, to not have sufficient knowledge of their child to complete the questionnaire. The importance of capturing potential influencing factors (e.g. relationship to the child, number of children, caregiving experience, etc.) is noted and is recommended for researchers to aid the interpretation of data. Research is further encouraged to explore the effects of these influencing factors on EQ-TIPS completion. Experts further suggested that the spillover of health consequences of the caregiver and family be captured on an appropriate outcome measure and not on EQ-TIPS. The potential for collecting spillover is higher for dimensions of eating and sleeping.

Experts across groups recommended that providing examples of observable behaviours, rather than reference to ‘age-appropriate behaviours’ would provide better comprehension and consistency of EQ-TIPS responses. Comparison to peers may give a stronger sense that the intention is to measure developmental capabilities rather than the child’s HRQoL. Furthermore, if there are cultural expectations around development, the proxy completer’s expectation about what is ‘age appropriate’ might systematically affect responses, limiting the ability to directly compare data between settings. Consideration could be given to comparing each dimension to children of the same age for further testing with parents or caregivers. Although Peasgood and colleagues highlight that inter-personal and personal comparability should be avoided in item framing for preference-based measures \[35\]; as comparison to others depends on the individual chosen for reference, and how people ‘usually’ feel can be biased by past or recent events \[36\]. The relevance of examples and dimension performance across the age range will similarly need to be considered during any EQ-TIPS revisions, and tested empirically. Experts acknowledged that the relative importance of dimensions may change for children of different ages. However, this is also true when one interrogates instruments that span childhood and adolescence or adulthood; for example, the relative importance of dimensions has been shown to differ according to the age of the child \[37–39\]. The important thing will be to ensure that revisions to EQ-TIPS’ dimensions do not affect their comprehensibility and relevance to caregivers of children across the relevant age range and with diverse health conditions.

A limitation of the study is that experts were recruited based on their known expertise to the research team or their extended networks, which may have biased the results. To protect the experts’ identity, quotations could only be attributed to a number and did not include additional information such as country of residence or work environment. Experts within each of the groups had a different level of knowledge about outcome measures and EQ-TIPS, which, in some instances, limited discussion and debate. Although preparatory reading material was provided, it may have been useful to have included a longer and more general introductory session before the discussion. On reflection, the breakout rooms worked very well to allow all voices to be heard but did limit general debate and consensus. This work aimed to engage with experts in the fields of outcome measurement, child health and development, while subsequent work is suggested to engage with parents and caregivers. We acknowledge that many of the experts included had experience as parents or primary caregivers and reflected on their own experiences. Details of their parenting or caregiving experience and lived experience caring for children with a health condition were, however, not collected.

## Conclusion

Overall, experts participating in these online consultations considered EQ-TIPS to be a necessary and potentially useful measure of HRQoL in toddlers and infants and provided several considerations and suggestions about how the instrument could be improved. Adding age-specific examples, rather than a reference to ‘age-appropriate behaviour’, to the dimension header was suggested to standardise interpretation. Current dimensions were accepted, and experts affirmed the need to test additional dimensions of sleep and emotion. Inclusion of experts from different backgrounds and cultural settings was useful in gaining a range of insights into how content should be adjusted and how it could be used in the future. Future development and testing should include respondents with lower literacy and diverse socio-economic settings to ensure global relevance and comprehension.
