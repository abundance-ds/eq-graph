The Dara Project conducted a comprehensive evaluation of the EuroQol Health and Wellbeing Instrument (EQ-HWB) in measuring outcomes of a multidimensional social program targeting vulnerable families with chronically ill children in Brazil. The study, executed from 2024-2025, tracked 104 participants through a structured intervention program focusing on health, edu-cation, citizenship, housing, and income support. The study population comprised predominantly single mothers (81.7%) with a mean age of 33.8 years, characterized by low educational attainment and limited income generation. Most participants (94.2% female) reported significant socioeconomic challenges, with 30.7% not completing elementary education and 95.1% generating no income. Methods The research employed a mixed-methods approach across five phases: pilot testing (n=10), baseline data collection, test-retest reliability assessment (n=45), cognitive debriefing (n=57), and post six-month evaluation. The translation process was externally conducted by RWS group with minimal researcher involvement. . Data collection utilized electronic Case Report Forms (RedCap) to minimize missing data. Validity assessments included convergent validity testing against EQ-5D-5L and SWEMWBS; test-retest. Cognitive interviews were recorded and analysed by two independent researchers using a structured assessment framework. After six month of the multidimensional social intervention, responsiveness and after six months, responsiveness (compared with EQ-5D-5L) and know-groups were also tested. . Key Findings Through this methodical approach, the study revealed important insights about instrument performance. The EQ-HWB showed moderate test-retest reliability (ρc = 0.504) and strong convergent validity with established measures. Inconsistent responses were observed in 33% of participants when transitioning from negative and positive items in the EQ-HWB long version (Moving from negative ques-tions 1 to 18 to positive questions 19, 20, and 21) The cognitive debriefing showed some 20% did not understand (or can explain) the concept of "Feel you had nothing to look forward to" . The intervention's impact analysis revealed statistically significant improvements across multiple measures. The EQ-5D-5L Index increased from 0.754 to 0.817 (p<0.001), while the EQ-HWB-S Index showed meaningful improvement from 0.602 to 0.713 (p=0.003). These changes were supported by significant gains in EQ-5D-5L Visual Analogue Scale scores. EQ-HWB seems to be more sensible to detect changes after social interventions. Discussion Implementation challenges emerged primarily around comprehension of the longer version and navigation between negative and positive items. The cognitive debriefing phase identified specific items requiring revision, particularly those involving abstract concepts or complex ter-minology. Response patterns showed a tendency toward extreme or neutral responses, sug-gesting possible cultural influences or reflection of participants' lived experiences. The study's findings have important implications for both measurement methodology and social intervention evaluation. The resultssuggests a need to balance comprehensive assess-ment with practical implementation considerations in vulnerable populations. The observed improvements across multiple measures validate the social program's effectiveness while high-lighting the instrument's responsiveness to change. Limitations of the study include the predominantly female sample and potential response bias in income reporting. Future research should explore the instrument's performance across more diverse populations and investigate the cultural factors influencing response patterns. This pioneering study represents the first documented longitudinal assessment of EQ-HWB in conjunction with social intervention for vulnerable populations. The findings support the in-strument's validity while highlighting crucial considerations for implementation in similar con-texts, contributing significantly to understanding both practical implementation challenges and the potential of standardized wellbeing measures in evaluating social program outcomes.
