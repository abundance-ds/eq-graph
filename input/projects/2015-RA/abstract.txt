Background. Constant proportionality (CPTO) — the assumption that individuals trade aconstant proportion of life duration for a proportional health improvement regardless of thetime horizon — is a foundational premise of the linear quality-adjusted life year (QALY)model and is closely related to time-trade-off (TTO) valuation methods. Departures fromCPTO and from linear utility of time have been reported in the literature; if theseassumptions do not hold empirically, values from standard elicitation may be affected, withimplications for EQ-VT methods and for all EuroQol-instrument valuation work. Whileindividual discounting in the monetary domain has been the subject of extensive cumulativemeta-analytic work, discounting and other CPTO violations in the health domain had notbeen systematically synthesised across elicitation methods.Objectives. To systematically review empirical evidence testing CPTO and the relatedassumption of linear utility of time in health preferences, summarize reported violations ofCPTO/linear utility (e.g. discounting behaviour), compare alternative functional forms head-to-head, and assess relevance for EuroQol valuation research.Methods. We searched PubMed, Scopus, EconLit and the Cochrane Library from inceptionto September 2025 for experimental or survey-based studies directly testing CPTO orestimating time-preference parameters in health-valuation tasks. Dual independentscreening and structured extraction captured sample characteristics, framing, elicitationmethod, time horizon, reported discounting direction and magnitude, and head-to-headcomparisons of alternative functional forms. Risk of bias and certainty were not formallyassessed (PROSPERO CRD420251019603).Results. We identified 77 publications (78 study records — one publication, Mulhern 2014,contributed two sub-studies), with ~36,700 participants across 24 countries. Samplesincluded general-population respondents, students, patients and proxies. Studies groupedinto four broad lines of evidence challenging CPTO: direct axiomatic tests, discounting / non-constant time preference (the dominant cluster), prospect-theoretic violations and other non-linear patterns. Across studies, CPTO was usually not supported, most commonly throughevidence of non-constant discounting. Positive discounting was the dominant direction(range 0%–93% of respondents within studies); zero/neutral discounting — the only directionstrictly consistent with CPTO — ranged 0%–56%; and negative discounting, an unusualpattern in health preferences, was nonetheless reported in 34/78 studies (44%). Among the8 studies formally comparing discounting model families head-to-head, non-exponentialspecifications (most often hyperbolic) provided better fit in 6/8 studies, withexponential/constant discounting preferred only in 2/8, using fit criteria such as AIC/WAIC,RMSE and R²/adjusted R². A quantitative synthesis of comparable discount-rate estimates iscurrently underway and will be reported in the accompanying manuscript.Conclusions. This is, to our knowledge, the largest systematic synthesis of empiricalevidence on CPTO and linearity in health preferences. The balance of evidence is largelyinconsistent with strict CPTO: departures are commonly observed across methods andvaluation contexts, with non-constant discounting accounting for the majority of thedocumented violations and non-exponential models typically outperforming exponentialdiscounting in head-to-head comparisons. These findings are directly relevant to EuroQolvaluation research — particularly to TTO interpretation, EQ-VT design and ongoing work onpaediatric and long-term outcome valuation. They suggest that CPTO and linear utilityshould be treated as empirically testable rather than self-evident assumptions, and that themagnitude and shape of observed time-preference effects, rather than their mere presence,should inform whether and how non-linear preferences are accommodated in value-set derivation — including through the corrective and generalised approaches now available inthe methodological literature.
