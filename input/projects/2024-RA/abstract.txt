Background:The EQ Health and Wellbeing (EQ-HWB) is a new generic measure intended to capture health-, social care-, and carer-related quality of life. Following developmental testing, additional work was required to assess the comprehensibility, translatability, and performance of both the Experimental and Modified versions of the EQ-HWB and EQ-HWB-9 across broader linguistic and cultural settings.Objectives:Primarily, this project aimed to analyse the understanding of the EQ-HWB-9 across four languages - German, Hungarian, Slovene and Dutch - to help inform an evidence-based decision towards the finalisation of the EQ-HWB-9 descriptive system made by the EQ-HWb working group. Secondly, this project aimed to summarise the experiences and findings from researcher-led translations and cognitive debriefing (CD) of the EQ-HWB and EQ-HWB-9 and to extract methodological lessons to support future instrument refinement and translation guidance. Methods:To investigate comprehension of the EQ-HWB-9 components, i.e. items, responce scales and instructions, results from the country team's CD interviews among a diverse convenience sample (N=64) of adults with varying health conditions were used. CD involved a structured ranking exercise of qualifier hierarchies (difficulty, frequency, severity), line-by-line item probing, and qualitative coding based on the EQ-HWB Working Group framework. Responses from translation, CD and ranking exercises in the four countries were transcribed and compiled into exercise specific Excel files. The EQ-HWB Working Group coding framework  was applied to categorize issues with understanding.  An on-line session was held to establish consensus on the nature of each code and to standardise the coding during which the Research Team collectively examined a sample of responses. In coding each item, researchers considered respondents’ interpretations and examples, as well as feedback from interviewers, translation teams, and VMC reviewers. Attention was given to translation quality, conceptual clarity, and formatting.Subsequently, each item was double-coded: one researcher conducted the initial coding, and another verified it. To ensure consistency, the team held further online consensus meetings where sample responses were reviewed, coding was standardized, and discrepancies were resolved.Analysis of the reports from the four countries provided a summarised qualitative view of the understanding of the original experimental and modified items and acceptability of the modifications over the original experimental items and response scales. Final grading of each item (red, orange and green) and recommendations as to possible amendments were based on reaching a shared consensus based on the frequency and the narrative data.Results:Overall, the EQ-HWB-9 was well understood across languages, though several cross-linguistic challenges emerged. Respondents frequently struggled to differentiate frequency qualifiers—particularly “only occasionally” versus “sometimes”—with one-third misordering these levels. Double-barrelled items (“getting around inside and outside”, “concentrating or thinking clearly”, “sad or depressed”) produced substantial interpretation difficulties, especially due to ambiguous constructs and problematic examples (e.g. mobility aids). Several terms (e.g. “anxious”, “exhausted”) showed language-dependent ambiguities. Qualifier hierarchies for difficulty and severity were generally well understood, whereas frequency qualifiers performed least well. Modified phrasings sometimes improved clarity (e.g. replacing “only occasionally” with “rarely”), though language-specific variation was substantial. Issues related to fluctuating symptoms (pain, sadness, depression) highlighted challenges in the 7-day recall period for certain constructs.Conclusions:The cross-country evaluation confirms good overall comprehensibility of the EQ-HWB-9 but identifies recurring linguistic and conceptual issues that warrant refinement before finalising the descriptive system. Key recommendations include revising or splitting double-barrelled items, reconsidering problematic qualifiers (especially frequency terms), reviewing examples involving mobility aids, and aligning stems with intensity-based response options. Addressing these issues now will strengthen cross-cultural validity and ensure robust future implementation of the EQ-HWB measures.
