# Validation of EuroQol instruments in paediatric patients and their caregivers in China: protocol for a prospective observational study

## Abstract

### Introduction

The EQ-5D-Y (the youth version of EQ-5D) is widely used to assess children’s health-related quality of life (HRQoL), yet its psychometric properties across administration modes remain insufficiently explored, particularly in paediatric oncology and rare diseases. Additionally, the broader impact of childhood illness on family caregivers (spillover effects) is underexamined. This study aims to evaluate the validity, reliability and responsiveness of the three-level version of EQ-5D-Y (EQ-5D-Y-3L) and the five-level version of EQ-5D-Y (EQ-5D-Y-5L) across different modes while also assessing the EQ-5D five-level version (EQ-5D-5L) and the new EQ Health and Well-being Short Version (EQ-HWB-9) in capturing spillover effects. Originally designed for social care interventions, the EQ-HWB-9 is expected to be applicable to caregivers.

### Methods and analysis

This prospective observational study will recruit children aged 5–16 years with pneumonia, central nervous system (CNS) solid tumours or immune thrombocytopenic purpura (ITP) from three hospitals in China, along with their caregivers. A total of 360 dyads (patients and their caregivers) are planned for recruitment. Children will complete EQ-5D-Y-3L, EQ-5D-Y-5L and Paediatric Quality of Life Inventory (PedsQL) in self-complete (SC), interviewer-administered (IA) and proxy-reported modes by caregivers. Caregivers will complete EQ-5D-5L and EQ-HWB-9 to assess spillover effects. Data will be collected at baseline and follow-up (2–3 weeks). Primary outcomes include psychometric assessments (construct validity, reliability and responsiveness) of all the instruments. Secondary outcomes include HRQoL scores, ceiling effects and the correlation between EQ-5D-Y and PedsQL. A qualitative substudy will explore children’s response interpretation and factors contributing to ceiling effects. Statistical analyses will include intraclass correlation coefficients for test–retest reliability, analysis of variance for known-groups validity, effect sizes for responsiveness, regression for spillover effects and thematic analysis for qualitative data.

### Ethics and dissemination

Ethical approval has been obtained from three ethics committees: the Guizhou Medical University (IRB:2024159), the Children’s Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine (IRB:2024-IRB-0158-P-01) and Guizhou Provincial People’s Hospital (IRB:2024073). Written informed consent will be secured from caregivers, and assent will be obtained from children aged 8 years and older. Study findings will be disseminated through national/international conferences and peer-reviewed journals.

### Trial registration number

ClinicalTrials.gov, [NCT06873672](NCT06873672).

## Introduction

The EQ-5D-Y (the youth version of EQ-5D) is a widely used instrument for assessing health-related quality of life (HRQoL) in paediatric populations. While the three-level version of EQ-5D-Y (EQ-5D-Y-3L) has been extensively validated,<sup>1</sup> the five-level (EQ-5D-Y-5L) has been recently published<sup>2</sup> and requires further psychometric evaluation to establish its validity. To date, most studies evaluating the EQ-5D-Y-5L have focused on self-complete (SC) versions, particularly in children with idiopathic scoliosis,<sup>3</sup> haematological diseases,<sup>4</sup> psychological disorders<sup>5</sup> and healthy young populations.<sup>6</sup> However, there is limited research on the proxy-reported version, with only a few studies examining its feasibility in children with scoliosis<sup>7</sup> and haematological conditions.<sup>8 9</sup> Furthermore, only one study has compared the interviewer-administered (IA) and SC versions in children aged 8–10 years, primarily focusing on construct validity.<sup>10</sup>

Despite extensive research on the psychometric properties of EQ-5D-Y-3L, several key knowledge gaps remain. First, there is limited evidence on responsiveness<sup>11 12</sup> and test–retest reliability,<sup>8</sup> particularly for the IA and proxy versions. Second, most published studies did not use index values, which are central to the EQ-5D framework. Additionally, comparative studies assessing EQ-5D-Y-3L and EQ-5D-Y-5L against other generic HRQoL instruments, such as the Paediatric Quality of Life Inventory (PedsQL),<sup>13</sup> are lacking, particularly in children aged under 5 years. Moreover, limited research has focused on the application of EQ-5D-Y in paediatric patients with cancer or rare diseases, further underscoring the need for validation in these populations. Such research is warranted, as the costs of many interventions in those populations are expensive and therefore in need of cost-effectiveness evaluations, which the EQ-5D-Y can help to provide.

For the reasons mentioned above, we will assess the psychometric properties of these two instruments in three different health condition groups, including one infectious disease (pneumonia), one rare disease (immune thrombocytopenic purpura (ITP)) and two malignant diseases (central nervous system (CNS) solid tumours).

Pneumonia remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality in children under 5 years of age.<sup>14</sup> The WHO estimates that pneumonia accounts for over 900 000 deaths annually among children.<sup>15</sup> In the Western Pacific region, the incidence of pneumonia in children under 5 years is reported at 0.11 in 2011, with 61 900 deaths attributed to the disease.<sup>16</sup> Pneumonia imposes a significant economic burden on families and healthcare systems. There is limited validation of EQ-5D-Y in children with pneumonia, although previous studies suggest its potential as an outcome measure in respiratory diseases. For instance, a study in the Western Cape found that EQ-5D-Y-3L IA demonstrates acceptable convergent validity and test–retest reliability in children aged 5–10 years suffering from various respiratory illnesses, including cystic fibrosis and bronchiectasis.<sup>17</sup>

Neuroblastoma and Wilms tumour are among the most common paediatric CNS solid tumours, accounting for approximately 8% and 6% of childhood malignancies, respectively.<sup>18</sup> However, research on EQ-5D-Y validation in these populations is scarce. One study involving 36 children with brain and solid tumours assesses the feasibility and validity of EQ-5D-Y-3L and EQ-5D-Y-5L, finding that EQ-5D-Y-5L has lower ceiling effects and higher discriminative power.<sup>19</sup> Furthermore, EQ-5D has been widely used to assess HRQoL in adults with brain tumours. A study in Norway found that EQ-5D-3L was responsive to deteriorating functional health in patients with glioma postsurgery, but not to improvements, highlighting the need for further validation in paediatric oncology patients.<sup>20</sup>

ITP is a rare acquired bleeding disorder with an estimated incidence of 5–10 per 100 000 children annually.<sup>21 22</sup> For children with ITP, HRQoL scores are lowest at diagnosis and improve over time, but the disease’s long-term impact remains significant.<sup>23</sup> Lower HRQoL scores have been reported in children receiving treatment at diagnosis, and disease persistence and platelet count reductions at 6 and 12 months have been associated with continued HRQoL impairments. Moreover, children experience greater activity restrictions compared with adults (23.3% vs 9.5%).<sup>24</sup> Chronic ITP significantly impacts HRQoL across multiple domains, including emotional, functional and mental health.<sup>25</sup> Severe fatigue, a frequently reported symptom in 39%–59% of adult patients with ITP, is often underrecognised by healthcare providers.<sup>26</sup> Given the substantial HRQoL burden of ITP, further validation of EQ-5D-Y in this patient population is essential.

Additionally, regarding the impact of a child’s disease burden on adult family members, referred to as spillover effects, these include indirect impacts experienced by caregivers, such as changes in their physical health, emotional well-being, social relationships and work productivity.<sup>27</sup> The the EQ-5D five-level version (EQ-5D-5L) is commonly used to assess these effects, providing insights into the physical and overall health of caregivers, particularly in terms of pain, mobility and daily functioning. However, its ability to capture the broader emotional and social impacts of caregiving, such as stress, anxiety and isolation, is limited.<sup>28</sup> To address this broader construct, the EQ health and well-being (EQ-HWB) instrument was developed, going beyond HRQoL to capture the full spectrum of spillover effects, including physical, emotional and social impacts of caregiving.<sup>29</sup> The difference between the two instruments highlights the importance of selecting appropriate measures for evaluating caregiver burden and spillover effects in both clinical and economic contexts needed.

In this project, we aim to quantitatively and qualitatively assess the psychometric properties of three administration modes of EQ-5D-Y-3L and EQ-5D-Y-5L, that is, SC versus proxy in children aged 8–16 years and IA versus proxy in children aged 5–7 years. The performance of another generic HRQoL instrument, PedsQL, will be used as a comparator. Moreover, we also aim to compare the psychometric performance of EQ-5D-5L and the short version of EQ-HWB (EQ-HWB-9) in capturing spillover effects on family caregivers. In addition to the methodological objectives, this study also aims to report HRQoL outcomes of both children and their caregivers, across three clinical condition groups, including one infectious disease (pneumonia), one rare disease (ITP) and two malignant diseases (CNS solid tumours).

## Methods and analysis

### Study design and setting

This is a prospective observational study being conducted in collaboration with three tertiary hospitals in different cities in China: Guizhou Provincial People’s Hospital, the Affiliated Hospital of Guizhou Medical University and the Children’s Hospital affiliated with Zhejiang University School of Medicine. The study will adhere to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki concerning the involvement of human participants.

We will implement a mixed-methods design, combining both qualitative and quantitative research techniques. The baseline data collection will take 2 years and started on 1 April 2024. We aim to complete the data collection by March 2026.

### Participants

Children aged 5–16 years diagnosed with pneumonia, CNS solid tumours or ITP will be recruited from inpatient and outpatient departments. Family caregivers of these children will also participate by completing proxy versions of the youth instruments and the EQ-5D-5L and EQ-HWB-9 for themselves. Eligible participants must have the ability to understand and complete the questionnaire either through the SC or IA version of EQ-5D-Y-3L, EQ-5D-Y-5L and PedsQL. Participants who have severe cognitive impairments that prevent them from completing the questionnaires or those with other comorbid conditions significantly affecting their HRQoL will be excluded from the study.

Participants will be recruited from both inpatient and outpatient settings to ensure adequate representation across different disease severities and treatment phases, with sufficient disease burden. Upon completion of the baseline survey for the first enrolled patient, a prospective cohort will be established to facilitate follow-up assessments, with a time interval of 2–3 weeks depending on the type of health condition and intervention.

Three distinct health condition groups will be included with standardised severity classifications. Pneumonia severity is determined based on the WHO guidelines,<sup>30</sup> with 50% of cases in this study categorised as severe. CNS solid tumours, including neuroblastoma and Wilms tumour, are classified using the International Neuroblastoma Staging System,<sup>31</sup> with 33% of cases in category 1, 33% in categories 2a and 2b and 33% in categories 3 and 4 or beyond. ITP is classified following the American Society of Haematology guidelines,<sup>32</sup> with equal distribution among mild (33%), moderate (33%) and severe (33%) cases based on platelet count and bleeding symptoms.

### Instruments

#### EQ-5D-Y-3L and EQ-5D-Y-5L

The EQ-5D-Y is a proxy or self-report five-item generic paediatric HRQoL instrument.<sup>33 34</sup> Both have a ‘today’ recall period and cover five dimensions: mobility, looking after self, usual activities, pain/discomfort and worried/sad. Respondents are asked to rate the severity of each item on a three-point scale for the EQ-5D-Y-3L and on a five-point scale for the EQ-5D-Y-5L. The EQ-5D-Y also includes a general health Visual Analogue Scale (EQ VAS). The EQ-5D-Y-3L was adapted from the EQ-5D adult version using cognitive interviews, and the EQ-5D-Y-5L was adapted from the EQ-5D-Y-3L.

#### Paediatric Quality of Life Inventory

The PedsQL generic core 4.0 is a self-report or proxy 23-item generic paediatric HRQoL instrument with five-item response levels and a 1-month recall period, covering four domains: physical functioning, emotional functioning, social functioning and school functioning. Validated versions exist for children aged 2–18 years.<sup>35</sup> The PedsQL 3.0 Cancer Module is a cancer-specific HRQoL instrument for children aged 2–18 years. It includes 27 items across eight domains: pain and hurt, nausea, procedural anxiety, treatment anxiety, worry, cognitive problems, perceived physical appearance and communication.<sup>36</sup> Respondents are asked to rate the frequency of each item over the past month on a five-point scale from 0 (never) to 4 (almost always).

#### EQ-5D-5L and EQ-HWB-9

The EQ-5D-5L measures five HRQoL dimensions (mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort and anxiety/depression) on a 1–5 scale (no problems to unable/extreme problems) and includes a 20-cm EQ VAS (0–100) for self-rated health, using a ‘today’ recall period.<sup>37</sup> The EQ-HWB-9 comprises nine items addressing physical, cognitive and emotional well-being, measured on a five-point Likert scale, with a 7-day recall period.<sup>38</sup> It covers eight domains: mobility, usual activities, energy, cognition, social relationships, control, anxiety/depression and pain. Both instruments have been validated as the HRQoL instrument for parental carers in our previous study.<sup>39</sup>

### Outcomes and measures

The primary outcomes of this study will focus on the psychometric performance of EQ-5D-Y-3L and EQ-5D-Y-5L, including assessments of reliability (test–retest reliability and child-proxy agreement), construct validity (known-groups validity and convergent validity), responsiveness and feasibility. Additionally, the study will compare different administration modes, such as SC versus proxy and IA versus proxy versions, to determine their relative performance.

Secondary outcomes will focus on HRQoL scores obtained from the EQ-5D-Y instruments, the correlation between EQ-5D-Y and PedsQL scores and an analysis of ceiling effects and response distributions. The study will use EQ-5D-Y-3L and EQ-5D-Y-5L in SC, IA and proxy versions, alongside corresponding PedsQL instruments (generic and cancer modules), the EQ-VAS and demographic and clinical data extracted from hospital records. Additionally, the overall health assessment (OHA) will be incorporated.<sup>40</sup> The study will also assess whether EQ-HWB-9 demonstrates superior psychometric properties, particularly in known-groups validity and responsiveness, compared with EQ-5D-5L in capturing spillover effects on family caregivers.

### Sample size

The sample size for this study is determined based on responsiveness analysis of studies conducted in Chinese children using EQ-5D index scores. The calculation follows the formula: n=7.85×2σ²/(μ₂–μ₁)², where μ₁ represents the anticipated mean response at baseline, μ₂ represents the anticipated mean response after treatment and σ is the assumed standard deviation (SD) of response.

The study will apply a level of significance (α) of 5% and a power (β) of 80%. A clinically significant mean difference of 0.05 is assumed, with an SD of 0.1 based on prior studies in Chinese children with idiopathic scoliosis<sup>11 41</sup> and haematological malignancies.<sup>42</sup> Using this formula, the required sample size per subgroup is calculated to be 63 participants. Given an expected follow-up rate of 70%, the final sample size per patient group will be increased to 120 dyads, yielding a total of 360 dyads (patients and their caregivers) across all conditions.

### Data collection

The data collection procedure will include a structured survey administered during both baseline and follow-up assessments, between 2 and 3 weeks. This time interval is chosen to balance an optimal trim frame for both the measurement of reliability and responsiveness.<sup>43</sup> For pneumonia patients, follow-up assessments will be conducted upon hospital discharge for inpatients or during a second outpatient visit. For patients with ITP and CNS solid tumours, follow-up will be scheduled according to disease severity and treatment progression.

The survey will consist of seven key sections: EQ-5D-Y-3L or EQ-5D-Y-5L, PedsQL, EQ VAS, demographic data, OHA and the adult EQ-5D-5L for children aged 12 years and older. Children aged 8 years and older will self-report, while those between 5 and 8 years will be interviewed using the IA version. Caregivers will complete the proxy versions of EQ-5D-Y, PedsQL and SC EQ-5D-5L and EQ-HWB-9, along with demographic and clinical information. Clinical data, including diagnosis, disease stage, medication use and comorbidities, will be obtained from hospital records.

To ensure consistency in administration and independent survey completion by caregivers and children, two trained interviewers (typically a nurse and a medical student) will be assigned to each hospital to conduct the IA version. Interviewers will receive formal training and complete at least three practice interviews before participating in the study.

In addition, a qualitative substudy will be conducted to explore potential ceiling effects and how children interpret EQ-5D-Y-5L responses. A total of 25 children (five per condition group) will be invited to participate in semistructured, face-to-face, one-on-one interviews after completing the questionnaire. To ensure rigour in data collection and analysis, the study will follow the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) checklist.<sup>44</sup> The interview questions, outlined in <a href="#T1" data-ref-type="table">table 1</a>, have been developed by HRQoL experts, as no prior studies have specifically examined this topic. The language has been carefully adapted to enhance comprehension, particularly for younger children (ages 5–10), ensuring they can comfortably understand and respond to the questions. Insights from these interviews will help contextualise the quantitative findings, such as high-ceiling responses or limited known-group validity, by revealing whether they stem from true health status or measurement limitations. Moreover, the qualitative data will be used to identify potential gaps in content coverage (eg, school or social life), misunderstandings of item wording and age-specific or disease-related difficulties in self-reporting. These insights will guide recommendations for refining the EQ-5D-Y-5L and its administration approach in paediatric populations, contributing to its cultural and developmental validity in the Chinese context.

### Statistical analysis plan

The psychometric validation of EQ-5D-Y-3L and EQ-5D-Y-5L will include assessments of feasibility (measured by response completion rates), ceiling effects (percentage of participants reporting no problems in any domain), known-group validity (Cohen’s d effect sizes<sup>45</sup> and ANOVA comparisons across severity levels), test–retest reliability and child-caregiver agreement (intraclass correlation coefficients for summary scores and Gwet’s AC1 for individual dimensions)<sup>46</sup> and responsiveness (Glass’ Δ effect sizes and response means to assess sensitivity to health status changes). The above psychometric properties of EQ-5D-5L and EQ-HWB-9 will be tested using the same statistical methods.

The study will also evaluate the convergent validity of EQ-5D-Y by examining Spearman’s correlations with PedsQL scores. Additionally, the performance of different administration modes will be analysed using Pearson’s correlations, response inconsistencies and comparisons of Level Sum Score and EQ-VAS across SC, IA and proxy versions.

For further analyses, logistic regression models will be used to predict changes in health status, while receiver operating characteristic curves and area under the curve assessments will evaluate the ability of EQ-5D-Y scores to distinguish between different health status groups (eg, improved vs unchanged).

To identify the correlation between health burden in family caregivers and children with three kinds of illness, assessed by both instruments, we will examine how paediatric clinical variables, demographic variables and caregiver burden impact the HRQoL of caregivers. This will involve using regression techniques to analyse the relationship between paediatric clinical variables, demographic variables, caregiver burden (as independent variables) and the index scores of EQ-HWB-9, EQ-5D-5L and EQ VAS (as dependent variables).

Thematic analysis will be used to analyse the interview data, following Braun and Clarke’s six-phase framework.<sup>47</sup> Researchers will first familiarise themselves with the transcripts before coding line by line to identify key patterns related to question comprehension, ease of response and relevance of the EQ-5D-Y dimensions. Focused coding will then group similar codes into broader themes, including response retrieval processes, mapping difficulties and reasons for reporting no problems. Axial coding will be applied to organise themes into conceptual domains, ensuring a structured interpretation of how children understand and respond to EQ-5D-Y items. NVivo software will be used for data management and coding.

### Patient and public involvement

This study adopts a mixed-methods design that actively involves patients and their caregivers in both the design and implementation phases. Input from paediatric patients and caregivers has informed the development of interview materials and the selection of outcome measures. In the qualitative substudy, children and caregivers will provide feedback on the comprehension and relevance of HRQoL items, ensuring that the instruments are appropriate and meaningful in the clinical and cultural context.

## Ethics and dissemination

The study has been approved by three ethics committees: the Guizhou Medical University (IRB: 2024159), the Children’s Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine (IRB: 2024-IRB-0158-P-01) and Guizhou Provincial People’s Hospital (IRB: 2024073). Written informed consent will be secured from caregivers, and assent will be obtained from children aged 8 years and older.

The findings of this study will be disseminated through publications in peer-reviewed journals, presentations at international conferences and contributions to the EuroQol Group’s research initiatives. The data generated will also be integrated into the EQ-5D-Y-3L–EQ-5D-Y-5L crosswalk dataset for future research applications.

## Discussion

This study is expected to have significant implications for the assessment of HRQoL in paediatric populations in China. By validating the EQ-5D-Y-3L and EQ-5D-Y-5L across three distinct paediatric conditions, the findings will provide robust evidence on the psychometric properties of these instruments, thereby supporting their wider adoption in clinical and research settings. Given that the EQ-5D-Y is increasingly used for health technology assessments and economic evaluations, this study will support the EQ-5D-Y to inform decision-making regarding the effectiveness and cost-effectiveness of paediatric healthcare interventions.

One of the key contributions of this study is the examination of different administration modes, including self-complete, interviewer-administered and proxy-reported versions of the EQ-5D-Y. The findings will provide insights into the feasibility and reliability of different data collection approaches, which is particularly important for younger children who may have difficulty completing self-reported measures. As both the different authors and the EuroQol Group have argued, this lack of guidance hampers the valid collection of data in this patient group. Understanding the level of agreement between child self-reports and proxy reports from caregivers will be crucial for interpreting HRQoL data in paediatric health research and practice.

Furthermore, this study will address existing knowledge gaps by comparing the EQ-5D-Y-3L and EQ-5D-Y-5L against other widely used HRQoL measures, such as the PedsQL. This comparative analysis will offer valuable information on the relative sensitivity and discriminative ability of these instruments in different paediatric populations. Such evidence will help guide researchers and clinicians in selecting the most appropriate instrument for assessing HRQoL in children with varying health conditions.

Additionally, by incorporating a diverse sample of paediatric patients from both inpatient and outpatient settings, this study will enhance the generalisability of its findings. The inclusion of children with pneumonia, CNS solid tumours and ITP ensures that the study covers a broad spectrum of paediatric health conditions, ranging from infectious diseases to malignancies and rare disorders. This diversity will allow for a more comprehensive understanding of how HRQoL varies across different disease trajectories and treatment pathways.

Another important contribution of this study is its focus on capturing spillover effects on family caregivers by validating both the EQ-5D-5L and EQ-HWB-9 instruments. Assessing the impact of a child’s health on their caregivers is a critical yet often overlooked aspect of HRQoL research and economic evaluations in paediatrics. By examining how these two instruments perform in measuring caregiver burden, this study will provide valuable insights into their respective strengths and inform future applications. Additionally, understanding the interrelationships between these EuroQol instruments will support the development of more comprehensive assessment strategies and guide future refinements to better capture the broader health and well-being effects within family units.

A key limitation of this study is that recruitment is limited to three hospitals in China, two in Guizhou Province (southwestern China) and one in Zhejiang Province (eastern China), which may affect the generalisability of findings to the broader paediatric population, particularly those in rural or less-resourced settings. Moreover, the cultural, linguistic and systemic variations across China’s vast and diverse population may influence the interpretation and relevance of HRQoL instruments. To mitigate this, we include both inpatient and outpatient participants across different disease severity levels and employ standardised recruitment criteria to enhance the representativeness of the sample. Additionally, while the study employs a mixed-methods approach, potential challenges in the test–retest reliability due to variations in health status over short intervals could influence responsiveness assessments. To address this, the study carefully selects follow-up intervals (2–3 weeks) based on clinical stability considerations and stratifies analyses by disease severity. Lastly, although the study evaluates multiple administration modes and caregiver spillover effects, it does not assess the direct impact of HRQoL instruments on clinical decision-making or long-term health outcomes. However, findings from this study will contribute to the refinement of HRQoL measurement tools and inform future research on their application in clinical and economic evaluations.

In summary, this study will contribute to ongoing international efforts to develop and refine HRQoL measures for paediatric populations. The data generated will not only inform the use of EQ-5D-Y instruments in China but will also add to the global body of evidence on their validity and applicability. The results will be disseminated through peer-reviewed publications, conference presentations and contributions to the EuroQol Group’s research initiatives, thereby fostering further research and collaboration in the field of paediatric HRQoL assessment.
