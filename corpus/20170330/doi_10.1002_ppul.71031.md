# Are EQ‐5D‐Y‐3L and EQ‐5D‐Y‐5L Useful Tools to Assess Health Outcomes in Children With Asthma? An Analysis of Child and Parental Carer Reporting

## Abstract

### Objective

The three‐level and five‐level child‐friendly versions (EQ‐5D‐Y) are widely employed for assessing health‐related quality of life (HRQoL) in children. However, their effectiveness in asthma remains understudied. We aimed to assess their psychometric properties in asthmatic children.

### Methods

Seventy‐six dyads of patients and their parents completed EQ‐5D‐Y‐5L and EQ‐5D‐Y‐3L, respectively, at two visits. Test‐retest reliability and patient‐caregiver agreement were assessed using intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). Known‐groups validity was assessed by comparing scores across varying asthma control levels. Responsiveness was assessed in children showing improved overall health and asthma control.

### Results

ICCs for test‐retest reliability of EQ‐5D‐Y‐3L and EQ‐5D‐Y‐5L summary scores ranged from 0.744 to 0.898 (self‐report) and 0.525 to 0.767 (proxy‐report), indicating satisfactory reliability. As expected, EQ‐5D‐Y summary scores for both versions were better in well‐ or partially‐controlled patients, with fewer problems reported for each dimension as well. Effect sizes ranged from 0.19 to 1.00 for the self‐completed version, from 0.39 to 0.83 for the proxy version, and from 0.26 to 1.40 for EQ VAS in patients showing improvement. Patient‐caregiver agreement ranged from moderate to good.

### Conclusion

The results support the use of the self‐complete and proxy versions of both EQ‐5D‐Y‐3L and EQ‐5D‐Y‐5L to assess health outcomes in children with asthma. The self‐complete EQ‐5D‐Y‐3L is preferred where possible, but parental carer reports can be used as a reasonable proxy when necessary. The EQ VAS offers a valuable complementary perspective on overall health. Further investigation of the EQ‐5D‐Y‐5L is recommended once a value set becomes available.

**Keywords:** EQ‐5D‐Y‐3L, EQ‐5D‐Y‐5L, health‐related quality of life, pediatric asthma, psychometric properties

## Introduction

Health‐related quality of life (HRQoL) questionnaires have become increasingly important instruments in assessing treatment effects and health outcomes in asthma, both in clinical trials \[1\] and routine outcome measurement \[2\]. There are several condition‐specific instruments available for use in adult \[3\] and pediatric \[4\] asthma populations. While condition‐specific instruments are particularly sensitive to specific changes in health, generic instruments designed for a wide range of conditions also play a crucial role. They enable comparisons of conditions and treatment effectiveness across different clinical populations, enriching our understanding of the broader impact of asthma on overall well‐being. Among generic instruments, EQ‐5D stands out as a widely adopted and preference‐weighted generic measure of HRQoL \[5\]. It is extensively used in routine outcome assessments for various conditions, extending beyond its original application in “economic” research \[6\].

Given the widespread use and relevance of EQ‐5D instruments in informing healthcare decision‐making, ensuring their satisfactory measurement properties in the specific populations and conditions of use is crucial. While prior studies have evaluated the reliability, validity, and responsiveness of EQ‐5D in asthma patients \[7, 8\], they mainly focused on the “adult” versions—EQ‐5D‐3L and EQ‐5D‐5L. There lacks research on the suitability of child‐friendly versions—EQ‐5D‐Y‐3L and EQ‐5D‐Y‐5L—in pediatric asthma populations. Although some studies have demonstrated the reliability and validity of EQ‐5D‐Y‐3L in pediatric asthma patients \[9, 10, 11, 12\], evidence on its responsiveness is scarce, and there is no existing data on the performance of EQ‐5D‐Y‐5L in asthma \[13\]. Furthermore, evidence is also lacking on the performance of proxy versions of EQ‐5D‐Y‐3L and EQ‐5D‐Y‐5L, which are crucial for younger children and those having difficulty completing the instruments independently. Acknowledging these gaps, further research on the efficacy of EQ‐5D‐Y in this population is warranted.

This study aimed to concurrently assess the reliability, validity, and responsiveness of both the self‐complete and proxy versions of the EQ‐5D‐Y‐3L and EQ‐5D‐Y‐5L in Chinese children and adolescents with asthma. Inter‐rater agreement between self‐complete and proxy versions was also examined.

## Methods

### Sampling and Study Design

This was a longitudinal non‐randomized study conducted between November 2018 and July 2019 at Renji Academic Hospital, Shanghai, China, focused on pediatric asthma outpatients (aged 8–17) and their parental carers. Inclusion criteria required an asthma diagnosis and the ability to read and understand Chinese. Patients with co‐morbidities such as atopic dermatitis or allergic rhinitis were not excluded, given their common association with asthma and potential impact on overall symptom burden and quality of life. Patients with other unrelated conditions were excluded. The same parent, either father or mother, completed both baseline and follow‐up surveys. The study was approved by Shanghai Jiaotong University ethical board (IRB number: 2018087).

### Procedures

Consenting patients and parental carers independently completed two electronic surveys (baseline and follow‐up) on a tablet in the pediatric clinics. The patient completed: the EQ‐5D‐Y‐5L (including EQ VAS), a 5‐point Overall Health Assessment (OHA) question, and the EQ‐5D‐Y‐3L (without EQ VAS, as it is the same VAS used in the EQ‐5D‐Y‐5L). The EQ‐5D‐Y‐5L was administered before the EQ‐5D‐Y‐3L in both baseline and follow‐up surveys to mitigate memory effect. Parental carers completed: (1) patient socio‐demographic and clinical characteristics; (2) EQ‐5D‐Y‐5L (proxy version, with EQ VAS); (3) OHA (proxy version); (4) parental carer demographics; and (5) EQ‐5D‐Y‐3L (proxy version, without EQ VAS). Clinicians assessed asthma control using GINA criteria \[14\], and a trained interviewer ensured independent survey completion by parents and children.

### Instruments

The EQ‐5D‐Y‐3L is a generic preference‐weighted measure of HRQoL designed for children and adolescents aged 8–15 years, where they rate their HRQoL on the survey day across five dimensions: mobility (walking about), looking after myself (washing or dressing myself), doing usual activities (going to school, hobbies, sports, playing, doing things with family or friends), having pain or discomfort, and feeling worried, sad or unhappy. Each dimension has three response levels: no problems, some problems, and a lot of problems. The EQ‐5D‐Y‐5L covering the same dimensions, provides five response options: no problems, a little bit of a problem, some problems, a lot of problems, and extreme problems/cannot \[15\].

The EQ‐5D‐Y‐3L combines five dimension levels to define 243 unique health states, each represented by a 5‐digit code. For instance, state 11232 indicates no problems with mobility or self‐care, some problems with usual activities, having a lot of pain or discomfort, and feeling a bit worried, sad or unhappy. This is transformed into an index value reflecting the perceived HRQoL on general population preferences. A value set for all potential health states is generated through a standardized valuation exercise. Both versions feature a 20‐cm hash‐marked vertical visual analogue scale (EQ VAS) for overall health rating \[16\]. In this study, Proxy version 1 was used, where parental carers provided their impression of the patient's health on the survey day.

The validated Overall Health Assessment question (OHA) assesses subjective health by asking “How is your overall health today?” The OHA does not come in a standard form. In this study, we used the outcomes “excellent,” “good,” “fair,” “poor,” and “very poor” \[17\]. It was used to test the validity and reliability of the EQ‐5D‐Y‐3L in multinational studies \[18, 19\], with a proxy version to test the validity and reliability of the EQ‐5D‐Y‐3L in multinational studies, a proxy version capturing caregivers' perspectives on patient health.

Global Initiative for Asthma (GINA) criteria assess asthma control based on cough and wheezing over the past 4 weeks: daytime symptoms \> 2 times/week, night awakenings, \> 2 relief medication uses/week, and activity limitations. Good control meets none of these criteria, partial control meets 1–2, and poor control meets 3–4 \[14\].

### Data Analysis

We assessed test‐retest reliability, known‐groups validity, and responsiveness of the EQ‐5D‐Y preference‐weighted index score, level sum score (LSS) for EQ‐5D‐Y‐3L and EQ‐5D‐Y‐5L, and EQ VAS (collectively referred to as “EQ‐5D‐Y summary scores”).

The Chinese EQ‐5D‐Y‐3L value set \[20\] was used to compute the preference‐weighted index (range: ‐0.088 to 1, higher values denoting better health). The no value set is available for EQ‐5D‐Y‐5L, LSS was computed as an alternative. The LSS, demonstrated to perform comparably to the Asthma Quality of Life Questionnaire (AQLQ) total score \[21\], orders respondents along a latent health variable, with higher scores indicating poorer health \[22\]. For coding, “no problem” = 1 to “extreme problems” = 5 cross five EQ‐5D‐Y‐5L dimensions, yielding a LSS range of 5 (best health)–25 (worst health). The same coding protocol was applied to EQ‐5D‐Y‐3L LSS.

Baseline health status was presented as the proportion of EQ‐5D‐Y‐3L and EQ‐5D‐Y‐5L responses per dimension. Ceiling effects were identified by respondents reporting no problems (11111) across all dimensions.

We assessed test‐retest reliability of EQ‐5D‐Y summary scores in stable patients using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC ≥ 0.7 for acceptable reliability) \[23\] and agreement percentage for individual dimensions. Patient‐caregiver agreement on EQ‐5D‐Y‐3L and EQ‐5D‐Y‐5L summary scores was also assessed using ICC with baseline data.

Known‐groups validity, that is, the extent to which EQ‐5D‐Y were able to distinguish between patient sub‐groups expected to show differences in HRQoL, was assessed using baseline data to compare “no problems” proportions on individual dimensions and summary scores among asthma control levels (well controlled/partially controlled vs. uncontrolled per GINA). Higher control levels were expected to show fewer problems, higher index/EQ VAS scores and lower LSS, indicating better HRQoL. Group differences were tested using two‐sample *t*‐tests, Mann‐Whitney U tests and Cohen's d effect size (ES = difference of mean/pooled SD) was used to evaluate the size of any differences \[24\]. Chi‐square and Fisher's exact tests were used for dimension‐level analysis, with odds ratios (ORs) calculated for “no problem” versus “any problems” in each dimension.

Responsiveness of EQ‐5D‐Y summary scores was assessed in patients who improved at least one level on OHA or GINA from baseline to follow‐up. Changes in “no problems” proportions were analyzed with chi‐square tests and odds ratios. Glass' Δ effect sizes (ES = mean difference/baseline SD) indicated change magnitude in summary scores (0.20–0.49: modest, 0.5–0.79: moderate, ≥ 0.8: good).

Direct comparison of index scores and LSS is unfeasible due to their weighted and unweighted scales and differing severity levels in EQ‐5D‐Y‐3L and EQ‐5D‐Y‐5L. Therefore, we used ICCs and effect sizes (ES) for comparisons, applying statistical methods suited for scale differences. Analyses employed IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 26.0).

## Results

A total of 76 pediatric outpatients and their parental carers participated in the baseline survey (Table <a href="#ppul71031-tbl-0001" data-ref-type="table">1</a>). 70 (92.1%) patients completed follow‐up, with a mean (SD) interval of 42.0 (21.3) days. According to GINA, 41% had uncontrolled asthma at first visit. Control improved in 37.1% patients by second visit, remained the same in 55.7% and worsened in 7.1%. At the first visit, 56% rated their health as good/excellent on the OHA, with 34.3% reporting improvement, 55.7% unchanged, and 10% deterioration by the second visit.

Baseline health status evaluated by EQ‐5D‐Y‐3L and EQ‐5D‐Y‐5L (Figure <a href="#ppul71031-fig-0001" data-ref-type="fig">1a, b</a>) showed over two‐thirds of patients reporting no problems in each dimension, with approximately half reporting no problems across five dimensions. The self‐complete EQ‐5D‐Y‐5L showed a slightly higher ceiling effect (59.2%) than the EQ‐5D‐Y‐3L (57.9%). Regarding the response distribution across three levels of asthma control (Table <a href="#ppul71031-tbl-0005" data-ref-type="table">A1</a>), the largest increase in reported problems between well‐controlled and uncontrolled patients was observed in “doing usual activity” and “feeling worried/sad/unhappy” dimensions. The EQ‐5D‐Y‐5L is particularly effective at detecting more severe health differences.

## Discussion

Previous studies show acceptable measurement properties for self‐complete EQ‐5D‐Y‐3L in pediatric asthma \[9, 10, 11, 12, 13\]. However, evidence on proxy versions and EQ‐5D‐Y‐5L is scarce. This study is the first so far to comprehensively examine the measurement properties of these versions in pediatric asthma, crucially considering a substantial population of approximately 5.16 million affected children in China \[25\]. Our findings demonstrate that the EQ‐5D‐Y‐3L exhibits favorable test‐retest reliability, construct validity, responsiveness and good patient‐caregiver agreement, supporting its use as a tool for assessing asthma children's HRQoL. Despite the limitation of lacking available preference weights, the EQ‐5D‐Y‐5L and EQ VAS also performed well, highlighting their potential value in clinical applications.

Figure <a href="#ppul71031-fig-0001" data-ref-type="fig">1a, b</a> illustrate that “pain/discomfort” and “worried, sad, unhappy” are the most affected dimensions in asthma patients, with 36.8% and 22.4% reporting problems on EQ‐5D‐Y‐3L. In comparison, a general population of primary school children in China reported 22.7% and 21.3% problems using self‐complete EQ‐5D‐Y‐3L \[26\]. The frequencies of problems were similar for “mobility”, “looking after myself”, and “usual activities” in both populations (5.3%, 1.3%, and 9.2% in the asthma population compared to 3.3%, 4.4%, and 9.7% in the general population). This might be expected because asthma was well‐ or partially‐controlled in almost 60% of our sample, aligning closely with the general population sample. Surprisingly, the self‐completed EQ‐5D‐Y‐5L detected slightly fewer health problems than the EQ‐5D‐Y‐3L, contrary to its intent to reduce the EQ‐5D‐Y‐3L ceiling effect. This unexpected finding may be attributed to initial misunderstanding or underreporting in the EQ‐5D‐Y‐5L that clarified in the subsequent EQ‐5D‐Y‐3L assessment (which came second). Further research with larger samples is needed to explore this trend's generalizability.

Both EQ‐5D‐Y‐3L and EQ‐5D‐Y‐5L self‐complete versions demonstrated favorable test‐retest reliability and known‐groups validity across summary scores (index score, LSS and EQ VAS). Our findings aligned with a Spanish study (ICC: 0.70–0.81; ES: 0.38–0.66) on persistent asthmatic children, consistently identifying issues in “doing usual activity”, “pain/discomfort”, and “worried/sad/unhappy” \[13\]. This supports prior research showing a high prevalence of these dimensions among elementary students with frequent allergic wheezing \[12\]. The EQ VAS showed slightly weaker discrimination, however, which may be due to children taking into account a wider range of health attributes when using the EQ VAS than those covered by the five dimensions or to the presence of coping strategies in children with uncontrolled asthma which lead them to perceive themselves as fairly healthy, even when experiencing some health problems. The EQ‐5D‐Y‐3L showed slightly better discrimination between asthma control groups based on LSS, while the EQ‐5D‐Y‐5L had slightly better test‐retest reliability. Future studies should assess EQ‐5D‐Y‐5L index values to confirm if these findings are influenced by the LSS approach. Additionally, it is worth mentioning that the difference in recall periods between GINA (last 4 weeks) and EQ‐5D‐Y (today) might introduce response variability, potentially resulting in an overestimation of perceived well‐being reported by EQ‐5D‐Y respondents in known‐groups validity assessment based on GINA evaluation.

Both self‐complete EQ‐5D‐Y‐3L and EQ‐5D‐Y‐5L demonstrated satisfactory responsiveness to improvement over time, with an increase in “no problems” reported for each dimension and improved summary scores observed. Specifically, when using the OHA as an external criterion, all the summary scores showed statistically significant changes over time, with large ESs for EQ‐5D‐Y‐3L index (1.00) and EQ VAS (1.40) and moderate ES for EQ‐5D‐Y‐5L LSS (0.50). However, with GINA as the criterion, changes were smaller (ESs: 0.19–0.29). These results aligned with the Spanish study that the ES for the EQ‐5D‐Y‐3L index score (0.29) in our study was slightly smaller than the result (0.38) based on Asthma Control Questionnaire (ACQ) criteria. However, the ES of EQ VAS was considerably larger (1.40 vs. 0.66) \[13\]. Furthermore, the ES of EQ‐5D‐Y‐5L was similar to findings in a German study of adult asthma patients, where EQ‐5D‐5L was moderately responsive to Asthma Control Test (ACT) changes at 7–19 weeks post‐intervention \[27\]. The results indicate that the EQ VAS is highly sensitive to capturing changes in asthma control, with EQ‐5D‐Y‐3L showing slightly better responsiveness than EQ‐5D‐Y‐5L, particularly when using OHA as an external criterion.

Given its favorable performance in test‐retest reliability, known‐groups validity, and responsiveness, the self‐report EQ‐5D‐Y‐3L is a valid tool for assessing HRQoL in pediatric asthma. Its sensitivity to clinically defined asthma control (strong responsiveness with OHA and moderate with GINA) makes it useful for capturing health impacts beyond specific asthma symptoms, providing a broad view of overall well‐being from the child's perspective. The EQ‐5D‐Y‐5L, with its finer gradation, is particularly valuable for capturing a child's subjective health assessment, especially in dimensions like “usual activity” and “worried/sad/unhappy” where differences become more pronounced between well‐controlled and uncontrolled asthma. This sensitivity extends to emotional and psychological aspects that may not align directly with clinical metrics like GINA. However, to fully exploit the potential of the EQ‐5D‐Y‐5L, a weighted index score is warranted. The EQ VAS, with its strong reliability and responsiveness, complements both measures by providing a quick, holistic view of patient‐perceived health. It is particularly valuable in regular follow‐ups, as shifts in overall HRQoL are as relevant as changes in asthma symptoms, allowing clinicians to adopt a comprehensive, patient‐centered approach to asthma management in children.

Our study also generally provided support for the known‐groups validity of the proxy versions of EQ‐5D‐Y‐3L and EQ‐5D‐Y‐5L. However, it is worth noting that the ICCs fell below the recommended cut‐point of 0.70, indicating potential limitations in the test‐retest reliability. ESs for EQ‐5D‐Y‐3L index score and EQ VAS demonstrated moderate to strong responsiveness using the OHA and the GINA rating as external criteria of change in health status. The EQ‐5D‐Y‐5L, theoretically expected to be more sensitive to small changes in health status, exhibited slightly weaker performance compared to EQ‐5D‐Y‐3L in terms of LSS. Whether this is due to factors such as the relatively small sample size available to test responsiveness and/or the use of the LSS rather than an index score to summarize responses requires further investigation. On the other hand, in comparison to the self‐completed version, the proxy version exhibited greater responsiveness to GINA‐defined improvement. This is attributed to parents being able to capture nuances of asthma control that patients may not accurately report, such as nighttime symptoms or activity limitations that occur when the child is not fully aware or awake. This underscores the importance of integrating parental carer or proxy input alongside patient self‐reporting to achieve a more comprehensive and accurate assessment of asthma control.

Finally, patients and their parental carers showed strong agreement on the five dimensions for EQ‐5D‐Y‐3L, the EQ‐5D‐Y‐3L index score and EQ VAS. However, agreement was less pronounced regarding the LSS for both EQ‐5D‐Y‐3L and EQ‐5D‐Y‐5L.

This study had several limitations. First, the sample size was only moderate which prevented an analysis of responsiveness in patients with worsened health status, though it was sufficient to assess construct validity and test‐retest reliability, and responsiveness in patients showing improved health. Moreover, the age distribution is skewed towards younger children. It would be fair to assert that further research, particularly within the age range of 12–16, would be beneficial. Second, all participants were recruited from one hospital in Shanghai, which has a high socioeconomic status. As asthma and the control of asthma is related to social economic status \[2\], the results presented here might not be generalizable to other regions or countries. Third, the EQ‐5D‐Y‐5L was assessed using a level sum score which may behave differently from the preference‐based index score. LSS may slightly overestimate test‐retest reliability and underestimate known‐groups validity and responsiveness due to its aggregated scoring. By combining all dimensions into a single score, LSS can mask minor variations within individual dimensions over time, making the total score appear more stable than it is, thereby inflating reliability. It may also miss subtle but clinically relevant differences across health dimensions, reducing its sensitivity to distinguish between groups with different health statuses and limiting its responsiveness to detect small, meaningful changes in specific dimensions. Once a EQ‐5D‐Y‐5L value set becomes available for China, a preference‐based index score will be used to enhance sensitivity to these differences. A final limitation is that there might have been an ordering effect by always presenting the EQ‐5D‐Y‐5L first. The disparity in recall periods between the GINA questionnaire (covering the last 4 weeks) and the EQ‐5D‐Y‐3L/EQ‐5D‐Y‐5L instruments (assessing health status today) may introduce response variability, potentially leading to an overestimation of perceived well‐being reported by EQ‐5D‐Y‐3L/EQ‐5D‐Y‐5L respondents in known‐groups validity assessment.

In conclusion, this study contributes to the growing body of evidence on the measurement properties of the youth version of the EQ‐5D in pediatric asthma. The results show that both the self‐complete and proxy versions of the EQ‐5D‐Y‐3L and EQ‐5D‐Y‐5L appear to be reliable and valid for assessing health outcomes of asthmatic children. The EQ‐5D‐Y‐3L also showed acceptable responsiveness and patient‐proxy agreement, while further research is required on those properties for the EQ‐5D‐Y‐5L. Based on the findings of this study, researchers aiming to apply EQ‐5D‐Y‐3L and EQ‐5D‐Y‐5L in pediatric asthma research for both clinical applications and economic evaluations should prioritize the self‐complete EQ‐5D‐Y‐3L version, as it is currently the only version with an available Index score. The EQ VAS also serves as a valuable complementary tool, providing a holistic view of patient‐perceived health beyond symptom‐specific concerns. Further testing of the EQ‐5D‐Y‐5L in asthma patients will be beneficial when a value set becomes available, enabling the calculation of Index scores and enhancing its applicability in economic assessments.
