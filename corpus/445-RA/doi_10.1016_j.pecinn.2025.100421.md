# Usability testing of an individualized decision aid for total knee arthroplasty

## Abstract

### Objectives

Osteoarthritis (OA) is a leading cause of total knee arthroplasty (TKA), affecting over 15 % of Canadians. With an aging population and suboptimal use of non-surgical options, TKA rates and wait times are rising. Although TKA is effective, 30 % of patients are dissatisfied due to unmet expectations, suggesting some surgeries may be inappropriate. Patient decision aids can set realistic expectations, improve decision quality, and enhance satisfaction. We developed an *individualized* online patient decision aid allowing patients to compare treatment outcomes based on similar characteristics (age, sex and body mass index) and evaluated its usability before clinical implementation.

### Methods

Participants were recruited from a high-volume urban hip and knee clinic. Eligible adults diagnosed with knee OA completed the decision aid online and subsequently filled out demographics and survey forms, including the Preparation for Decision Making Scale (PDMS), System Usability Scale (SUS), and Acceptability Scale. Data were analyzed using descriptive statistics and content analysis of open-ended responses.

### Results

There were 20 participants (mean age 68 years, 65 % female). The average PDMS score was 66.4, indicating above-average preparedness for decision-making. The SUS score averaged 63.4, suggesting marginal usability. Females and participants under 70 years reported higher PDMS and SUS scores. Most participants rated the information presentation as “good” or “excellent,” with 75 % finding the decision aid's length appropriate and information balanced. Feedback highlighted the need to simplify content, reduce variables, and offer the aid earlier in treatment.

### Conclusions

The decision aid demonstrated reasonable usability, acceptability, and usefulness for routine practice. Future research should explore its impact on long-term patient outcomes and satisfaction, including among non-surgical populations.

### Practice implications

Incorporating this decision aid into routine practice can help patients set realistic expectations and make informed decisions, reducing dissatisfaction. Offering it earlier in the patient journey may enhance its impact, especially for non-surgical options.

## Introduction

Osteoarthritis (OA), the major reason for total knee replacement surgery, affects over 15 % of Canadians, mostly women, the elderly, and low-income earners \[1\]. Nearly 15 % of patients with OA will require total knee arthroplasty (TKA) \[1\]. With Canada's aging population and the suboptimal use of non-surgical interventions such as pain-relieving medications and physical therapy, the number of individuals requiring TKA continues to rise, resulting in increased wait times for surgery \[1,2\]. Despite the proven effectiveness of TKA, up to 30 % of individuals who undergo surgery will be dissatisfied with their surgical outcomes partly due to unrealistic expectations, suggesting that surgical intervention may have been inappropriate for some patients \[\[3\], \[4\], \[5\], \[6\]\].

Patient decision aids for TKA help patients set realistic expectations with improvements in decision quality, satisfaction with the decision and overall care \[\[7\], \[8\], \[9\]\]. By completing a decision aid, patients weigh the risks and benefits of surgical versus non-surgical interventions and engage in shared decision-making with their care provider \[7\]. This can reduce the number of inappropriate patients proceeding to surgery \[7\]. However, most decision aids for TKA are based on information about “average” risk and are limited in the outcomes that influence patients' day-to-day life \[\[7\], \[8\], \[9\]\].

To address these limitations, we developed an *individualized* online patient decision aid allowing patients to consider individuals with similar characteristics (i.e. age, sex, and body mass index (BMI)) to compare the likelihood of outcomes (1-year post surgery) to their unique situation \[10,11\]. Evidence from a pragmatic randomized control trial showed that patients who completed this decision aid, which includes a summary report, had twice the odds of making quality decisions about TKA (OR = 2.08, 95 % CI: 1.08 to 4.02) and fewer underwent surgery (71 % vs 83 %), suggesting that patients may have tried non-surgical therapies or adjusted their expectations of surgery \[12,13\]. In that study, quality decisions were defined as patients feeling more informed and having a treatment option congruent with their treatment preference \[12,13\]. With these positive results, plans are underway to implement this individualized decision aid into routine clinical practice in Alberta, which has a publicly funded healthcare system.

The original individualized TKA decision aid was developed according to International Patient Decision Aid Standards (IPDAS) \[10,14\] and underwent usability testing. Based on our BEST-Knee study, we have since modified the decision aid to addresses patient expectations of surgery related to: pain relief; kneeling function; mental wellbeing; functional improvement; and overall wellbeing \[3,13\]. Additionally, we enhanced the presentation of patient-reported outcome (i.e., EQ-5D-5L) data within the decision aid based on patient feedback \[15\]. We added questions to help patients consider the trade-off of wait times with certain aspects of surgery and non-surgical treatments, such as pain/discomfort and mobility. Lastly, we used data visualization techniques \[\[16\], \[17\], \[18\], \[19\]\] to improve the summary report for patient use. The objective of this study is to evaluate the usability of the revised individualized decision aid prior to its large-scale implementation in routine clinical practice.

## Methods

### Design

In collaboration with clinic staff, participants were recruited from a high-volume, publicly funded, urban hip and knee clinic after their surgical screening appointment from April to May 2024. A sample of 10–20 participants has been shown to identify 80–95 % of usability issues in decision aids \[20\]. Eligible participants were ≥ 18 years old; diagnosed with knee osteoarthritis (OA), and referred to the clinic as possible candidates for surgery; and could read, speak, and understand English and provide informed consent. People with soft tissue knee injuries, rheumatoid arthritis, or other non-OA knee conditions was excluded from this evaluation, as they were not intended participants for the decision aid in this care pathway. Potential participants were approached by a researcher after their surgical screening appointment to inform them of the study, obtain written consent, and provide the URL to complete the online decision aid at home to reflect routine use in clinical practice as intended.

### Intervention

The decision aid is described in detail elsewhere \[10,11\]. Briefly, it provides comprehensive information about OA and outlines TKA and non-surgical treatments (e.g. weight loss, physical therapy, and pain medication) in several areas including: health-related quality of life - EQ-5D \[21\] (mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort, anxiety/depression); chance of revision surgery; anticipated recovery period; and risks. Individualized EQ-5D data are available for pre-surgery and 1-year post surgery. The decision aid also generates a summary report, summarizing the individuals' responses and comparisons, with an option to print or save the report.

### Data collection

Upon completion of the decision aid, participants received a hyperlink to self-complete an online demographics form (age, sex, gender identity, and ethnic background) and evaluation survey (Supplementary File 1). The survey, hosted on Qualtrics \[22\], included three validated instruments to assess the usability, usefulness, and acceptability of the decision aid. The 10-item Preparation for Decision Making Scale (PDMS) assessed the decision aid's usefulness in preparing patients for healthcare provider consultations and making healthcare decisions, using a Likert scale from ‘not at all’ to ‘a great deal’ \[\[23\], \[24\], \[25\]\]. Scores range from 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating greater perceived preparedness. The 10-item System Usability Scale (SUS) used a Likert scale from ‘strongly disagree’ to ‘strongly agree’ and scores range from 0 to 100, with a cut off score of 68 indicating average usability \[26,27\]. The SUS included one open-ended question. The Acceptability Scale evaluated how well information was presented and included two open-ended questions \[28\]. Items were analyzed individually as there is no total score. Additionally, we developed and included three open-ended questions specific to the summary report. All open-ended questions were optional. Participants who did not complete the online survey within one week received up to two weekly reminders via email.

### Data analysis

Closed-ended data were analyzed using descriptive statistics. We used mean and standard deviation (SD) to summarize age and frequencies and percentages to summarize categorical outcomes. Responses to SUS and PDMS were scored according to the developers' guidelines, with scores ranging from 0 to 100 \[24,27\]. SUS scores were categorized into usability levels: not acceptable (\<50), low marginal (50–62.6), high marginal (\>62.6–70), and acceptable (\>70) \[26,29\]. Results were stratified for age (based on median) and sex. Quantitative analysis was conducted using Stata version 16.1 \[30\].

Open-ended data was managed using Microsoft Excel, analyzed using content analysis \[31\], and reported by main topic areas, not individual survey question. We presented results that provide additional context or insights beyond what was captured and reported from closed-ended questions. For example, comments such as “Easy to understand” or “It was too long” were excluded as they were addressed by specific closed-ended questions. Illustrative comments were labeled with a study ID (e.g., 001).

We identified four main topics from the open-ended data: simplification, information provided, personalization, and timing.

Participants suggested simplifying the decision aid, stating *“the information could have been given much more straightforwardly*” (006) and to consider the needs of older adults: *“older seniors would have difficulty interpreting some of the data*” (005). Recommendations included “*reducing the number of variables*” (022) and shortening it (018). For the summary report, participants suggested revising “*confusing*” (005) sentences, including the sentence about willingness to trade wait time for symptom relief (006).

Participants found the information in the decision aid and summary report helpful (e.g. “*good to know*” (009) or “*made me think about the complete picture, not just the goal of lessening pain*” (015)). They appreciated comparing treatment outcomes (e.g. *“\[I liked\] the comparison between surgical and non surgical*” (008), “*data on post-surgical results”* (005)) or themselves to others (e.g. “*How I compare to other Albertans*” (019)). However, some found the information challenging (e.g. “*Helpful, not easy*” (004)) particularly when it contradicted patient expectations: *“\[The\] numbers were not as good as expected after knee surgery*” (010).

Regarding personalization, some participants wanted more individualized content. Suggestions including “*More specific lifestyle areas… \[and\] numbers of my age and BMI categories reflecting activity levels, nutrition, etc.* (008); “*narrowed age and severity field… e.g. age 60-64 and 65-69 as having it 60-70 seemed like a big age difference*” (001); and incorporating “*Testimonials from real patients who have actually experienced the procedure \[surgery\]*” (010). Some participants preferred direct communication with their surgeon over the self-administered decision aid: *“I would still rather speak to the surgeon*” (004).

Participants commented on the timing of completing the decision aid and accompanying summary report. Some found it too late, as they had already decided on treatment: “*I had already chosen surgery, because I spent years trying other treatments that didn't work for me*” (023). Others suggested it would be more useful *“at the start of knee problems rather than when you're already at the point of surgery*” (015). However, some found the timing ideal (e.g. “*relevant to where I am in my treatment decision*” (019)) or confirmed their treatment decision: “*Confirmed what I needed done \[or\]…what other doctors said*” (013). However, one participant indicated continued uncertainty about their decision: “*still so unsure*” (004). Participants also expressed interest in revisiting the decision aid or summary report to “*review my decision*” (021) or as their condition changes (e.g., “*as my knee becomes more debilitating as I assume the time will come when I will choose surgery*” (001)). A “*two year follow-up*” (017) was also recommended.

## Discussion and conclusion

### Discussion

The original individualized TKA decision aid was developed according to IPDAS. Our study evaluated the usability of the revised individualized decision aid prior to its broader implementation in routine clinical practice. Overall, we found that the decision aid is reasonable in terms of usability, usefulness, and acceptability. The overall mean score of 66.4 on the PDMS indicates that participants felt prepared to make treatment decisions after using the aid, aligning with previous research suggesting that decision aids enhance patients' preparedness and confidence \[32,33\]. However, some participants found that while the amount of information was helpful, it did not make their decision easier particularly when the information contradicted their expectations. This observation highlights the potential for decision aids to increase cognitive load by presenting extensive data \[34,35\], underscoring the need to balance thoroughness with clarity.

The acceptability of the decision aid was high, with most participants rating the presentation of information as “good” or “excellent” across all content areas. A majority (75 %) found the decision aid appropriate in length and balanced in presentation, reflecting satisfaction with its comprehensiveness and clarity, crucial for informed decision-making \[36\]. The System Usability Scale (SUS) showed a mean score of 63.4, indicating that the decision aid is sufficiently usable. In the broader context of medical decision aids, the mean SUS score is comparable to that found in usability testing of a decision aid for people with multiple sclerosis \[37\].

The overall average scores for the PDMS and SUS were not high, with the SUS average below the suggested cut point for average usability. However, participants younger than 70 years and females reported higher PDMS and SUS scores, suggesting that these groups found the decision aid more useful and user-friendly. This emphasizes the importance of tailoring digital health tools to different demographic groups to improve usability and address the digital divide between younger and older people \[38\]. Simplifying the decision aid by making information more straightforward and reducing the number of variables can help make it more accessible, especially for older adults who may struggle with complex data, as the target population for this decision aid is generally for older people.

Participants generally appreciated the decision aid's information, particularly the comparisons between surgical and non-surgical outcomes. However, at times the information about possible treatment outcomes did not aligned with some participants' perceived expectations. This highlights the importance of presenting balanced, realistic outcomes to manage patient expectations effectively \[39\]. Furthermore, participants expressed a preference for direct communication with their surgeons, underscoring the decision aid's role as a complement to, not a replacement for, personalized medical consultations \[40\] and shared decision-making.

Opinions on the timing of the decision aid were mixed. Some participants felt it was too late, having already made their decision, while others suggested it would be more useful earlier in their treatment journey. This feedback supports the recommendation for flexibility in the timing of administering decision aids to match patients' decision-making stages \[41\]. In addition, our results contribute to the literature by showing that patients would like opportunities to revisit their responses and/or repeat the decision aid at different times in their OA treatment journey.

While our study provided valuable insights, it had some limitations. The decision aid was given to participants after their surgical screening appointment rather than prior to it, as is intended in routine implementation. This timing discrepancy may have influenced the participants' experiences and feedback. We recruited participants from one out of the 12 surgical clinics in Alberta. While this may raise concerns about generalizability, we believe our findings are representative of the broader patient population considering TKA in the province, as the demographics of this clinic's patients are similar to those across Alberta. Similarly, the recruitment of participants that are English language and computer literate reflects the patient population that will use the decision aid in routine clinical practice. However, this also means our findings may not be applicable to non-English-speaking or less technologically proficient patients, highlighting a need for future research to explore the decision aid's effectiveness across more diverse populations. Although the original decision aid was based on IPDAS standards, future research should focus on refining the decision aid based on user feedback and expanding its reach to diverse patient populations.

### Innovation

Our study confirmed the usability of an individualized total knee arthroplasty (TKA) decision aid, demonstrating its suitability for routine clinical use for patients considering TKA. This individualized decision aid is innovative in several ways. First, unlike the typical practice of decision aids presenting outcomes for an “average” patient, the individualized decision aid allows for personalized outcome comparisons based on age, sex, and BMI. Furthermore, incorporation of historical patient-reported outcome measures in a decision aid is a novel micro-level application that enhances patients' understanding of potential treatment outcomes, thereby supporting their decision-making. Lastly, we employed novel data visualization techniques throughout the decision aid (e.g. use of the Sankey Diagram to provide patients with a clearer representation of changes in outcomes over time).

### Conclusion

Our usability testing of the individualized decision aid for patients considering TKA demonstrated its high perceived usefulness, marginal usability, and high acceptability. Therefore, we do not recommend any further revisions to the decision aid prior to its implementation into routine practice. Our results highlight the importance of personalization and timely administration of decision aids to support informed patient decision-making. There are plans for future longitudinal studies with both surgical and non-surgical populations to assess long-term impacts of the decision aid on patient-reported outcome measures and decision satisfaction.

### Practice implications

Incorporating this decision aid into routine clinical practice could help patients set realistic expectations and make informed decisions about their care, potentially reducing dissatisfaction with surgical outcomes. To maximize its impact, clinicians should consider introducing the decision aid earlier in the patient journey, particularly for those exploring non-surgical options.
