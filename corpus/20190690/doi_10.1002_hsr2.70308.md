# Using Patient‐Reported Outcomes in Two Program Examples Measuring Health Status in Primary Care: An Observational Study

## Abstract

### Background and Aims

Patient‐reported outcome measures (PROMs) are increasingly used in primary care practices; however, the broad characteristics of this population pose unique challenges. Generic PROMs (e.g., EQ‐5D‐5L) measure general health status from the patient's perspective, contributing towards a comprehensive evaluation of health programming. This study describes the integration of the EQ‐5D‐5L within program evaluation in primary care in Alberta, Canada, using two program examples.

### Methods

A retrospective, longitudinal, observational design was used. The Edmonton O‐day'min Primary Care Network (EOPCN) routinely collects the EQ‐5D‐5L in their exercise and active living and nutrition programs. Data collected between January 1, 2021 and March 31, 2022 was analyzed descriptively, by dimension, index, and visual analog scale (VAS) scores. Minimally important differences were used to interpret index (0.04) and VAS scores (7.0) and the Pareto Classification of Health Change was applied to the dimensions. Effect size of change in index and VAS scores and population comparisons were examined.

### Results

In the exercise and active living program (*n* = 301), 72% were female with a mean age of 57 (±16) years, and 90.2% reported any problems (levels 2–5) in pain/discomfort. The mean index and VAS scores were 0.74 (±0.18) and 66.5 (±18.9), respectively. Among those with repeated measurement (n = 112), the greatest improvement was in usual activities (17%); the greatest deterioration was in pain/discomfort (25%). In the nutrition program (*n* = 573), 71% were female with a mean age of 50 (±17) years, and 75.9% reported any problems in pain/discomfort. The mean index and VAS scores were 0.77 (±0.19) and 70.1 (±15.6), respectively. Among those with repeated measurement (*n* = 212), the greatest improvement was in anxiety/depression (16%); the greatest deterioration was in pain/discomfort (26%). In both, health status was lower than the general Alberta population norms. No baseline characteristics were found to be statistically significant.

### Conclusion

These results inform future programming, to help patient needs.

## Introduction

Patient outcomes are measured using a variety of tools, techniques, and data points, however the majority of these are from the health care system perspective. Historically, patient‐reported outcomes measures (PROMs) have been used in clinical trials as secondary outcomes, documenting the patient's perspective towards an intervention, device, or medical treatment \[1\]. PROMs are standardized, validated questionnaires completed by patients (or a proxy on their behalf), to measure health status, functionality, or health‐related quality of life \[2\]. They can be generic in nature, or disease‐specific depending on the population and outcome of interest \[2\]. Often different versions of the same instrument exist, balancing the length of the instrument with the information warranted \[2\]. There are over 1000 PROMs available to measure various health outcomes, including health‐related quality of life and well‐being \[3\]. They vary in terms of their specificity (generic vs. disease specific) and focus (e.g., physical, mental or social health). Some common examples of generic PROMs include the EQ‐5D \[4\], 12‐item Short form Survey (SF‐12) \[5\]; examples of disease or condition specific PROMs include Edmonton Symptom Assessment System (ESAS) \[6\], Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ‐9) \[7\], and General Anxiety Disorder‐7 (GAD‐7) \[8\]. All measure various dimensions of health depending on the purpose and scope of the tool, as well as perform differently in terms of psychometric properties in different populations \[9\].

There are three different levels for the use of PROMs data throughout healthcare settings \[10\]. At the micro level, data is used at the individual patient level to inform clinical care and management. Here, PROMs provide an opportunity for patients to inform their own care and communicate with healthcare providers in a systematic way \[10\]. For example, screening or monitoring for depressive symptoms to inform clinical decision making and treatment options. At the meso level, PROMs data is examined aggregately to identify certain outcomes or symptoms in a group of patients and is used to compare or monitor patient outcomes across the group. This could be a panel of patients, a certain clinic or treatment site or a program \[10\]. For example, assessing pain or discomfort among patients with osteoarthritis to inform program planning/resource allocation. Using PROMs at the macro level is a high‐level examination across jurisdictions. PROMs can be used to compare and benchmark processes or outcomes such as the performance of healthcare providers. More commonly, they can also be used to evaluate cost and value of health services at a broader system level \[10\]. For example, examining the health status of patients using the emergency room across the province and identifying the sites/regions that require additional resources.

More recently, PROMs have been incorporated into primary care practices at the micro, meso and macro levels (Figure <a href="#hsr270308-fig-0001" data-ref-type="fig">1</a>) \[11\], however the broad characteristics of this population poses unique challenges to their implementation. Primary care practices must address a wide range of diseases, many with mild and indistinguishable symptoms \[12\]. For this reason, the selection of the appropriate PROM(s) is critical to match the purpose of measurement and at what level the data will be used. In Alberta, more momentum and use of PROMs are being reported in primary care, mainly at the meso level for program evaluation purposes \[11\]. It is this integration of the patients' perspective that is critical to identify unmet needs and tailor interventions to improve care \[13\]. Regular assessment of PROMs enables the evaluation of program effectiveness, supports shared decision‐making and informs policy by reflecting the patient's perspective \[14\].

Primary care programs aim to enhance overall well‐being by addressing physical, mental, and social aspects of health, recognizing that health is a holistic concept. This is critical to incorporate into primary care program evaluation \[15\]. Primary care program evaluation forms the cornerstone of health service improvement, aiming to assess the efficacy, efficiency, and overall effectiveness of primary care services \[16, 17\]. The foundation of primary care program evaluation is built on several critical components. These include problem definition, where the scope of the evaluation and the issues it addresses are clearly identified. This is followed by planning and design, where the methods of data collection, analysis, and interpretation are decided. Data collection is the next step, gathering necessary information through methods like surveys, interviews, and observation. Data analysis comes next, interpreting the collected information to draw conclusions about the program's effectiveness. Evaluation dissemination, the final step, involves reporting the results to the stakeholders involved in the primary care program. In essence, these components form a systematic process that aids in understanding the value and impact of a primary care program, and if necessary, identifies areas that require improvement \[16\]. Using PROMs as part of a program evaluation provides direct insights into the patient's perceived health outcomes, enabling more accurate assessments of program effectiveness outside of clinical outcomes, and can help identify areas for improvement \[18\]. PROMs, including the EQ‐5D, capture the broader dimensions of health by assessing not just clinical outcomes but also patient perspectives on aspects of their quality of life \[19\].

The aim of this paper is to describe the integration of PROMs within a program evaluation context in primary care. We report two examples in the exercise and active living and nutrition programs that use the EQ‐5D‐5L to examine patients' perspective by measuring their health status pre‐ and post‐program, contributing towards a more comprehensive evaluation of the programs.

## Methods

### Design and Setting

The Edmonton O‐day'min Primary Care Network (EOPCN) is in central Edmonton, Alberta, Canada. The network comprises 230 family physicians (in independent practices) who are EOPCN members. These physicians have the capability to refer any patient to any EOPCN program or service (i.e., allied health providers, group classes, individual counseling, etc.) in addition to providing routine clinical care. EOPCN programs include nutrition services, mental health, chronic disease management, exercise and active living. Overall, EOPCN acts as a central hub, offering programs and services to help patients mange their health, by supporting member family physicians. Its allied health care staff provides a wide range of care, addressing health concerns and helping patients achieve a healthy lifestyle \[20\]. As part of routine measurement, all referred patients complete a baseline assessment upon entry into any program, including the EQ‐5D‐5L. Patients complete the EQ‐5D‐5L on a tablet while in the waiting room. Repeat measures are tailored individually depending on the nature of the treatment, for example some patients required more extensive care and had assessments completed more frequently. Individuals with a completed baseline assessment (pre) and at least one repeated assessment (post) were included in the change analysis. Records were excluded if pre and post assessments were completed on the same day (*n* = 25). If individuals completed multiple repeated assessments, the last assessment was taken as it captures the final outcome of interest during the study period. A total of 2,199 adult EOPCN patients had data drawn from electronic medical records between January 1, 2021 and March 31, 2022. For the purposes of this analysis, only the exercise and active living (*n* = 301) and nutrition (*n* = 573) programs were examined. Due to the routine collection of this data for quality assurance purposes, ethical approval for secondary use of the data was granted from the University of Alberta Health Research Ethics Board (Pro00144776).

### Program Descriptions and Populations

EOPCN serves a population of 192,700 within the city of Edmonton, Alberta. Within the exercise and active living program \[20\], patients typically present with mobility or exercise specific concerns. Referrals can originate from a family physician, or patients may self‐refer to different virtual group programs or classes. The program targets patients living with chronic diseases, like hypertension, high cholesterol and/or diabetes to improve and manage their condition(s) by increasing their physical activity. An exercise specialist works 1‐1 and in group classes to teach those with injuries or chronic conditions how to exercise properly. The team encourages life‐long physical activity to enhance quality of life. As part of its routine evaluation, the logic model/framework for the program uses the EQ‐5D‐5L to measure health status alongside other outcomes. Other measures/indicators include patient satisfaction, reported improvements in mobility and exercise capacity, and qualitative feedback gathered from patient interviews. These metrics were internally developed and along with the EQ‐5D‐5L, provide a comprehensive assessment of the program's effectiveness and its impact on the patients' wellbeing in accordance with internal reporting processes.

The nutrition program \[20\] includes dietary counseling and group classes promoting healthy eating as part of weight management and chronic disease prevention. Patients are family physician referred for many different types of nutritional/dietary concerns, such as: celiac disease, diabetes and pre‐diabetes, Crohn's/colitis, disordered eating, iron or other nutrient deficiency, prenatal and postpartum nutrition, postbariatric surgery, and others. Patients can self‐refer for any virtual nutritional classes. As part of its routine evaluation, the logic model/framework for the program includes the EQ‐5D‐5L as well as other outcome measures of interest. Like the exercise and active living program, additional outcome measures of interest were developed internally. These include patient dietary adherence, self‐reported improvements related to nutrition and eating habits, and patient‐reported satisfaction and feedback about the program.

### Measures

The EQ‐5D‐5L is a generic PROM, measuring health status in five dimensions (mobility, self‐care, usual activities, pain/discomfort and anxiety/depression) \[21\]. It has five levels of response categories: no problems (1), slight problems (2), moderate problems (3), severe problems (4) and extreme problems (5). Using the five dimensions, a health status profile is created (e.g., 11323) of a possible 3125 distinct health profiles. An index score is also calculated for each health profile using a country‐specific value set. The Canadian EQ‐5D‐5L value set was applied to calculate index scores for this analysis \[22\]. Another component is the visual analog scale (VAS), in which patients choose a value from 0 to 100 (0 being poor health; 100 being perfect health) indicating their health status “today.” Other variables collected included age group, gender, visit type (initial, follow‐up, group, or class), history of condition or concern, primary concern, and body mass index (BMI). The EQ‐5D is particularly valuable in primary care settings because it provides a standardized, easy‐to‐use framework for evaluating both health status and well‐being across different populations and over time \[23, 24, 25\]. Nonetheless, given that primary care populations tend to be relatively healthy, we expected only modest changes over time.

### Data Analysis

General characteristics of the sample and changes in pre and post EQ‐5D‐5L measurements were examined in two programs (i.e., exercise and active living and nutrition groups). Change in dimensions were examined using the Pareto Classification of Health Change (PCHC) \[26\] as follows:

- Deteriorated: deterioration in the level of problems reported in one or more dimensions from intake to end of program, with no improvement in other dimensions.

- No change: Level of problems reported (2–5 only) was the same at intake and end of program.

- Maintained perfect health: No problems reported (level 1) at intake and end of program.

- Improved: improvement in the level of problems reported in one or more dimensions from intake to end of program, with no deterioration in other dimensions.

- Mixed change: some dimensions improved, some deteriorated.

Change scores were calculated for the EQ‐5D‐5L index and VAS scores. To define change groups in the EQ‐5D‐5L index and VAS scores, we used previously published minimally important differences (MID); 0.04 \[27\] and 7.0 \[28\] \[29\] respectively, as follows:

#### EQ‐5D‐5L Index Score

- Deteriorated: change score ≤ −0.04.

- No change: −0.04 \< change score \< 0.04.

- Maintained perfect health: 0.95 (i.e., health state 11111) at “pre” and “post”.

- Improved: change score ≥ 0.04.

#### VAS Score

- Deteriorated: change score ≤ −7.0.

<!-- -->

- No change: −7.0 \< change score \< 7.0.

- Maintained perfect health: 100 at “pre” and “post.”

- Improved: change score ≥ 7.0.

To assist in the interpretation of change in the EQ‐5D‐5L index, and VAS score from pre to post, two approaches were used. First, the effect size of the change was calculated by dividing the average change score by the standard deviation of the score at baseline. Effect size estimates were considered small (\< 0.2), moderate (0.5) or large (\> 0.8) \[30\]. Second, the distribution of EQ‐5D‐5L dimensions, and mean (SD) index and VAS scores were compared across programs and compared to Alberta general population norms (based on matched age and sex) \[31\]. We anticipated our findings to be below the Alberta general population norms, as these patients were referred for programming by their family physician. All analyses were conducted in STATA 14.2 (Stata Corp LLC. College Station, Texas, USA).

## Results

### Exercise and Active Living Program

In the exercise and active living program, 301 patients completed the baseline EQ‐5D‐5L. Of these, 112 repeated the measurement. At baseline, 72% were female and 39% were between 45 and 64 years old, with a mean (SD) age of 57 (16) years (Table <a href="#hsr270308-tbl-0001" data-ref-type="table">1</a>). The mean (SD) duration of care in the program was 73 (62) days. Of the patients that had medical history data, the top three conditions for referral to the program among these patients were muscle, bone, or joint disorder (23.6%), obesity/overweight (17.6%) and high blood pressure (17.6%). Pain management was the most common primary concern (22.9%). The mean (SD) BMI was 33.7 (8.6).

### Nutrition Program

In the nutrition program, 573 patients completed the EQ‐5D‐5L at baseline with 212 repeating the measurement. At baseline, 71% were female and 37% were between 45 and 64 years, with a mean (SD) age of 50 (17) years (Table <a href="#hsr270308-tbl-0001" data-ref-type="table">1</a>). The mean (SD) duration of care in the program was 113.7 (82.3) days.

At baseline, the proportion of nutrition program patients that reported any problems (levels 2–5) was 34.9% in mobility, 18.9% in self‐care, 45.7% in usual activities, 75.9% in pain/discomfort, and 67.0% in anxiety/depression (Table <a href="#hsr270308-tbl-0002" data-ref-type="table">2</a>). The mean (SD) index score was 0.77 (0.19) and the mean VAS score was 70.1 (15.6). The proportion of patients reporting any problems in all dimensions was much higher than those of the general Alberta population. The mean index and VAS scores were also lower than the general Alberta population norms. Despite slight improvements at post, self‐reported health (i.e., EQ‐5D‐5L dimensions, index, and VAS scores) remained much lower than that of the general Alberta population.

There was an 8.4% increase in the proportion of patients reporting no problems on all EQ‐5D‐5L dimensions (i.e., health profile 11111) in the nutrition program. The usual activities and self‐care dimensions had decreases in the proportion of patients reporting no problems in the post survey; there was only slight improvement in mean index and VAS scores.

Based on the Pareto Classification of Health Change (PCHC), 37.3% had an improvement, 24.5% had a deterioration, 17.0% had a mixed change, and 21.2% had no change in their health status. At the post measurement, there was an average increase of 0.02 (SD 0.11) in the index score (effect size = 0.1) and an increase of 1.0 point (SD 15.6) in the VAS (effect size = 0.1). All changes were of small magnitude and did not reach the MID thresholds.

The largest deterioration was in pain/discomfort (26%), followed by anxiety/depression (24%), and usual activities (20%). About half improved or maintained perfect health based on the index (55%) and VAS score (46%).

## Discussion

We found modest levels of improved health status, in specific dimensions of health, and maintenance of perfect health (as measured by the EQ‐5D‐5L) in the EOPCN's exercise and active living and nutrition programs during the study period. These findings underscore the critical role primary care plays in preventive care and early interventions. Through these programs, primary care can help manage patients' conditions early on, preventing symptoms from worsening, and thus contributing to better quality of life. Although these findings are without statistical significance, together with other components of the evaluation, they provide insight from the patient's perspective on their health status. The field of program evaluation is distinguished to collect and report findings from multiple sources, comprehensively examining a program to describe it's overall worth from multiple perspectives. Patient‐reported outcome measures, like the EQ‐5D, systematically document the patient's perspective, encouraging the inclusion of patients views towards decision‐making and program planning at this meso level \[10\]. The utilization of such measures can directly inform policy decisions and healthcare practices, leading to patient‐centric models of care. Combining them with other validated and reliable measures, creates a robust framework to measure program outcomes across populations and over time \[11\].

Any population‐level outcome measurement is challenging in primary care environments. Specifically, limited responsiveness to change is one issue of using generic PROMs in primary care \[32\]. This is because of the diversity of conditions, symptoms and functions of patients using primary care services. No single PROM exists for primary care program evaluation, however it is important to purposefully choose and incorporate an appropriate PROM into evaluation activities. In the exercise and active living and nutrition programs, health status was previously determined to be an important indicator, and therefore using the EQ‐5D‐5L was a good fit. Applying the same generic PROM gives the ability to compare outcomes across the two programs. Additionally, with the normative values available for the province of Alberta \[31\], collecting the EQ‐5D‐5L on these patients allows for another layer of comparison at a larger population level. However, as new research on measuring health status and other patient‐reported outcomes emerges, it is important to consider the potential use of other PROMs in future primary care settings \[9\]. Additionally, by exploring the collection of other PROMs in primary care, other uses for the data at the micro or macro levels may provide insights for the future. This includes incorporating disease or condition specific PROMs, alongside generic measures like the EQ‐5D‐5L.

The typical primary care patient referred to the exercise and active living or nutrition programs are part of a relatively healthy population. Although a patient may have underlying chronic conditions or have complex health needs, they are usually able to work and contribute in a normal way, with the primary goal being to remain out of the acute care system. Thus, many of the programs like the exercise and active living and nutrition program have been developed, to maintain and promote healthy behaviors. For this reason, often maintaining a certain level of health, even if not perfect health, is a positive outcome and warrants reporting in program evaluation. This contrasts with more attractive outcomes of improvement. As in our sample, the modest outcome of maintenance of health status (as measured by the EQ‐5D‐5L), may contribute to reduced medical expenses \[33\] and perceived longer life span \[34\], by avoiding deterioration of multiple domains of health.

Additionally due to the generic nature of the population, PROMs measurement in primary care is often limited by ceiling effects, which can occur with the EQ‐5D‐5L. This can obscure subtle differences in health status, particularly in populations with generally good health, or in those experiencing mild improvements \[35, 36\]. To address this, examination of the five levels in each dimension is important to fully describe the population, as well as considering the clinically important differences instead of only statistically important differences. This will describe the practical importance or meaningfulness of a program in improving outcomes, including those from the patient's perspective. Comprehensive program evaluation is of great benefit here, as it is multi‐faceted and truly considers all outcomes of interest. Future evaluations may expand to include condition or disease‐specific PROMs as appropriate to the population of interest to be more sensitive. This can enhance the evaluation of an interventions' impact, allowing for more nuanced understanding of patient outcomes \[37\]. However, while it would provide more detailed information on disease‐specific issues, it may not capture broader aspects of health and well‐being, which can be critical when comparing different types of people, or programs or intervention across conditions \[38\], like in the exercise and active living and nutrition programs. While patient experience surveys are administered regularly and satisfaction levels, areas for improvement and recommendations are reported, they are not often combined with other outcomes like the EQ‐5D‐5L. In the future, it would be valuable to examine the relationship between patient satisfaction/feedback and self‐reported health status.

### Strengths and Limitations

Key features of this analysis include routine primary care measurement within a naturalistic setting and patient populations. While real‐world data is presented, within a real‐world context representing a diverse patient population, limitations exist. There could be a differential loss to follow up of those who only completed a “pre” survey. Missing data and not having a control arm presents methodological challenges and limited our sample size. It was not possible to link EQ‐5D‐5L data to other patient data, which limited the analysis. Additionally, our findings are not generalizable to other population, however, we may consider them to transferrable to other primary care populations with similar programming.

## Conclusion

The implementation of generic PROMs, such as the EQ‐5D‐5L, as part of routine data collection in the EOPCN has the potential to inform program decisions to improve the quality of care and service delivery. The results are meant to inform the exercise and active living and nutrition programs by describing the characteristics and health status of patients assessing these programs to help meet the needs of patients. In effect, this approach emphasizes the preventive and proactive role of primary care in maintaining community health. Beyond the EOPCN, using the same tools throughout other primary care settings as indicators of health status maintenance or change are valuable to examine at the population‐level. Combining PROMs with other validated and reliable outcome measures in primary care creates a robust framework to measure program outcomes across populations and over time.
