# Exploring the feasibility of using discrete choice experiment (DCE) to elicit preferences among children and adolescents: protocol for a convergent parallel mixed methods study

## Abstract

### Abstract

### Introduction

Discrete choice experiments (DCEs) are increasingly used to elicit value sets for paediatric health-related quality of life instruments, including the EQ-5D-Y. In a multinational pilot study that compared EQ-5D-Y-5L health state preferences among children, adolescents and adults, more inconsistencies in DCE responses were observed among children and adolescents than adults. Possible explanations for inconsistencies could be youths’ limited understanding of health state preferences or lack of engagement with the online survey. This protocol describes the methods of a study that explores the feasibility of eliciting health state preferences for the EQ-5D-Y-5L from Canadian children and adolescents using DCE.

### Methods and analysis

A convergent parallel mixed methods study will be conducted with a purposive, stratified sample of 36 Canadian children and adolescents (13–18 years). In the quantitative study component, participants will complete an online questionnaire with EQ-5D-Y-5L and DCE exercises. The quantitative data on participants’ sociodemographic characteristics, EQ-5D-Y-5L, and feasibility indicators of DCE (ie, incompletion, speeding, flatlining, violation of dominant choice task) will be analysed using descriptive statistics. The second component will be a qualitative focus group discussion to understand their experiences of completing the DCE tasks. Six focus groups (with six participants per group) will be conducted. The qualitative data will be analysed using content analysis. The findings from both components will then be integrated using narrative synthesis.

### Ethics and dissemination

Ethics approval was obtained from the Hamilton Integrated Research Ethics Board (#17034). We will publish findings in peer-reviewed journals and present at key conferences. We will also disseminate results within the EuroQol community as the study findings are part of a methodological research programme aimed at informing the design of an EQ-5D-Y-5L valuation study in Canada.

## Introduction

There is a growing interest in measuring and valuing health-related quality of life (HRQoL) in children and adolescents.<sup>1</sup> The EQ-5D-Y-5L is the youth version of the EQ-5D, one of the most widely used generic measures of HRQoL. The EQ-5D instruments contain a descriptive system that describes an individual’s health state according to five dimensions of HRQoL (ie, mobility, self-care, usual activities, anxiety/depression, pain/discomfort) and either three (3L) or five (5L) levels of severity. Responses to this descriptive system can be weighted by preferences obtained from the general population (called value sets) to produce health utilities. Health utilities provide a single index value that can compare HRQoL across interventions and diseases. For this reason, they are used for a variety of applications, including resource allocation decisions,<sup>2</sup> population health surveillance and facilitating patient involvement in screening, monitoring or treatment decisions.35

Despite the publication of the international valuation protocol for the EQ-5D-Y-3L,<sup>6</sup> the outstanding methodological and normative issues justify the need for local stakeholder engagement when developing a value set for the EQ-5D-Y-5L. Valuing children and adolescents’ HRQoL is more challenging, both theoretically and methodologically, than valuing adults’ HRQoL. Key considerations include what dimensions of HRQoL should be considered for children and adolescents across developmental stages (eg, from newborn to late teens); who should be asked to complete the valuation (eg, self vs proxy report by parents, caregivers or clinicians); whose perspective should be considered (eg, self vs hypothetical 10-year-old child); and how should health states be valued (eg, discrete choice experiment (DCE) vs time trade-off (TTO)). In a qualitative interview study with 15 stakeholders in Canada (ie, individuals who are working in healthcare, university, government and industry settings), most participants expressed that preferences should be elicited directly from children and adolescents using their own perspectives.<sup>7</sup> The majority of stakeholders preferred the use of DCE over TTO as the method of valuation.<sup>7</sup>

DCEs are increasingly used to elicit preferences and develop value sets for paediatric HRQoL instruments, including the EQ-5D-Y-3L.<sup>8</sup> Our research team conducted an international pilot study to compare the use of DCE among children, adolescents and adults in Canada, Australia, Spain, China and the Netherlands. Preliminary analyses showed that there were more inconsistencies in preferences among children and adolescents than those of adults (ie, no monotonic decrease in health utilities according to levels of severity, indicating logical inconsistencies). Logical inconsistencies are problematic because they can lead to situations where a respondent indicates a preference for a worse health state over a better one, making the resulting value set unreliable for use in economic evaluations and healthcare decision-making.<sup>9</sup> This is particularly important in a Canadian setting, where the EQ-5D is recommended by Canada’s Drug Agency to inform their pricing and reimbursement recommendations for new drugs and medical devices.<sup>2</sup>

There are several reasons as to why inconsistencies with the application of DCE among children and adolescents were observed in the pilot study. It is possible that children and adolescents did not fully understand abstract concepts involved in measuring health state and health state preferences. For example, respondents might have been unable to accurately imagine what a health state is like because of their lack of experience with it.<sup>10</sup> Lack of engagement may have also impacted responses. For example, some respondents may not have put reasonable efforts into the survey (eg, because they were in a rush or distracted) and instead randomly clicked on the screen. As the pilot study was restricted to an online survey, more engagement with children and adolescents is required to fully understand this issue.

### Aim of research

The aim of this mixed methods study is to obtain a better understanding of the feasibility of eliciting health state preferences for the EQ-5D-Y-5L directly from Canadian children and adolescents using DCE. Specifically, we want to confirm whether Canadian children and adolescents can express and assign a preference to hypothetical health states using DCE, as well as to deepen our understanding of the process they employ to complete the tasks. The specific research questions that will be addressed are:

#### Quantitative research question

- Is it feasible for Canadian children and children and adolescents to complete a series of valuation tasks using DCE to assign preferences to hypothetical health states?

#### Qualitative research question

- What is the process employed by Canadian children and adolescents to engage in and attempt to complete a series of health state valuation tasks using DCE?

#### Mixed methods research question

- To what extent do the focus group findings align with the quantitative results on the feasibility of using DCE to elicit preferences from children and adolescents, as perceived by children and adolescents themselves?

## Methods and analysis

### Study design

Mixed methods are ideally suited to explore complex phenomena, such as methodological and normative issues related to the design of valuation studies in children and adolescent HRQoL. For this study, we will use a convergent parallel mixed methods design. In the quantitative component, participants will complete a questionnaire with DCE exercises. In a second parallel component, a qualitative descriptive study<sup>11</sup> will be completed with the same participants engaging in a focus group discussion on their experiences and perceptions of the health state valuation process. The quantitative and qualitative data will be analysed independently, with the results brought together in the interpretation phase (see <a href="#F1" data-ref-type="fig">figure 1</a> for details).<sup>12</sup>

The decision to use a convergent parallel mixed methods design was based on analyses from the multicounty pilot study. Although children and adolescents participating in this study reported that they felt it was easy to understand and complete the online DCE questions, there were more logical inconsistencies in the modelling results compared with adults.<sup>7</sup> These initial findings need further explanation as there could be many reasons for these discrepancies (eg, children and adolescents not understanding the concept of health state valuation or not being engaged in the online survey). On their own, the quantitative data are insufficient to fully understand this issue. The intent of a convergent design is to obtain different but complementary data on the same topic. Therefore, we will use focus group data (qualitative component) to determine whether individuals’ experiences with the valuation tasks converge or diverge from the observed data on the feasibility of using DCE (quantitative component)—and if so, why. Given the nature of inquiry, greater priority will be given to the qualitative component of this mixed methods study (QUAL+quan).<sup>13</sup> This study design will allow for a more comprehensive evaluation of the feasibility of eliciting health state preferences directly from Canadian children and adolescents using DCE.

### Study context and sample

The study will be conducted within Hamilton and the Greater Toronto Areas. The same sample of participants will be used for both the quantitative and qualitative components of the study. Stakeholders in both Canada and the USA suggest that children and adolescents\>12 years should be cognitively capable of completing the valuation tasks.<sup>7 14</sup> Because age is a crucial factor in group dynamics among children and adolescents,<sup>15</sup> we will target the following age groups: (1) 13–14 years old; (2) 15–16 years old; and (3) 17–18 years old.

To be eligible to participate in the study, children and adolescents must be:

- Able to speak and read in English;

- Have the parent or legal guardian provide written consent (for participants aged≤16).

- Able to provide written assent (for participants aged≤16) or consent (for participants aged\>16); and

- Able to complete an online questionnaire.

Children and adolescents with self-reported physical disabilities or neurodiverse conditions will be eligible for inclusion provided they are able to complete the study tasks (ie, online questionnaire and focus group participation).

### Sample size

For this study, an a priori sample size of 36 participants was estimated, stratified into six groups (n=6 per group) conducted separately according to age (13–14 years; 15–16 years; and 17–18 years) and gender (boys and girls). Studies in which focus groups are not stratified by any characteristic typically require a smaller sample to answer the research question (eg, three to six groups). A study such as ours, where focus groups are stratified by specific characteristics, may require a larger number of groups.1618 As data collection and analysis are concurrent in qualitative studies, the number of focus groups conducted will be adapted to the variety of experiences captured, in line with the principle of information power.<sup>18</sup> The information power approach considers the research aims, sample specificity, quality of dialogue, and analysis strategy to guide decisions with respect to our estimated sample size.

### Recruitment

A snowball sampling strategy was used to recruit a stratified, purposeful sample of participants. We contacted individuals involved with community-based organisations (eg, Hamilton Youth Soccer Club, Hamilton Public Library) that offer structured activities to children and adolescents to ask for their support with recruitment. Specifically, in identifying eligible participants and distributing a recruitment flyer.

### Data collection

At the time of submission of the present protocol manuscript, data collection had been completed, but no analysis had yet begun. All data collection activities took place between June and September 2024, at a location that was convenient for the group (eg, a library room near a soccer field where team practice is being held immediately before/after the focus group). Quantitative and qualitative data collection occurred in a single session and took approximately 60 min (ie, 15 min for introductions, 15 min for the online survey and 30 min for the focus group discussion).<sup>19</sup> The time allocated for the focus groups was extended if the discussion was ongoing at the end of the 60 min, and there was group consensus to stay longer. Pizza and drinks were provided, and participants received a gift card as a token of appreciation for their time. Reimbursement for transportation was available on request.

Two members of the research team were responsible for data collection, with one acting as the moderator and the other as co-moderator during the focus group sessions.<sup>15 20</sup> The moderator acted as the group facilitator, encouraging the input of participants, asking questions and keeping the discussion on track. The co-moderator coordinated site logistics, ensured all consent forms were completed, assisted with administration of the online questionnaire and managed the technical equipment (ie, set up audio recording for focus group discussion).<sup>15</sup> To minimise bias, there was one male (FX) and one female (BH) moderator at each session.

#### Beginning of session

The moderators began the session by welcoming participants and introducing themselves. The intent is to help participants perceive their relationship with the moderator as more informal than with their teachers.<sup>19 21</sup> Participants were then asked to introduce themselves as an icebreaker. After introductions are complete, the moderator explained the purpose of the study and established rules for group behaviour.<sup>19</sup> These rules include confidentiality of what is discussed within the group, the right to express one’s opinion freely without threat of being ridiculed or belittled, the fact that it is not expected that group members agree on different points of discussion, and reminding participants that there are no right or wrong answers.<sup>22</sup>

#### Quantitative data collection

For the quantitative data collection component, each participant was provided with a laptop and asked to complete an online self-administered questionnaire. The questionnaire contains basic sociodemographic questions (eg, age, gender, level of education), the EQ-5D-Y-5L, EQ-Visual Analogue Scale (EQ VAS), 13 DCE choice tasks (for all ages) and 3 DCE with duration (for ages 15–18). <a href="#F2" data-ref-type="fig">Figures2</a><a href="#F4" data-ref-type="fig">4</a> are screenshots of the EQ-5D-Y-5L, EQ VAS and DCE components of the survey, respectively.

The *EQ-5D-Y-5L* is a generic instrument that considers five broad dimensions of HRQoL: (1) ‘mobility’; (2) ‘looking after myself’; (3) ‘doing usual activities’; (4) ‘having pain or discomfort’; and (5) ‘feeling worried, sad or unhappy.’ Each dimension has five response levels, ranging from ‘no problems’ to ‘cannot/extreme problems.’ The number of possible health states that the EQ-5D-Y-5L can describe is 3125.<sup>23</sup> The EQ-5D-Y-5L was adapted from an adult version (ie, EQ-5D-5L) to have more child-friendly wording. For example, the dimension of ‘anxiety/depression’ in the EQ-5D-5L was rephrased as ‘feeling worried, sad or unhappy’ in the EQ-5D-Y-5L. The EQ-5D-Y-5L is intended for self-completion among children and adolescents aged between 8 and 15 years.<sup>24</sup>

For the *EQ VAS*, participants are asked to rate their ‘health today’ using a vertical VAS that ranges from ‘The best health you can imagine’ to ‘The worst health you can imagine’.

For each *DCE* choice task, participants will be presented with two health states using the attributes and levels of the EQ-5D-Y-5L. Participants will then be asked to choose which health state they prefer (without consideration of the time spent in the health states). In line with a previous pilot study conducted by the research team, participants aged 15–18 years will also complete three ‘DCE with duration’ tasks where the health states described also include an additional attribute specifying the number of years spent in a health state before dying.<sup>25</sup> All participants received the same version of the survey.

#### Qualitative data collection

After completion of the questionnaire, the moderator proceeded with the qualitative descriptive component of the study. Participants engaged in a focus group discussion for the remainder of the session. A focus group can be broadly defined as ‘a type of group discussion about a topic under the guidance of a trained group moderator’.<sup>26 27</sup> This methodology has been used to elicit the perspectives and experiences of children and adolescents on a range of health-related issues, such as alcohol use, transition to adulthood, and embarrassment.<sup>19</sup>

One of the benefits of focus groups is that they avoid some of the power imbalances between researchers and participants that may occur in a one-on-one interview, especially between an adult (ie, researcher) and a child.<sup>19</sup> Another benefit is that, in a focus group, it is the interaction between participants (rather than between the participant and the researcher in an interview) that generates the data. This group dynamic encourages participants to reflect on and clarify their own experiences in understanding and completing health state preference tasks (eg, if there was something they did not like or understand). Group interactions are also useful for identifying unanticipated issues as participants share and question one another’s ideas.

A semi-structured guide was used to facilitate the focus group discussion. This guide ensured that the focus group discussion is structured around key topics while remaining flexible enough for participants to freely express their ideas on a rather complex subject or for the moderator to ask follow-up questions based on observations from the questionnaire (eg, if a participant expressed confusion when answering a specific question). It contains four sections, with questions and prompts covering:

1.  Their understanding of the hypothetical health states, including whether the health state descriptions were easy to understand, if they focused on any aspect of the health states (eg, an EQ-5D-Y dimension or level of severity), and whether they were able to imagine themselves in a health state that they have never experienced before;

2.  Their experience completing the questionnaire, including the perceived ease and/or difficulty of assigning their own preferences to a health state, their understanding of the questions, and their ability to use the online technology;

3.  The challenges they experienced when completing the questionnaire and the decisions they made to address those challenges; and

4.  Any feedback or suggestions for improving either content, technical features, or any other aspects of the online questionnaire.

To facilitate the discussion, printed examples of the DCE exercises were placed in front of participants. Throughout the focus group discussion, the moderator used various techniques to manage group dynamics (eg, verbalisation of common traits between subjects, presentation of the focus groups’ objectives). The moderator also sought to promote individual participation (eg, by paraphrasing, inviting participants to comment on others’ responses).<sup>28</sup>

#### Additional data collection

An additional source of data will be the research team’s field notes.<sup>15</sup> During quantitative data collection, two student investigators, who are also members of the research team, recorded situational information. This includes both verbal (eg, the number of times a participant expressed confusion or asked for help when completing the online survey) and non-verbal (eg, head nodding, eye contact, voice volume during the focus group discussion) communication. To facilitate this information-gathering process, a data collection template developed by Onwuegbuzie and colleagues has been adapted for the purpose of this study.<sup>29</sup>

### Data analysis

#### Quantitative data analysis

The quantitative data on participants’ sociodemographic characteristics, EQ-5D-Y-5L and DCE feasibility will be analysed using descriptive statistics (eg, range, mean, SD). Given the sample size, no modelling will be conducted. Feasibility will instead be assessed based on quantitative indicators related to how DCE tasks were undertaken, which may suggest that participants are not understanding or paying sufficient attention.<sup>30 31</sup> These are:

- *Incompletion:* Although unlikely to occur in person, participants may not complete all of the assigned DCE choice tasks.

- *Speeding:* Some participants may answer DCE choice tasks so fast that they are not processing all relevant information. Based on previous studies,<sup>30 31</sup> we will consider benchmarks of below 3 or 5 s response times as an indicator for speeding. Multiple benchmarks will be explored given the arbitrary nature of this outcome definition.<sup>32</sup> We will also report the mean time per task.

- *Flatlining:* Rather than considering their preferences for each choice task, participants may consistently choose the health state on the left or right, or switch between the two (ie, left, right, left, right).

- *Violation of dominant choice task:* One choice pair with a dominant health state (ie, better on all EQ-5D-Y-5L dimensions) will be included.

#### Qualitative data analysis

The qualitative data collected from each focus group will be audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim. Any information about the participant’s identification will be removed from the transcripts. The data will be analysed using qualitative content analysis. Content analysis enables a systematic coding of data by organising the information into categories to discover patterns. This type of descriptive approach is conducive to conducting exploratory work in an area such as ours where not much is known and the reporting of common issues is desired (eg, Do children and adolescents understand the concept of health state valuation? Are there any challenges in completing the tasks?).<sup>33</sup>

Two independent researchers with experience in both qualitative analysis and preference elicitation (FX and BH)<sup>5 34 35</sup> will conduct the content analysis according to a process described by Elo and Kyngäs.<sup>36</sup> In the first phase, researchers will familiarise themselves with the data by transcribing the focus groups and reading the transcripts several times. In the second phase, the researchers will start organising the data by creating categories and grouping codes under higher order headings. As part of this process, the definitions for each code, subcategory and category will be developed.<sup>37</sup> Because there are no previous studies in this area, an inductive approach will be taken where the codes are derived directly from the text data.<sup>33 37</sup> An initial list of codes will be created based on a subset of the data. Any discrepancies in codes will be discussed by the two researchers until consensus is reached. The initial version of the coding scheme will then be reviewed by the wider research team, including senior researchers with expertise conducting qualitative research among youth (SMJ)<sup>38 39</sup> and preference elicitation methods (EP and AO).<sup>34 35</sup> On approval, the coding scheme will be applied to the remaining data using Microsoft Word.<sup>40 41</sup> Codes will then be grouped into larger categories. The third and final phase of qualitative data analysis is reporting, during which the researchers will create a table to present each code alongside its definition and an illustrative quote from the transcripts. The decision to use Microsoft over other qualitative software (eg, NVivo) was made in consideration of the small number of transcripts (n=6 focus groups) and the relatively straightforward analytical approach (ie, content analysis). From a practical perspective, this saves the research team both training time and costs associated with using new software.

#### Integration of quantitative and qualitative data

Once the quantitative and qualitative analyses have been analysed separately, the findings will be integrated narratively. As part of this process, researchers will compare the results from the analysis of both datasets, and make an interpretation as to whether the results support or contradict each other. For example, quantitative indicators of feasibility (eg, taking a long time to complete the questionnaire, providing responses that violate the DCE dominant choice task) may be explained by qualitative data on participants’ experience completing the valuation tasks (eg, if they mention confusion about the health states or that they did not understand a question) or data collected by the student investigators in their field notes (eg, number of times a respondent expressed confusion when completing the DCE).

The same participants were included in both the quantitative and qualitative data collection, so that the data can be more easily converged or compared. If the integrated analysis identifies discordance (ie, contradictions or inconsistencies) between quantitative and qualitative findings, we will verify the methodological rigour of each component of the study, the comparability of datasets and the delivery of the questionnaire and focus groups.<sup>42</sup> We will also consider how these contradictions can generate new research questions.<sup>43</sup> One example of discordance is if a participant states in the focus group that they carefully complete the online questionnaire (qualitative data), but then their DCE response pattern indicates flatlining (quantitative data).

### Methodological rigour

A range of strategies will be used to verify credibility (internal validity), dependability (reliability or consistency of findings) and confirmability (neutrality) of data. A checklist of these strategies is presented in <a href="#SP1" data-ref-type="supplementary-material">online supplemental table S1</a> as supplementary material. Given the complexity of mixed methods research,<sup>44</sup> the rigour of the quantitative, qualitative and mixed methods components of the study will be assessed separately.<sup>12</sup>

### Patient and public involvement

Two adolescents (aged between 13–18 years) have joined the research team as student investigators. The intent is to engage Canadian youth in a co-creation process where they are involved in study design, implementation and evaluation. Their engagement will take place in various stages throughout the research process:

- *Before data collection commences*: The research team has organised a group introductory call on Zoom with the student investigators. To start, the research team introduced themselves and the study, and discussed roles and responsibilities. It was emphasised that, as members of the research team, the student investigators bring a different but equally valuable perspective to the project. Then, the research team reviewed the study materials presented within this protocol with the student investigator to get their feedback (eg, on language, acceptability, feasibility of recruitment strategy).

- *During data collection:* The student-investigators joined the data collection activities at the study site. They supported the research team in recruitment, preparing the focus groups (eg, setting up the room), welcoming participants and taking field notes.

- *After data collection:* The student-investigators will meet with the research team to debrief on the data collection activities and share their thoughts on this process. They will also contribute to data analysis (eg, transcription and coding) and interpretation.

Ethics approval was obtained from the Hamilton Integrated Research Ethics Board (#17034). We will publish findings in peer-reviewed journals and present at key conferences. We will also disseminate results within the EuroQol community as the study findings are part of a methodological research programme aimed at informing the design of an EQ-5D-Y-5L valuation study in Canada.

## Discussion

There is a growing interest in measuring and valuing HRQoL in children and adolescents.<sup>1</sup> Since the development of the EQ-5D-Y in 2010,<sup>45 46</sup> empirical studies using the instrument have been conducted worldwide.4756 Stakeholders in Canada and the USA suggest that children and adolescents be included in child health valuation.<sup>7 14</sup> For countries that are keen to develop a value set for the EQ-5D-Y involving children or adolescents, it is critical to know if using the DCE among this population is feasible given the logical inconsistencies that have been observed in a multicountry pilot study. This project, which is part of a methodological research programme, will provide both qualitative and quantitative evidence on the feasibility of eliciting health state preferences directly from Canadian children and adolescents using DCE. The findings will be used to inform future EQ-5D-Y-5L valuation studies in Canada and other countries.

An important strength of this study is the use of multiple sources of evidence to understand a complex phenomenon, such as health state valuation. Data from the online self-administered questionnaire, focus group transcripts, and the research team’s field notes on situational information will be analysed together to deepen our understanding of the feasibility of eliciting health state preferences from Canadian children and adolescents using DCE. The mixed methods study design is intended to support a convergent line of inquiry, with integration occurring at multiple points throughout the research process (eg, defining research questions, sampling strategy, data analysis).

Another strength of the study design is the in-person data collection. We decided to do in-person data collection because the information collected during the international pilot study was insufficient to fully understand the inconsistencies observed in the DCE data. We will use the same online technology as the pilot study. While it is possible that participants may act differently when completing the questionnaire in-person (eg, less likely to rush), valuable information can be obtained by watching their verbal (eg, number of times a question is asked aloud or confusion is expressed) and non-verbal (eg, facial expressions, body language) communication, and asking follow-up questions directly. To this end, we have devised various ways to maximise engagement with children and adolescents throughout the research process (eg, inclusion of student investigators, focus group discussions).

One limitation is that the sample size is too small to apply DC modelling and estimate the coefficients necessary for producing health state utility values. Based on previous studies, this would require approximately 1000 participants.<sup>57</sup> As the objective of this study was to obtain a better understanding of the feasibility of eliciting health state preferences directly from Canadian children and adolescents using DCE, the sample size was determined based on the amount of information required to answer the qualitative research question. A second limitation of the sample is with regard to the recruitment strategy, which may have introduced selection bias. Since there are logistical challenges recruiting through public schools, we used a snowball sampling approach. The implication is that potentially eligible participants experiencing socioeconomic disparities or access issues may be less likely to participate (eg, if not on a sports team). To mitigate this, we used multiple outreach strategies (eg, public library, soccer clubs), minimised the burden on parents (eg, by offering a meal and gift card) and ensured maximum variation with regard to key characteristics of age and gender. The impact on results is expected to be minimal as we are not developing a value set for the EQ-5D-Y and so a representative sample of the general population is not required. Priority is instead given to the qualitative component of this mixed methods analysis.
