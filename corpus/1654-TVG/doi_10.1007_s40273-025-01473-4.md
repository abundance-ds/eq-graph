# EQ-5D-5L or EQ-HWB-S: Which is the Better Instrument for Capturing Spillover Effects in Parental Carers of Children with COVID-19?

## Abstract

### Background and Objectives

‘Caregiver health spillovers’ refer to the broader impacts of an individual’s illness and interventions on informal caregivers’ health and well-being. This study focuses on the spillover effects experienced by parental carers of children with coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19), aiming to compare the psychometric properties of the EQ-5D-5L and the experimental EQ Health and Wellbeing Short version (EQ-HWB-S) in capturing these effects.

### Methods

A longitudinal study was conducted with 861 parental carers of children aged 0–18 years with COVID-19 and 231 parents of healthy children as the control group. The EQ-5D-5L and EQ-HWB-S were used to assess parental health and well-being. Analyses included known-groups validity (multivariable regression), test–retest reliability (Gwet’s AC1, intraclass correlation coefficient) and responsiveness to health improvement (Glass’ Δ effect size).

### Results

Parents of infected children reported more problems than those of healthy controls. The EQ-HWB-S better discriminated between sub-groups defined by the child’s COVID-19 presence, caring time and work impact. Test–retest reliability was fair to good for EQ-HWB-S dimensions (Gwet’s AC1: 0.33–0.79), moderate to good for EQ-5D-5L (Gwet’s AC1: 0.40–0.76), and good for index scores and EQ VAS (intraclass correlation coefficient: 0.70–0.77). Parental health and well-being improved as children recovered, with the EQ-5D-5L showing slightly higher responsiveness (effect size: 0.77–0.87) than EQ-HWB-S (effect size: 0.62–0.74).

### Conclusions

Both EQ-HWB-S and EQ-5D-5L are valid, reliable and responsive for measuring parental spillover effects related to a child’s COVID-19 infection. EQ-HWB-S outperformed in distinguishing social and emotional impacts of caregiving, while EQ-5D-5L better captured physical health improvements. The choice between tools may depend on study objectives.

## Introduction

In paediatric populations, the challenges posed by the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic extend beyond the direct infection to the impact on parents and other family caregivers. Viewing health as a collective family concern shifts paediatric clinical decision making to emphasise caregiver well-being, which encompass ‘spillover effects’. According to the Spillovers in Health Economic Evaluation and Research (SHEER) consensus, ‘family and caregiver health spillovers’ in the context of a cost-effectiveness analysis (CEA) refer to the positive and negative impacts of an individual’s health on the health-related quality of life (HRQoL) of family members and/or caregivers. Specifically, these wider impacts may result from the individual’s health condition, the intervention targeting it, or both \[1\]. In this paper, spillover effects refer to the overall impacts, including the psychological, physical, financial and emotional burdens, both positive and negative, experienced by parental carers while caring for a sick child \[2\].

Recent investigations highlight the crucial role of spillover effects in healthcare economics \[3\]. For instance, incorporating family spillover effects into a paediatric CEA has been shown to reduce incremental cost-effectiveness ratios by an average of 31% (US\$40,000/quality-adjusted life-year), thereby making interventions appear more cost effective \[4\]. Global Health Technology Assessment (HTA) guidelines recommend including spillover effects in CEAs to enable a more comprehensive and standardised evaluation \[1, 5–7\]. This is vital for understanding the full impact of healthcare interventions and shaping policies for unpaid family caregivers. Key recommendations include using validated instruments to ensure consistency and comparability across studies, specifying spillover inclusion criteria, prioritising primary data collection, presenting scenario analyses and integrating impact inventory tables for transparency.

This highlights the need for validated tools. However, some preference-based instruments face limitations in capturing caregivers’ HRQoL. For instance, the SF-6D tends to underestimate utility values at the lower HRQoL spectrum, and the EQ-5D has a ceiling effect, potentially underrepresenting the severity of the caregiver burden \[8\]. Similarly, the EQ-5D-3L/5L struggles to reflect key dimensions of caregiving. Studies on caregivers of children with autism and those with aromatic L-amino acid decarboxylase deficiency reported minimal problems in EQ-5D dimensions such as ‘usual activities’, ‘pain/discomfort’ and ‘anxiety/depression’, despite significant disruptions in work, household tasks, back pain and emotional strain \[9, 10\]. This indicates that these instruments may inadequately reflect the full impact of caregiving. Nonetheless, the EQ-5D remains the most commonly used utility method worldwide and in China, employed in two thirds of studies for calculating incremental cost-effectiveness ratios for spillover effects \[4, 11, 12\]. However, few studies have assessed parental spillovers in children with COVID-19; one found ‘pain/discomfort’ as the only EQ-5D-5L dimension significantly differing between caregivers of affected and unaffected children \[12\]. Moreover, the SHEER guideline recommends, when possible, using the same measure for patient and caregiver outcomes to enable effective aggregation \[1\]. Given the widespread use of EQ-5D-Y in paediatrics and its conceptual alignment with EQ-5D, testing the EQ-5D-5L ensures consistency and relevance in evaluating spillover effects.

The EQ Health and Wellbeing Short version (EQ-HWB-S) questionnaire was developed to provide a broader assessment of health and social care, including their impacts on patients, social care users and caregivers \[13, 14\]. As a preference-weighted measure for economic evaluations, it remains experimental, with limited validation and a definitive value set established \[15\]. While conceptually overlapping with the EQ-5D-5L in mobility, activity and feelings, the EQ-HWB-S also encompasses autonomy, cognition and relationships. Only two studies have validated the EQ-HWB-S in parental carers \[16, 17\]; however, its comparative performance against the EQ-5D-5L in this population remains unexplored.

However, accurately evaluating children’s HRQoL is also a fundamental aspect. Commonly used generic preference-weighted instruments, such as the EQ-5D-Y-3L, EQ-5D-Y-5L, Child Health Utility 9D, AQoL-6D and Health Utilities Index, have demonstrated good psychometric properties across conditions \[18\]. However, few have been validated for children with COVID-19. Additional challenges include a lack of valid measures for infants, reliance on proxy reports, and discrepancies between self-reports and proxy reports \[19\]. This study used the EQ Toddler and Infant Populations Questionnaire (EQ-TIPS) for children aged 0–4 years and the EQ-5D-Y-3L for those aged 5–18 years. Both instruments have demonstrated reliability, child-proxy agreement, known-groups validity and responsiveness in our prior research \[20\].

Our study provides crucial evidence on the validity of the EQ-HWB-S and EQ-5D-5L for HTA of COVID-19 interventions. The pandemic amplified parental stress, uncertainty and fear because of disrupted routines, financial strain and limited support, exacerbating spillover effects as mental health declines affected physical health, caregiving capacity and overall quality of life \[21\]. Evaluating spillover effects during an acute illness enables the measurement of how caregiver burdens evolve from active illness to recovery. Moreover, the burden of COVID-19 extends to broader social impacts, including the caregiver burden as part of this perspective \[22\]. The aim of the present study is to compare the psychometric properties of the existing EQ-5D-5L and the experimental EQ-HWB-S in capturing spillover effects in parental carers of children with COVID-19. In this way, we aim to determine whether the new EQ-HWB-S is a valuable addition for measuring HRQoL in carers, or if the EQ-5D-5L is already sufficient in this respect.

## Methods

### Sampling

A total of 861 paediatric patients with COVID-19 and their parental carers were recruited from an academic hospital in Shanghai (May 2022 to January 2023). Recruitment involved approaching all eligible patients and their carers during hospital visits. Of the 920 eligible participants, 59 declined to participate. The control group included 231 parents of COVID-19-negative children, recruited via snowball sampling of patients’ siblings and friends.

Eligibility patients were aged 0–18 years, newly diagnosed with COVID-19 (7–10 days prior) via polymerase chain reaction or antigen testing, with no prior COVID-19 history, and hospitalised or receiving outpatient treatment. Exclusions included other respiratory infections (e.g. influenza, adenovirus, respiratory syncytial virus or *Mycoplasma pneumoniae*) within 3 months before the survey or other underlying illnesses.

The control group included children aged 0–18 years with no COVID-19 history, no symptoms or exposure in the past 3 months, and no recent known infections, confirmed by a recent negative polymerase chain reaction/antigen test. Exclusions were physical limitations or a lack of guardian consent.

Parental carers included primary carers present during the child’s outpatient visits or hospitalisation who were cognitively able to complete surveys. Parents with severe long-term conditions were excluded, but those with COVID-19 were included. Informed consent was obtained from all guardians.

### Procedures

All consenting patients and parents completed the survey separately, without mutual interaction: patients and carers in clinics or wards using tablets, and healthy controls at home via online platforms to ensure reliable communication. Standardised instructions ensured consistency, and one trained interviewer administered all surveys. Participants were required to respond to each item as fully as possible. Parents provided: (1) sociodemographic data; (2) the EQ-5D-Y-3L proxy version 1 for children aged over 4 years or the EQ-TIPS for children aged under 4 years, with the EQ VAS; (3) their own and their child’s Overall Health Assessment (OHA); (4) the EQ-5D-5L with the EQ VAS; and (5) the EQ-HWB-S.

For children aged over 6 years, the survey included the self-completed EQ-5D-Y-3L with the EQ VAS and OHA. At follow-up (2–3 weeks later), only 311 of 861 participants with COVID-19 completed the same questionnaire, with absences because of transfers, refusals, other infections and non-return (Fig. <a href="#Fig1" data-ref-type="fig">1</a>). Clinicians recorded symptoms, length of illness, compliance, severity and treatment per Chinese COVID-19 guidelines \[23\].

### Instruments

The EQ-HWB-S comprises nine items addressing physical, cognitive and emotional well-being, measured on a 5-point Likert scale, with a 7-day recall period (see Fig. <a href="#Fig2" data-ref-type="fig">2</a> for detailed dimension descriptions) \[14\]. The Chinese version was translated and culturally adapted per EuroQol Group guidelines \[24, 25\], showing good acceptability and construct validity \[26\]. The EQ-HWB-S index values were scored via the UK value set (−0.384 to 1), as no regional utility is available \[27\]. In this study, a comparative analysis focused on effect sizes (ESs) and regression models, emphasising intergroup differences over absolute index scores, which minimised potential bias from using different value sets \[28\].

The EQ-5D-5L measures five HRQoL dimensions (mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort, anxiety/depression) on a 1–5 scale (no problems to unable/extreme problems) \[29\] and includes a 20-cm EQ VAS (0–100) for self-rated health, using a ‘today’ recall period \[30\]. Index values, scored via the Chinese value set (−0.391 to 1), reflect better health with higher scores \[31\].

The EQ-5D-Y-3L assesses children’s HRQoL in five dimensions (mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort, feeling worried/sad/unhappy) with three response levels, using the Chinese value set (−0.088 to 1) \[32\]. The EQ-TIPS, a proxy tool for ages 0–3, covers six dimensions (movement, play, pain, social interaction, communication, eating) with three response levels. In this study, we used a level sum score that ranged from 6 (no problems) to 18 (most severe) \[33\].

The OHA is a validated measure of subjective health for both children and parents \[34\]. In this study, it was phrased as “How is your overall health today? Excellent, good, fair, poor, or very poor?” Its proxy version collected the carer’s impression of the child’s health.

Paediatric COVID-19 severity is categorised as: (1) mild: respiratory symptoms such as dry throat, cough and fever; (2) moderate: high fever (\>3 days), cough, shortness of breath, respiratory rate \<30/min, oxygen saturation \>93% and pneumonia on imaging; and (3) severe: high fever over 3 days, tachypnoea, oxygen saturation ≤93%, nasal flaring, wheezing or stridor, altered consciousness, seizures and feeding difficulties with dehydration signs \[23\]. The Chinese criteria for COVID-19 recovery: (1) normal temperature \>3 days; (2) significant symptom improvement; (3) a computed tomography scan showing lesion absorption (for pneumonia); and (4) two consecutive negative reverse transcription-polymerase chain reactions (Ct ≥35) or three consecutive negative antigen tests \[23\].

### Data Analysis

We conducted descriptive statistics to summarise demographic, socioeconomic and clinical characteristics, and assessed the distribution, construct validity, test–retest reliability and responsiveness of summary scores (EQ-HWB-S, EQ-5D-5L index scores, EQ VAS) and descriptive systems (dimensions). Response patterns were analysed by item-level frequencies and the proportion reporting ‘no problems’ across all items, indicating ‘full health’ (‘11111’ for the EQ-5D-5L and ‘111111111’ for the EQ-HWB-S).

Known-groups validity was assessed using a gamma distribution with a log-link generalised linear regression model to address the skewed EQ summary scores. Dependent variables were the parent’s EQ-HWB-S/EQ-5D-5L index scores and the EQ VAS, whereas the independent variables (the ‘known groups’) included child’s COVID-19 status, COVID-19 severity, parent’s daily caring time and work impact. The model was adjusted for confounders such as demographics, illness length, parental characteristics and household factors. To fit this model, scores were transformed to disutility as 1—utility for right skew, and the Modified Park test assessed model fit \[35, 36\].

We hypothesised that carers of children in better health (no COVID-19 or less severe disease) and with a lower caregiver burden (less caring time or work impact) would have higher EQ-HWB-S, EQ-5D-5L index scores and EQ VAS. Independent t-tests were used to compare mean scores; Cohen’s d ES (difference of mean/pooled standard deviation) indicated discrimination ability (0.20–0.49 modest, 0.5–0.79 moderate, \>0.8 large). Dimension-level differences were test using the Chi-squared test and Fisher’s exact test, and odds ratios (ORs) assessed the likelihood of reporting ‘no problem’ versus ‘any problems’ in each dimension.

Test–retest reliability was assessed in parents with an unchanged OHA from baseline to follow-up \[37\], using an intraclass correlation coefficient for summary scores, with an intraclass correlation coefficient \>0.7 indicating good reliability \[38\]. Dimension-level reliability was measured by Gwet’s agreement coefficient (Gwet’s AC1: \<0.2 poor, 0.21–0.4 fair, 0.41–0.6 moderate, 0.61–0.8 good, \>0.8 very good) \[39\].

Responsiveness was assessed by correlating a child’s health improvement with a parent’s HRQoL change in summary scores. Improvement, defined as COVID-19 recovery or better scores on a child’s EQ-TIPS, EQ-5D-Y-3L or OHA from baseline to follow-up, were compared using *t*-tests. Glass’ Δ ES (mean difference divided by baseline standard deviation) measured responsiveness (0.20–0.49 modest, 0.5–0.79 moderate, \>0.8 large) \[40\]. Changes in ‘no problems’ responses by dimension were assessed with ORs. Responsiveness analysis focused on parents of improved children, as most patients were stable or improved at follow-up.

A *p*-value \<0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were conducted using SPSS 26.0 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA).

## Results

A total of 1092 children and their parental carers completed the baseline survey, with 78.8% (861) of the children infected with COVID-19 (see Fig. <a href="#Fig1" data-ref-type="fig">1</a>). A total of 311 parents of children with COVID-19 completed the follow-up survey. Baseline characteristics (Table <a href="#Tab1" data-ref-type="table">1</a>) show in the COVID-19 group that 73.3% of children were over 4 years, compared with 67.1% in the healthy group (*p* = 0.038). Most parents were highly educated (92.1%), two thirds were mothers and one third of infected children had parents with COVID-19.

Figure <a href="#Fig2" data-ref-type="fig">2</a> illustrates the distribution of the EQ-HWB-S and EQ-5D-5L dimension responses among parents. In general, parents of COVID-19-infected children experienced more problems across all dimensions than those of non-infected children (all *p* \< 0.05). The largest differences in the EQ-HWB-S were in ‘activity’, ‘exhaustion’, ‘anxiety’, ‘control’ and ‘physical pain’, whereas the EQ-5D-5L showed the largest differences in ‘pain/discomfort’ and ‘anxiety/depression’. Regarding overlapping dimensions, EQ-HWB-S’s ‘day-to-day activity’ (42.9%) and ‘physical pain’ (39.7%) reported fewer problems than EQ-5D-5L’s ‘usual activity’ (54.9%) and ‘pain/discomfort’ (40.1%). Notably, the ‘no problem’ percentage for EQ-5D-5L’s ‘anxiety/depression’ (46.2%) closely matched EQ-HWB-S’s ‘sadness/depression’ (46.0%) rather than ‘anxiety’ (34.6%). Table <a href="#Tab1" data-ref-type="table">1</a> of the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM) shows that 6.6% of parents with COVID-19-infected children reported 111111111 on the EQ-HWB-S, whereas 25.9% reported 11111 on the EQ-5D-5L.

Known-groups validity results (Table <a href="#Tab2" data-ref-type="table">2</a>) showed that parents of children with COVID-19, more severe or prolonged illness, and a greater work impact had lower summary scores, indicating lower HRQoL (*p* \< 0.05). Compared with the EQ-5D-5L, the EQ-HWB-S index scores were more strongly correlated with increased caring time and work impact. Significant differences in index scores of two instruments were observed between mild and severe COVID-19 cases. Analyses controlled for variables such as parental COVID-19 status, showing expected differences (e.g. fathers and higher income parents reported better HRQoL). Unexpectedly, parents of older children reported slightly lower HRQoL, possibly owing to higher infection rates in children aged over 4 years. Cohen’s d ESs revealed stronger discriminative ability for EQ-HWB-S (ES: 0.29–0.72) than EQ-5D-5L (ES: 0.26–0.65) across children’s COVID-19 infection status, caregiving time and work impact (Table 1 of the ESM). The EQ VAS demonstrated comparable known-groups validity (ES: 0.21–0.80).

Closer examination of individual dimensions revealed that a child’s clinical condition impacted the parents’ physical functioning more than emotional well-being, especially in severe cases (Table 1 of the ESM). For example, in terms of children’s COVID-19 presence, the EQ-HWB-S dimensions most affected were ‘physical pain’ (OR = 5.22) and ‘day-to-day activity’ (OR = 4.07), whereas the EQ-5D-5L showed the greatest impact on ‘pain/discomfort’ (OR = 5.70) and ‘self-care’ (OR = 2.76). In contrast, for daily caregiving time and work impact, the EQ-HWB-S showed larger effects in ‘loneliness’, ‘exhaustion’ and ‘concentration’, whereas the EQ-5D-5L impacted ‘anxiety/depression’, ‘self-care’ and ‘usual activity’.

To assess test–retest reliability, 65 parent carers with an unchanged self-rated OHA were selected (Table <a href="#Tab3" data-ref-type="table">3</a>). Intraclass correlation coefficient values for the EQ-HWB-S and EQ-5D-5L index scores were comparable (0.76 vs 0.77), with the EQ VAS slightly lower (0.70), indicating good reliability. Gwet’s AC1 values ranged from 0.33 (‘anxiety’) to 0.79 (‘mobility’) for the EQ-HWB-S, and 0.40 (‘anxiety/depression’) to 0.76 (‘mobility’) for the EQ-5D-5L, indicating moderate-to-good reliability, except for the EQ-HWB-S ‘exhaustion’ and ‘anxiety’.

## Discussion

This study is the first to assess paediatric spillover effects using the newly developed EQ-HWB-S alongside the widely used EQ-5D-5L, and to compare their psychometric properties. It provides the first evidence of test–retest reliability and responsiveness among parental carers of children with varying disease severity and caregiver burden. Both instruments demonstrated strong discriminative ability, with the EQ-HWB-S better differentiating between child health conditions (COVID-19 status) and caregiver burden (caring time and work impact). Both instruments showed moderate-to-large ESs when children’s recovery and improved health status were used as external criteria, specifically, the EQ-5D-5L showed better responsiveness in physical functions, whereas the EQ-HWB-S outperformed in capturing caregiver burden, particularly in emotional and social dimensions. The EQ VAS performed comparably to the EQ-HWB-S and EQ-5D-5L in terms of known-groups validity, test–retest reliability and responsiveness.

In our study, a distribution analysis revealed that parents of infected children, regardless of their own COVID-19 status, encountered more physical challenges than those of healthy children. This contrasts with a study using the EQ-HWB-S in parents of children with chronic conditions such as disabilities, special healthcare needs or mental disorders, where ‘exhaustion’, ‘loneliness’, ‘cognition’, ‘anxiety’, ‘sadness/depression’ and ‘control’ showed greater well-being declines than ‘mobility’, ‘day-to-day activities’ and ‘physical pain’ \[17\]. This disparity may reflect the immediate physical demands of acute COVID-19 versus the sustained emotional strain from chronic illnesses.

However, it is worth noting that while proxy instruments such as EQ-TIPS or proxy version of EQ-5D-Y-3L are indispensable for assessing HRQoL in young children unable to self-report, their use in capturing spillovers poses challenges. When the same caregiver evaluates both their child’s health and their own HRQoL this may introduce biases. A study in school-aged children found that caregivers’ emotional states and caregiver burden influenced their perceptions during the COVID-19 pandemic, with 50% underestimating their children’s HRQoL \[41\]. However, when caregivers adopt the perspective of the care recipient, their assessment of the caregiving burden may differ from their own perception \[42\]. Further research is warranted, such as separating the assessment of parent’s HRQoL or improving instructions to distinguish the caregiver burden from health status.

In addition, the observed difference in the proportion of respondents reporting ‘full health’ on the EQ-HWB-S (nine dimensions) and the EQ-5D-5L (five dimensions) aligns with findings in carers of chronic diseases, which report ceiling effects of 7.5% for the EQ-HWB-S and 34.7% for the EQ-5D-5L \[43\]. However, this may be due in part to the EQ-HWB-S’s more comprehensive nine-item assessment and its longer recall periods (‘7 days’ vs ‘today’ for EQ-5D-5L). The different time frames have different strong and weak points but it is possible that the EQ-HWB-S identifies more problems by asking about a longer time span. Which recall period is more appropriate and useful in patients with acute illnesses remains an open question.

Regarding known-groups validity, the two descriptive systems and the EQ VAS effectively discriminated among parents based on children’s disease status and caregiver burden. The EQ-HWB-S outperformed the EQ-5D-5L in differentiating groups by caregiving time and work impact, likely because of its focus on well-being dimensions such as exhaustion, cognition and loneliness. This aligns with findings in caregivers of adult cancer survivors, where the EQ-HWB-S better distinguished between caregivers and non-caregivers, as well as between different caregiving intensities \[44\]. There were significant differences in ‘no problems’ percentages between groups with greater and lesser caring time or work impact, particularly in well-being dimensions such as exhaustion, cognition and loneliness. These findings align with the intended purpose of the EQ-HWB-S, which captures a broader range of HRQoL constructs beyond physical and mental health. This contributes to the validation of the EQ-HWB-S as an effective tool for assessing variations in caregiver burden, in contrast to the EQ-5D-5L, which gives more weight to physical health.

However, we observed small ESs and non-significant differences between mild and moderate COVID-19 cases, as well as moderate known-groups validity between mild and severe cases. Overlapping symptoms (e.g. fever, cough, respiratory problems) contribute to similar functional impacts in children, making it difficult for generic instruments such as EQ-TIPS and EQ-5D-Y to detect substantial differences. Notably, COVID-19 severity classifications are primarily based on prognostic markers, rather than symptom severity, which may not align with the caregiver perception or the child’s functional limitations. Additionally, caregivers of severely ill children might adapt their expectations, under-reporting burdens \[45\]. Future research should include more balanced severity distributions to better evaluate the impact of illness severity on caregiver spillovers.

In general, the EQ-HWB-S, EQ-5D-5L and EQ VAS demonstrated good test–retest reliability for summary scores and moderate-to-large reliability for individual dimensions, with the EQ-5D-5L performing slightly better. A study involving 25 parental carers reporting a higher intraclass correlation coefficient (0.87) and dimension agreement (82.7% for ‘daily activity’ to 90% for ‘sadness/depression’) for the EQ-HWB-S than our study \[16\], possibly because of their focus on chronic stable stressors, which led to more consistent responses. In contrast, parents of children with COVID-19 experienced more fluctuating challenges, particularly in terms of ‘exhaustion’ and ‘anxiety’, leading to more variable responses. The health and well-being of parents are influenced by both ‘caring for’ and ‘caring about’ their children, with the emotional burden heightened in uncertain scenarios such as the COVID-19 pandemic, where a lack of control exacerbates psychological strain \[46\].

Both instruments demonstrated robust responsiveness concerning clinical recovery from COVID-19 and health improvements, with substantial ESs. The increase in the EQ-HWB-S index score (0.13–0.16) was higher than that reported in a study on parents experiencing life challenges (0.005–0.094) related to finances, housing, employment, family relationships or broader social needs such as immigration or discrimination \[16\]. This suggests that the EQ-HWB-S is sensitive to changes in caregiver burden-related domains such as ‘activities’, ‘exhaustion’ and ‘physical pain’ in the context of COVID-19. The ‘usual activity’ and ‘pain/discomfort’ dimensions in the EQ-5D-5L were notably affected, aligning with evidence that pain and discomfort were the most frequent problems, reported by 75% of primary caregivers of children with COVID-19, due to muscle weakness and fatigue \[47\]. However, at follow-up, the EQ-HWB-S index scores were lower than EQ-5D-5L scores across all categories, possibly because the extended recall period of the EQ-HWB-S leads respondents to focus more on negative issues or emotions \[48\]. Additionally, specific EQ-HWB-S dimensions, such as ‘daily life control’, ‘loneliness’ and ‘cognition’, showed less responsiveness to short-term improvements compared with the EQ-5D-5L, which focuses on more visible changes in essential health domains during recovery.

Our findings revealed a strong correlation between parental carers’ health and well-being and their children’s health status and caregiving responsibilities. Spillover effects encompass two main components: the caregiving effect, reflecting the physical, emotional and time demands of caregiving, and the family effect, arising from concerns about the child’s health, often captured through mental health dimensions such as ‘anxiety’ or ‘depression’ \[46\]. The EQ-HWB-S, with its broader range of well-being dimensions, captures both effects, while the EQ-5D-5L, with its narrower focus on physical health, primarily reflects caregiving effects. Our study highlights the ability of the EQ-HWB-S to assess the emotional and social aspects often overlooked by traditional tools. Excluding these dimensions risks underestimating the value of caregiver-support interventions. During the uncertainty of COVID-19, the emotional burden of “caring about” a child blurred the distinction between caregiving demands and family stress, potentially causing overlaps in spillover assessments. Future research should aim to develop tools to better separate these effects to enhance our understanding of the caregiver burden.

When considering spillover effects, a key normative question arises: should they be part of a standard CEA? Including spillovers requires consistent accounting across cost-effectiveness ratios for valid comparisons. Current guidelines recommend their inclusion for relevant interventions \[1, 5–7\], such as vaccination programmes enhancing community immunity or school-based initiatives improving family dietary habits \[49, 50\]. Our results show the EQ-5D-5L focuses on physical health improvements, while the EQ-HWB-S captures broader emotional and social dimensions. For instance, caregivers with a greater work impact reported larger declines in EQ-HWB-S scores, highlighting its sensitivity to caregiving burdens. These differences could influence incremental cost-effectiveness ratios, making interventions targeting caregiver well-being appear more cost effective. However, challenges remain, such as determining how many caregivers to measure, avoiding double counting and addressing whether social health insurance should cover caregiving costs \[51–53\]. Variability in relevance and measurement further complicates incorporating spillover effects into a ‘base-case scenario’, emphasising the need to align instrument selection with CEA objectives.

This study has several limitations. First, the data were collected at a single centre in Shanghai, with participants who had relatively high education and socioeconomic status, which may limit generalisability. Second, test–retest reliability was assessed using a 2- to 3-week interval to balance reliability and responsiveness, which may have allowed changes in health status, affecting reliability estimates. Additionally, the small sample size of 65, while adequate for a preliminary analysis, may limit generalisability and underestimate true reliability. Unmeasured confounders, such as unreported health conditions or financial and social challenges, might also have influenced results. Third, during the study period, most children were unvaccinated, and the vaccination status for both children and parents was not recorded, limiting the ability to assess its potential impact. As there is no Chinese value set for the EQ-HWB-S, we used the UK value set, which may disadvantage its performance compared to the EQ-5D-5L with the Chinese value set. Further research could explore cultural differences in the applicability of both instruments. For example, in East Asia, spillover effects may be influenced by stronger familial obligations, greater grandparent involvement and somatisation emotional distress \[54\]. Finally, as the EQ-HWB-S is experimental, it may be subject to future modifications.

## Conclusions

Both the EQ-HWB-S and the EQ-5D-5L are promising instruments for estimating the spillover effects associated with a child’s COVID-19-related health condition on parental carers. Our comprehensive assessment highlights each instrument’s strengths and areas of applicability. The nuanced insights provided by the EQ-HWB-S make it a valuable tool for capturing the emotional and social impacts of caregiver burden, whereas the EQ-5D-5L offers a more sensitive overview of how a child’s health improvements affect parental health. Researchers and practitioners can leverage these findings to select the most appropriate instrument for specific caregiving contexts. Further research is needed to validate these findings across diverse populations and to explore the application of country-specific value sets for the EQ-HWB-S to improve its accuracy and cultural relevance.
