# Findings from a roundtable discussion with Pakistani stakeholders on measuring and valuing health and health-related quality of life for children and adolescents

## Abstract

### Background

Children and adolescents represent a distinct population with specific healthcare needs, requiring appropriate consideration in health-related quality of life (HRQoL) measurements and economic evaluations. In Pakistan, the absence of local value sets for children and adolescent health states and the limited application of health technology assessment (HTA) pose challenges to informed equitable decision-making and resource allocation. Valuing children and adolescents’ health involves methodological and ethical complexities, particularly in determining whose preferences should be considered and how trade-offs between length and quality of life should be addressed. This study aimed to explore the views of key national stakeholders on these valuation issues to inform the development of a localized EQ-5D-Y-3 L value set for Pakistan.

### Methods

A total of 12 stakeholders were identified via the investigators’ network from different cities in Pakistan and diverse backgrounds. An in-person 3.5-hour meeting was held in Islamabad to discuss a range of topics including (a) the need for a Pakistani value set for the EQ-5D-Y-3 L, (b) willingness to pay more for quality-adjusted life-year (QALY) gains for children versus adults, (c) source of health preferences (adults vs. children), (d) potential ethical concerns and, (e) other challenges in a youth valuation to obtain deeper insights for understanding of the health valuation. The session was recorded, transcribed, and summarized.

### Results

Stakeholders confirmed that QALYs have not yet been widely used in Pakistan’s healthcare system, although HRQoL instruments like EQ-5D-3 L and EQ-5D-5 L are increasingly applied by researchers for evidence generation. Stakeholders agreed that adolescents aged 11 and above can complete valuation tasks and should be involved in data collection. Moreover, 33% of stakeholders (4 out of 12) specifically recommended using adult preferences initially due to budgetary and technical constraints, while 66% (8 out of 12) did not explicitly oppose using adult preferences initially, they emphasized the importance of involving adolescents in the process either concurrently or in subsequent phases. Participants acknowledged that societal willingness to pay more per QALY for children varied, with some viewing it as a strategic investment. Ethical concerns about life-year trade-offs for children were not seen as major barriers in the local context. Parents or caregivers were deemed the most appropriate proxy respondents when self-reporting was not possible. Challenges identified included limited awareness of QALYs among policymakers, scarce local utility data, technical capacity gaps, and constrained health budgets.

### Conclusion

Pakistani stakeholders encouraged the use of health valuation data such as the EQ-5D-Y-3 L measures in decision-making and provided useful perspectives to youth valuation with a local context.

## Introduction

The focus on measuring and valuing health and health-related quality of life (HRQoL) of children and adolescents has increased during the recent years. Children and adolescents have unique healthcare needs and are significant consumers of healthcare resources \[1\]. New and emerging health technologies that target children or involve patient groups consisting of children and young individuals are faced with significant challenges in assessing the efficacy and cost-effectiveness of the new interventions \[2\]. A common challenge, globally, in conducting health technology assessment (HTA) for pediatric interventions is the limited availability of robust data on children’s health-related quality of life (HRQoL) \[3\]. Currently there are existing measures specifically designed to assess HRQoL in children, potentially serving as tools to generate the needed evidence required for HTA \[4\].

The EQ-5D-Y-3 L is a generic, preference-based HRQoL measure developed by the EuroQol Research Foundation in 2009, which is used to assess HRQoL in children and adolescents. Adopted from its adult counterpart, the EQ-5D-Y-3 L shares common applications with other EQ-5D family of instruments, such as the use in clinical research and practice, population health surveys, and economic evaluations. Updated from the adult EQ-5D-3 L design, the EQ-5D-Y-3 L revised the framing of the questions by using more child-friendly languages. The five health dimensions of the EQ-5D-Y-3 L include (1) mobility, (2) looking after myself, (3) doing usual activities, (4) having pain or discomfort, and (5) feelings worried, sad, or unhappy. Each dimension features three severity levels ranging from no problems, some problems to a lot of problems. By design, the EQ-5D-Y-3 L provides a total of 243 (= 3^5) possible health states. EuroQol group dissimilated the user guide for the EQ-5D-Y-3 L stating that for a) children aged 4 to 7 years, a proxy version should be used, 2) for children aged 12 to 15 years, both youth and adult versions could be used but the youth version is preferred, 3) for children 16 years and older, adult version should be used. Hence, the EQ-5D-Y-3 L is applicable for children 4 to 15 years using different administration modes: proxy version for children 4 to 7 years and self-complete for children 8–15 years \[5\].

It is well recognized that value sets based on adult health state preferences are generally not appropriate for calculating health state utility values in children and adolescents \[6\]. Further, it is necessary to develop distinct value sets tailored to different countries and/or regions. EuroQol Research Foundation has been conducting valuation studies in countries around world to generate country-specific value sets \[7\]. The value sets are developed to generate specific values for the health states in the EQ-5D-Y-3 L. For this purpose, an International Valuation Protocol outlining the procedures for conducting valuation tasks and generating value sets for the EQ-5D-Y-3 L was established and released in 2020 \[8\]. This protocol serves as fundamental guidance for researchers undertaking valuation studies around the globe and helped various countries to publish EQ-5D-Y-3 L valuation studies \[9, 10\]. Although the International Valuation Protocol for the EQ-5D-Y-3 L offers guidance, youth valuation is linked with diverse challenges as compared to valuing adults, which may vary across countries and settings \[11, 12\]. These challenges include, but not limiting to, consideration of the source of health state preferences (adult vs. children), perspectives used in the design (whose views matter), respondent sampling and sample sizes that could represent the general population of the country, among others \[13, 14\].

Pakistan, considered as a lower-middle-income country in Asia, ranks as the fifth most populous country globally, with a population of more than 245 million people. Moreover, it caters the world’s second-largest Muslim population \[15\]. Approximately 45% of the country’s population consists of children, while adolescents aged 10–19 years make up 22.7% of the population \[16\]. Currently, healthcare resource allocation in Pakistan is primarily driven by traditional budgeting, clinical judgment, and historical spending, with limited use of formal economic evaluations or structured frameworks like HTA (Akhtar, Khan and Haque, 2020; Zaidi et al., 2019). Regulatory decisions such as those by the Drug Regulatory Authority of Pakistan (DRAP) focus on safety, affordability, and availability, often without incorporating utility-based measures like QALYs (DRAP, 2018). Although HTA is still in its early stages in Pakistan, there is a growing government interest in cost-effectiveness analysis (CEA). For example, pilot projects supported by the World Health Organization (WHO) and the International Decision Support Initiative (iDSI) are being conducted, reflecting an increasing recognition of its importance (Chalkidou et al., 2017; WHO, 2021). At the same time, there are also barriers such as limited technical capacity, scarce local health utility data, and the absence of institutionalized HTA structure (Khan, Malik and Abbasi, 2021). Further, in this evolving landscape, initiating youth health valuation studies such as the EQ-5D-Y-3 L is a timely and strategic initiative. Developing local value sets for both adults and children will strengthen the foundation for future HTA applications in Pakistan. As part of this initiative, obtaining stakeholders’ perspectives on valuation of children’s HRQoL is an important step.

In recent years, the current research team developed Pakistan’s first EQ-5D-3 L value set for adults, which is now available to support health economic evaluations and research on health-related quality of life (HRQoL) within the country \[17\]. However, no value set currently exists for the EQ-5D-Y-3 L, which is designed to measure and value HRQoL in children and adolescents. To address this gap, the research team has undertaken the development of a locally relevant EQ-5D-Y-3 L value set for Pakistan. As a preparatory step, engaging national stakeholders to understand their views on the relevance, uptake, and interpretation of valuing children’s health is essential before moving forward with large-scale valuation activities. Similar efforts have been made by the EuroQol investigators in the US, Canada and UK, where stakeholders’ recommendations played positive and significant roles in their youth valuation tasks \[5, 18\].

The aim of this study was to explore and understand the views of the key national stakeholders regarding the measurement and valuation of health and HRQoL for children and of adolescents in Pakistan using the EQ-5D-Y-3 L. Specifically, we aimed to obtain their views on the usefulness of health state utility measures within the Pakistani context, the appropriate sources of health preferences to be elicited, potential ethical considerations, and other anticipated challenges in the valuation process.

## Methods

Purposive sampling was employed in this study to ensure the inclusion of stakeholders with relevant expertise, experience, or influence in healthcare decision-making particularly in areas such as child health, health economics, pharmaceutical regulation, and early childhood development. The selection was guided by predefined criteria aimed at capturing diverse perspectives from sectors involved in healthcare policy, financing, regulation, service delivery, and advocacy. Participants were chosen based on their professional background in pediatrics, health economics and outcomes research (HEOR), regulatory affairs, healthcare financing, or direct work with children. To incorporate non-technical perspectives, the sample also included at least one layperson. Although limited to 12 participants, the panel was purposefully composed to reflect a broad range of relevant viewpoints. This approach was deemed appropriate for the exploratory and formative nature of the roundtable, which was designed to inform the early planning of EQ-5D-Y-3 L valuation activities in Pakistan rather than generate generalizable findings.

Key stakeholders were identified through the professional networks of the investigators, with an emphasis on selecting individuals from diverse and relevant backgrounds. It was essential that participants had sufficient understanding to engage in discussions on health state valuation. In this context, sufficient understanding referred to the ability to comprehend the fundamental principles of utility measurement, the rationale for valuation studies, and the implications of preference-based approaches in healthcare decision making. To ensure this, two complementary strategies were employed. First, stakeholders were selected from roles directly relevant to healthcare policy, child health, economics, or industry, where a baseline familiarity with such topics could be reasonably assumed. Second, capacity building materials were provided in advance, including concept notes, explanatory videos, and relevant published literature. A structured presentation was also delivered at the start of the session to further enhance comprehension. These steps were aimed at creating an informed platform for meaningful participation and dialogue. The final group included: \[1\] pediatric clinicians and child health researchers (*n* = 1); \[2\] representatives from the Drug Regulatory Authority of Pakistan (*n* = 3); \[3\] a third-party payer (*n* = 1); \[4\] laypersons with direct experience caring for or working with children (*n* = 2); \[5\] academicians and researchers with expertise in health economics and outcomes research (HEOR) (*n* = 3); and \[6\] professionals from the private healthcare industry (*n* = 2), including one participant from the pharmaceutical sector and one from the medical device sector. Although grouped together under the private healthcare sector category, these participants were selected to reflect distinct perspectives from their respective segments of the healthcare market.

A total of 12 stakeholders were invited to participate. Prior to the meeting, they were provided with a concept note (Appendix A) outlining key elements such as an introduction to the EQ-5D-Y-3 L instrument, the importance of health-related quality of life (HRQoL) measures, and the critical role of stakeholder engagement in the valuation study process. Furthermore, videos focusing on the EQ-5D descriptive systems and relevant published literature were also provided for their better understanding of the important challenges in valuing youth health \[14, 19\]. Additionally, a survey consisting of 5 items that was previously designed and utilized by US investigators in the US stakeholder meeting was also distributed \[5\]. The survey was used to obtain deeper insights into the participants’ backgrounds and their overall understanding of health and HRQoL valuation. The same survey was used not only because these questions were pertinent but also enabled potential cross-country comparisons on stakeholders’ input from different countries. The in-person roundtable discussion was conducted on 20th May, 2023, which lasted for three hours and thirty minutes. Two of the participants joined the discussion online through zoom. The initial 150 min were allocated to providing background information, including \[1\] an overview of HRQoL measurement; \[2\] the EQ-5D-Y-3 L instrument; \[3\] valuation study methodology; \[4\] use of health value data; \[5\] experience of other countries utilizing health value data sets, especially India and Indonesia; and \[6\] road map for utilizing health value data sets for health resource allocation in Pakistan regarding: statistics, need of valuation studies, progress made till date, use of datasets by academicians/researchers, policy makers and pharmaceutical industries, along with response to queries raised by government officials regarding the Eq. 5D while conducting Eq. 5D 3 L valuation. The remaining 60 min were assigned to semi-structured discussions aligned with predetermined questions. These questions were generated through iterative discussions involving the study team, using the interview guide previously used for US stakeholder meeting as guidance.

The list of the questions was refined to \[1\] reflect content considered most relevant by investigators for a valuation study in Pakistan; \[2\] ensure coherence for stakeholders from diverse backgrounds; and \[3\] be effectively covered within a 60-minute discussion. Ultimately, eight discussion questions or prompts were chosen (Table <a href="#Tab1" data-ref-type="table">1</a>).

During the roundtable discussion, participants were invited to share their views on the need for generating health state utilities for both adults and children in Pakistan as part of the initial exploration of valuation priorities. During the roundtable discussion, participants were invited to share their views on whether society should be willing to pay more per quality-adjusted life year (QALY) gained for children as compared to adults. This question aimed to explore societal preferences regarding the prioritization of healthcare resources, particularly in the context of Pakistan’s demographic structure and economic constraints. Every participant was given the opportunity to express their views, however, specific stakeholders were asked to answer each discussion question posed before opening the discussion to the wider group. Stakeholders were motivated to participate and address their viewpoints raised by other stakeholders. Acting as discussion moderators, the investigators posed follow-up questions to foster deeper dialogue. Recording and transcribing was carried out for the 60 min discussion. The transcription of the meeting was reviewed individually by the investigators. Each investigator took notes to consolidate participant responses, as these addressed various topics beyond the initial prompts. After the compilation of the findings, data was subjected to thematic analysis for generating major themes. An honorarium was paid to each stakeholder for their participation. Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the Pak-Austria Fachhochschule Institute of Applied Sciences and Technology Institutional Review Board (IRB; \#29).

Five main themes emerged from the data during analysis:

1.  Whether QALY is used in the current healthcare system and needs assessment for generating health utilities specifically for adults as well as children.

2.  Considerations regarding the appropriate sources of health preferences whether adults, children/adolescents, or both and whether it is appropriate to ask respondents to imagine a child’s health and make decisions based on their willingness to trade off quantity and quality of life.

3.  Difficulty faced with expressing a willingness to prioritize the length of years a child lives at the expense of the quality of their life.

4.  Most appropriate source for the proxy perspective when self-reporting is not possible.

5.  Challenges in utilizing health preference values for adults and children in Pakistan.

Each of the major themes is discussed below.

### Current use of QALYs in Pakistan and the need for adult and child health utility data

All of them reported that QALYs has not been used in the healthcare system of Pakistan until recently, however, many academicians/researchers are using the HRQoL instruments including EQ-5D-3 L and EQ-5D-5 L for producing evidence-based data for assessment of HRQoL in general population as well as in different diseases. Some stakeholders expressed that while Pakistan’s healthcare system currently relies heavily on clinical endpoints, there is a need to shift toward including patient-reported outcomes (PROs) in decision-making. They believed this shift could help address key challenges of affordability and accessibility of medicines. However, they emphasized that incorporating PROs may not directly reduce medicine prices; rather, it could support more efficient resource allocation by highlighting treatments that offer meaningful improvements in patients’ quality of life, daily functioning, and symptom relief. By prioritizing interventions that reflect real patient needs, this approach could lead to better adherence, improved outcomes and more cost-effective use of limited healthcare resources. In general, they endorsed the need for generating health state utilities for adults as well as children to employ cost-effectiveness analysis for new and existing medical interventions in the country.

#### Societal willingness to pay per QALY: children vs. adults in the Pakistani context

During the roundtable discussion, participants were invited to share their views on whether society should demonstrate a greater willingness-to-pay more per QALY gained for children compared to adults. Nearly 42% of the stakeholders (5 out of 12) expressed support for this approach. Several of these participants highlighted that approximately 60% of Pakistan’s population comprises youth, including 22% adolescents, positioning them as a vital segment of the future workforce \[16\]. They viewed investment in children’s health as a strategic means to foster long-term social and economic development in the country. In contrast, 58% of the stakeholders (7 out of 12) expressed caution or disagreement with prioritizing higher payments per QALY for children. These participants emphasized the current lack of robust, evidence-based data to justify allocating greater resources to child health interventions relative to adults. They also noted that HTA and health economic evaluations remain relatively new and underutilized disciplines in Pakistan, with limited application in policy decision-making to date. Moreover, healthcare is not currently a high government priority, as evidenced by the allocation of only around 1% of the national budget to the sector \[20\]. From their perspective, prioritizing QALY gains for one age group without sufficient supporting evidence could risk reinforcing or exacerbating existing health disparities across population subgroups. Overall, the discussion reflected a diversity of perspectives on the ethical and practical implications of assigning greater societal value to health gains in children. While some stakeholders supported a child-focused investment strategy, others advocated for a more cautious, evidence-driven approach. The dialogue highlighted the need for broader deliberations on priority setting within Pakistan’s evolving HTA framework, with careful consideration of national capacity, equity, and long-term health system goals.

### Sourcing health preferences for children: balancing ethics, accuracy, and feasibility

A central theme in the roundtable discussion was the selection of appropriate sources of preferences for the Pakistani EQ-5D-Y-3 L valuation study. Most stakeholders recommended using a mix of both adults and adolescents as respondents. They believed that this approach would provide a more comprehensive understanding of societal health preferences and would reflect the perspectives of both healthcare payers (adults) and end-users (children and adolescents). Several participants emphasized that adolescents, especially those aged 11 years and above, possess the cognitive maturity to understand valuation tasks and can imagine the health experiences of a 10-year-old child. One of the participants stated:

> *“Adolescents can relate to the life of a 10-year-old and can independently complete these tasks their voice should be included to reflect their own experiences and views.” (Stakeholder 007)*.

However, the discussion also highlighted concerns about the practical implementation of this approach, particularly considering limited health budgets and the absence of prior health utilities data for children in Pakistan. Some stakeholders argued that although adolescents could be included in the valuation study, adults as the primary taxpayers and funders of the healthcare system should initially serve as the primary source of preferences for developing the value set. Another participant stated:

> *“Since adults are the taxpayers and ultimately fund healthcare decisions*, *it makes sense to begin with their preferences while we build the capacity to include youth perspectives.” (Stakeholder 005)*.

In general, they recommended approaching adolescents aged 11 years as appropriate. In terms of the appropriateness of asking respondents to consider hypothetical scenarios involving death and life-year trade-offs for children, stakeholders were generally comfortable with the idea. Many attributed this to cultural factors, including the majority Muslim population’s acceptance of discussions around death as part of religious belief systems. One of the stakeholders stated:

> *“In our society*, *the concept of death is not taboo; it is understood as part of life. So*, *asking these questions should not be an issue if approached sensitively.” (Stakeholder 006)*.

During the roundtable discussion, there was broad consensus among stakeholders that adolescents aged 11 years and above are cognitively capable of completing valuation tasks and should be included in the data collection phase of the EQ-5D-Y-3 L valuation study. However, when discussing the most appropriate source of preferences for the development of the initial value set, views were more mixed. While 33% of stakeholders (4 out of 12) specifically recommended using adult (i.e., taxpayer) preferences in the initial phase citing concerns related to budget constraints, limited availability of local health utility data, and alignment with current policy-making practices, the remaining 66% did not oppose this position explicitly. Rather, their focus was on ensuring that adolescents be involved in the process, either in parallel or in future phases, without expressing strong opposition to initiating the value set with adult preferences. The discussion reflected a pragmatic recognition that while adolescents should ideally contribute to youth valuation data, initiating the value set using adult preferences may be a necessary interim step given the early stage of HTA development in Pakistan.

### Challenges in prioritizing child life length vs. quality

All stakeholders agreed that while respondents might be able to imagine different health states of child, many would still find it challenging to make decisions about trading off the length of a child’s life in favor of improving their quality of life. They further added that the responses to the valuation exercises might vary in case of (a) one imagining a hypothetical child, (b) one already has his/her own child(ren) or, (c) one has known child(ren) from close relatives/friends vs. one does not know any.

### Optimal proxy selection in absence of self-reporting

During the discussion on appropriate sources of health preference data when children are unable to self-report, stakeholders consistently identified parents or primary caregivers as the most appropriate proxy respondents. This preference was based on their close familiarity with the child’s daily functioning, emotional well-being, and health-related challenges. For younger children, in particular, caregivers were seen as best positioned to provide reliable assessments of health states. Alternative proxies such as teachers, healthcare providers, or unrelated adults were considered but generally viewed as less suitable. Stakeholders noted that these individuals may have limited interaction with the child or focus primarily on clinical observations, potentially missing broader psychosocial aspects essential to health-related quality of life (HRQoL). There was broad consensus that, when self-report is not feasible, parents or primary caregivers should serve as the preferred source of proxy preferences due to their unique insight into both medical and social dimensions of the child’s health.

### Key challenges in implementing health preference values in Pakistan

Stakeholders identified several critical challenges that may hinder the effective use of health preference values for both adults and children within Pakistan’s healthcare system. A major concern was the limited awareness and understanding of health utility measures such as quality-adjusted life years (QALYs) among policymakers, healthcare professionals, and other decision-makers. This lack of familiarity was viewed as a significant barrier to integrating such data into healthcare planning and reimbursement processes. One of the stakeholders stated:

> *“There is still a lack of familiarity with how to use such data for decision-making. Without proper training and awareness*, *the use of health preferences will remain on paper.” (Stakeholder 008)*.

Another key issue was the shortage of technical expertise and institutional capacity to conduct health technology assessments (HTAs) and economic evaluations, both essential for effectively applying health preference data.

> *“We do not have enough trained professionals who understand how to conduct these valuations or interpret their results properly*,*” (Stakeholder 002).*

The constraint of limited financial resources was also emphasized. With only about 1% of the national budget allocated to health, several stakeholders questioned the feasibility of adopting valuation-based approaches without stronger policy support.

> *“When the overall health budget is so restricted*, *it becomes even harder to justify investments in valuation studies or their use in pricing decisions*,*” (Stakeholder 003).*

Cultural attitudes and potential resistance were also noted as barriers. Concepts such as trading off life years for quality of life may be misunderstood or viewed as ethically sensitive by segments of the public, religious groups, and even healthcare providers.

> *“It will take time and effort to help people understand the idea of valuing life in this way*, *there could be resistance if not communicated properly*,*” (Stakeholder 006).*

In addition to these specific challenges, stakeholders emphasized broader implementation concerns, including insufficient resources, limited technical capacity, and potential reluctance from key actors. These included healthcare providers unfamiliar with health economic evaluations, policymakers unaccustomed to using cost-effectiveness evidence, pharmaceutical industry representatives concerned about pricing implications, and members of the public who may find valuation methods difficult to grasp. These barriers were seen as significant obstacles to the routine use of health state value sets and cost-utility analyses in healthcare policy and practice. To address these challenges, there was a strong call for enhanced collaboration among government bodies, academia, and the pharmaceutical industry to support the generation, acceptance, and application of health preference data. Industry representatives stressed the importance of their involvement in policy discussions, given their role as key end-users of utility data in pricing and reimbursement decisions. Despite these hurdles, stakeholders acknowledged a growing interest from the government in incorporating cost-effectiveness and cost-utility analyses into healthcare decision-making. This emerging momentum was seen as a promising step toward the eventual integration of health preference values in pricing, resource allocation, and broader HTA frameworks in Pakistan.

## Discussion

Valuing the health of children poses distinctive methodological and normative challenges. Engaging stakeholders can offer valuable perspectives and guidance in addressing these complex issues \[5\].The stakeholders from Pakistan contributed insights that are relevant for planning EQ-5D-Y-3 L valuation study in Pakistan. They offered diverse perspectives for the need assessment for generating health utilities for both adults and children, willingness to pay for QALY gains, sources of preferences, willingness to prioritize, and challenges in utilization of health preference values for children in Pakistan. Health economic evaluations are less commonly used in low- and middle-income countries compared to high-income countries, primarily due to factors such as limited research capacity, insufficient funding, lack of trained personnel, and weak institutional frameworks to support health technology assessment \[21\]. This roundtable discussion revealed that there is a need for generating health utilities for both adults and children in Pakistan. All stakeholders agreed that QALYs have not yet been utilized within the Pakistani healthcare system and supported the idea of using a QALY-based approach to HTA in Pakistan would be a good idea to guide healthcare resource allocation and decision-making. Several participants highlighted that, given the constraints of Pakistan’s healthcare budget, the use of systematic and transparent evaluation methods like QALY-based cost-utility analysis could facilitate more rational and equitable distribution of limited resources.

> *“Our healthcare system is operating with a very restricted budget without proper economic evaluations like QALYs*, *decisions are often based on assumptions or political priorities rather than actual evidence.” (Stakeholder*, *002)*.
>
> *“We need to move beyond clinical endpoints alone. Decision-makers should have data on both the effectiveness and the value for money of interventions this is where QALY-based HTA can really help.” (Stakeholder*, *005)*.

The discussion regarding the willingness to pay for QALY gains in children as compared to adults revealed diverse perspectives among the stakeholders. While a minority of stakeholders (33.3%) supported paying more per QALY for children, viewing it as a strategic investment in the future workforce, others expressed reservations. Those who disagreed highlighted the lack of evidence-based data demonstrating long-term benefits of such an approach, particularly given that health technology assessment (HTA) and health economics are still emerging fields in Pakistan. They cautioned that prioritizing children without robust justification could risk reinforcing existing health disparities across different population subgroups. Notably, similar perspectives emerged during stakeholder roundtable discussions in the United States, where participants acknowledged an emotional inclination to prioritize children and supported higher QALY valuations for them, while also recognizing the need for careful ethical and economic consideration \[5\].

This roundtable discussion also revealed other insights in terms of sources of preferences for Pakistani valuation study. All the stakeholders favored the idea of asking health state preference tasks for trade-off of life years for quality of life or compare health states with immediate death. All stakeholders acknowledged the importance of including both adult and adolescent perspectives to enhance clarity and relevance of health preferences for effective budget al.location. While there was broad agreement that adolescents aged 11 and above can complete valuation tasks and should be included in the study, some stakeholders emphasized that, given current policy structures and fiscal constraints, adult preferences particularly those of taxpayers may be more appropriate for developing the initial EQ-5D-Y-3 L value set. This was viewed as a pragmatic starting point, with the understanding that adolescent preferences could be incorporated in subsequent phases as the valuation framework becomes more established within Pakistan’s healthcare decision-making processes. Meanwhile, most of the US and Canada stakeholders expressed that adolescents were capable of self-completing valuation tasks and, they reached a consensus that adolescents should be included in the valuation study for developing the EQ-5D-Y-3 L value set \[5, 21\].

This roundtable discussion was the first ever to discuss the idea of using health preference data in efficacy and cost-effectiveness in healthcare policy making in Pakistan. One of the limitations of this study was that the Pakistani stakeholders were not very familiar with the methodologies of the valuation tasks and lacked HTA experiences or understandings. Hence, uncomprehending the current EQ-5D-Y-3 L valuation study protocol. Nevertheless, engaging stakeholders was a crucial step to inform the design of the youth valuation to align with the expectations of local stakeholders. Thus, this meeting with Pakistani stakeholders fostered a discussion regarding the level of understanding or misconceptions associated with health valuation studies. In addition to the core findings, this study underscores the broader significance of stakeholder engagement in health valuation research within the Pakistani context. Meaningful stakeholder involvement ensures that valuation processes are culturally appropriate, ethically sound, and aligned with national policy considerations. It also promotes a sense of ownership and enhances the likelihood of uptake by decision-makers. In a setting like Pakistan where formal stakeholder consultation is not yet institutionalized in health economics research or policy development this roundtable represents a pioneering effort to engage a diverse group of actors in discussions on health valuation. The insights gathered highlight the importance of sustained dialogue and indicate that future sessions involving a wider array of participants, including policymakers, healthcare professionals, and community representatives, would be valuable to validate and expand upon the current findings. Reflecting on the process, one key limitation was the restricted time for interactive engagement, only 60 out of the 210 min were allocated for open discussion. Although this structure allowed for necessary background briefings, extending the dialogue segment in future sessions could foster more in-depth exploration of complex issues. To further enhance future panels, the investigators recommend offering pre-meeting educational webinars, organizing breakout sessions for targeted conversations, and incorporating anonymous feedback tools to ensure inclusive participation. These recommendations may serve as a useful model for researchers conducting stakeholder engagement in other low- and middle-income countries, where such practices are still in early stages of development.

Our stakeholders included professionals of diverse backgrounds including pediatricians, child health researchers, regulators from industry, medical devices, early childhood educators and layperson who share their direct experience with children. The discussion questions were drafted with the objective of reaching a consensus on measurement of health value sets of youth according to the international protocol. The discussion was useful to educate various players involved in healthcare decision making in the Pakistani context; it also helped to identify different challenges for utilizing health value data sets in policy and practice such as scarce resources, capacity building and sensitization of some of the stakeholders. The stakeholders suggested taking local pharma companies on board, as they will be one of the major end-users of the value set for cost effectiveness analyses. It was encouraging to see that all stakeholders were open to continued dialogue with investigators and were willing to attend a follow-up meeting.

### Study limitations

It is important to acknowledge several methodological limitations that may have influenced the findings of this roundtable. First, the limited familiarity of stakeholders with health economics concepts particularly QALYs and health state valuation methods could have shaped the depth and framing of their responses. To address this, the investigators provided a structured background presentation prior to the discussion, moving from general awareness-building to more specific valuation topics. This approach was intended to create a shared understanding among participants and enable more meaningful engagement. Feedback indicated that stakeholders generally felt comfortable contributing and were able to comprehend the material presented. However, we recognize that the way these complex concepts were framed and the sequence in which discussion points were introduced may have influenced how participants interpreted the issues, introducing potential framing and ordering effects. As a result, the perspectives shared in this session likely reflect early-stage opinions that may evolve with further exposure and capacity-building. Second, the study employed a small, purposive sample (*n* = 12) without accounting for geographical or regional representation. While participants were selected to reflect diverse professional backgrounds, the views expressed may not be generalizable to the broader population of stakeholders or the general public in Pakistan.

To strengthen future engagements, we recommend incorporating pre-meeting educational materials, extending interactive discussion time, using breakout groups for deeper dialogue and adopting randomized or parallel discussion formats to reduce potential biases. Expanding geographic diversity and stakeholder representation would also enhance the comprehensiveness and inclusiveness of subsequent consultations. These reflections may serve as a valuable guide for researchers planning stakeholder involvement in health valuation and HTA studies, particularly in low- and middle-income countries where such practices are still emerging.

## Conclusion

Pakistani stakeholders supported the use of health valuation data, such as the EQ-5D-Y-3 L measures, to inform healthcare decision-making and offered valuable context-specific insights for youth health valuation. There was consensus among all participants on the importance of directly involving adolescents in the valuation data collection process. However, some stakeholders suggested that, given the constraints of Pakistan’s limited health budget particularly the absence of dedicated funding for child health and the lack of prior health utility data, adult preferences, especially from taxpayers, could be pragmatically used as the initial basis for developing value sets. These stakeholder insights will inform the planning and design of the upcoming EQ-5D-Y-3 L youth valuation study in Pakistan and guide potential adaptations to the EuroQol International Valuation Protocol. As the first published report from a developing country on stakeholder engagement in youth health valuation, this work may serve as a reference point for researchers in other low- and middle-income countries pursuing similar studies.
